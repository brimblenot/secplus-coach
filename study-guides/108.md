## Security Considerations (Domain 5)

Security programs don't exist in a vacuum — they're shaped by laws, regulations, privacy principles, and organizational realities. This lesson covers the external and internal forces that define what your security program *must* do and what happens when it doesn't.

---

### Regulatory compliance: why it matters first

**Regulatory compliance** means identifying every law and regulation that applies to your organization based on your industry, geography, and the data you handle — then meeting those requirements.

Consequences of noncompliance:
- Fines reaching **millions of dollars**
- Criminal penalties for individuals
- Civil lawsuits from affected parties
- Loss of ability to operate in a regulated industry

---

### Key regulations you must know

**GDPR** — the General Data Protection Regulation — applies to *any* organization processing personal data of EU residents, regardless of where the org is located.

- Requires a **lawful basis** for every data processing activity
- **72-hour breach notification** to supervisory authorities
- Must appoint a **Data Protection Officer** (DPO) for certain orgs
- Data subjects have rights to access, correct, erase, and port their data

**CCPA/CPRA** — the California Consumer Privacy Act and its amendment, the California Privacy Rights Act — apply to businesses collecting personal info of California residents.
- Rights to know, delete, and opt out of sale of personal info

**HIPAA** — the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act — applies to healthcare providers, health plans, clearinghouses, and their **business associates** (vendors handling protected health information, or **PHI**).
- Requires **Business Associate Agreements (BAAs)** — written contracts mandating vendors implement safeguards and report breaches

**PCI DSS** — the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard — is *not a law* but a **contractual requirement** enforced by card brands (Visa, Mastercard, etc.).
- Applies to any org that stores, processes, or transmits **cardholder data**
- Noncompliance → monthly fines → loss of ability to accept card payments

**SOX** — the Sarbanes-Oxley Act — applies to **publicly traded U.S. companies**; governs integrity of financial reporting.
- IT controls over financial systems must be documented, tested, and attested to by executives
- Affects access controls, change management, and audit logging of financial data

**GLBA** — the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act — applies to **U.S. financial institutions**.
- Must maintain a comprehensive information security program
- Must provide **privacy notices** to customers about how their data is used

| Regulation | Who it covers | Key hook |
|---|---|---|
| GDPR | Any org touching EU residents' data | 72-hr breach notice, data subject rights |
| CCPA/CPRA | Orgs with CA residents' data | Right to know, delete, opt out |
| HIPAA | Healthcare + business associates | BAAs required for vendors |
| PCI DSS | Any org handling cardholder data | Contractual, not law; lose card privileges |
| SOX | Public U.S. companies | IT controls over financial data |
| GLBA | U.S. financial institutions | Security program + privacy notices |

### Due care vs. due diligence

**Due care** is taking *reasonable minimum precautions* against foreseeable harm — patching systems, using encryption, requiring strong authentication.

**Due diligence** goes further: *actively and continuously* investigating, verifying, and monitoring your own security posture and your third parties' practices.

- Failing due care = **legal liability** for foreseeable harm
- Negligence after a breach is increasingly the basis for civil lawsuits
- Orgs that demonstrate reasonable practices have a stronger legal defense

> Think of due care as wearing a seatbelt, and due diligence as also checking your mirrors, maintaining your brakes, and auditing your driving habits monthly.

---

### Intellectual property protections

**Intellectual property (IP)** — trade secrets, patents, copyrights, and proprietary information — must be protected from unauthorized disclosure.

- Trade secrets classified at highest protection level; protected by access controls, encryption, and **NDAs** (non-disclosure agreements)
- Source code repos secured against both external attacks and insider threats
- **Employee IP agreements** establish that work created on the job belongs to the organization, and employees can't disclose it after leaving

---

### Privacy principles beyond compliance

**Data minimization** — collect *only* the personal data actually necessary for the stated purpose.

**Purpose limitation** — data collected for one purpose cannot be used for another without additional consent or legal basis.

**Consent management** — where consent is the legal basis for processing, it must be specific, freely given, informed, and unambiguous; withdrawal must be easy.

**Privacy impact assessments (PIAs)** — conducted *before* deploying new systems involving significant personal data, to identify and mitigate risks early.

**Privacy by design** — privacy protections built *into* systems from the start, not bolted on after the fact.

---

### Data sovereignty and residency

**Data sovereignty** — the legal principle that data is subject to the laws of the country where it is physically stored.

**Data residency** — the requirement that data remain *physically stored within specific borders*.

- Russia requires personal data of Russian citizens stored on servers in Russia
- China's **PIPL** (Personal Information Protection Law) imposes similar requirements
- **Schrems II** — a EU Court of Justice ruling that *invalidated* the **Privacy Shield** framework previously used to transfer EU data to the U.S.
  - Organizations must now use **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)** or **Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs)** for EU-to-U.S. data transfers
  - Must conduct **transfer impact assessments** to verify destination country's legal framework provides adequate protection

---

### Cloud and remote work considerations

The **shared responsibility model** — cloud providers secure the *infrastructure*; customers are responsible for *what they put in it* (access controls, data protection, monitoring).

- **Hybrid environments** (on-prem + cloud) require consistent security policies across both — operationally complex
- **Remote work** shifts focus to endpoint protection, **VPN** or **zero-trust network access**, and home network security guidance

---

### Organizational culture as a security control

Even perfect technical controls fail if people don't support them.

- Security awareness must be **ongoing**, not a one-time event
- Leadership must **visibly model** secure behaviors
- A **blame-free reporting culture** lets employees report mistakes without fear, giving security teams early-warning visibility before incidents escalate

---

### Recap

- Regulations (GDPR, HIPAA, PCI DSS, SOX, GLBA) impose specific, enforceable obligations based on industry and data type
- **Due care** = minimum reasonable precautions; **due diligence** = continuous, active monitoring and verification
- Privacy principles (minimization, purpose limitation, consent, PbD) go beyond compliance to respect individuals' expectations
- **Schrems II** killed Privacy Shield; EU-to-U.S. transfers now require SCCs or BCRs
- Organizational culture and blame-free reporting are security controls, not soft extras

### Exam flags

- **PCI DSS is contractual, not law** — easy trap; know who enforces it and what noncompliance costs
- **GDPR's 72-hour breach notification window** — a specific, testable detail
- **Due care vs. due diligence** — expect a scenario question distinguishing minimum compliance from ongoing monitoring
