## Risk Management

Risk management is the systematic process of identifying, assessing, and responding to risks so that an organization can make informed decisions about where to spend security resources. This lesson covers the core vocabulary, key processes, and communication tools that Security+ tests heavily across multiple domains.

---

### Core vocabulary

**Risk management** does not aim to eliminate all risk — that is impossible and cost-prohibitive. The goal is to understand, quantify, and manage risk to acceptable levels.

Four foundational terms:

- **Threat** — any circumstance or event with potential to harm organizational assets (e.g., cybercriminals, nation-state actors, natural disasters, accidental misconfiguration, hardware failure)
- **Vulnerability** — a weakness that a threat could exploit (e.g., unpatched software, weak passwords, missing MFA — **MFA**, multi-factor authentication, requiring more than one proof of identity)
- **Asset** — anything of value the org must protect: data, hardware, software, people, facilities, IP, reputation
- **Risk** — the combination of likelihood a threat exploits a vulnerability AND the impact if it does; conceptually: *Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Asset Value*

> Think of it like a triangle: a lit match (threat) near dry wood (vulnerability) in a server room full of equipment (asset) = high risk. Remove any leg and risk drops.

---

### Risk appetite vs. risk tolerance

**Risk appetite** — the amount and type of risk an organization is *willing to accept* in pursuit of its goals; set at the board or senior-management level.

- A bank handling customer deposits → very low appetite for data breach risk
- An early-stage startup → higher appetite for certain risks to move fast

**Risk tolerance** — the *acceptable variation around* the risk appetite; the operational threshold above which a specific risk must be addressed.

| Concept | Analogy | Practical Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Risk appetite** | Posted highway speed limit | "Patch critical vulns within 14 days" |
| **Risk tolerance** | The ~5 mph band before a ticket | Exception allowed if patch breaks a critical app pending testing |

> The speed limit is the policy; the tolerance band is the real-world wiggle room before enforcement kicks in.

---

### The risk register

The **risk register** is the central document that catalogs every identified risk. Think of it as the organization's official risk spreadsheet.

Each entry tracks:
- **Risk owner** — the person accountable for managing that specific risk
- **Inherent risk score** — risk level *before* any controls are applied
- **Residual risk score** — risk level *after* current controls are accounted for
- **Risk response strategy** — what the org plans to do about it
- **Target residual risk**, due date, and current status

> The risk register is the single source of truth for the org's risk posture — reviewed regularly by risk owners and the security team.

### Risk identification techniques

**Threat modeling** — a structured technique for systematically identifying threats to a system or application before they are exploited.

Three frameworks the transcript covers:

- **STRIDE** — categorizes threats into six types: **S**poofing identity, **T**ampering with data, **R**epudiation of actions, **I**nformation disclosure, **D**enial of service, **E**levation of privilege
- **PASTA** — **P**rocess for **A**ttack **S**imulation and **T**hreat **A**nalysis; a risk-centric framework that aligns business objectives with technical requirements
- **Attack trees** — diagrams that model how an attacker could reach a specific goal, branching from the root goal down through prerequisite steps

Additional identification inputs:
- **Vulnerability assessments** — automated scans that find technical weaknesses
- **Penetration testing** — actively exploits vulnerabilities to prove real-world risk (goes further than scanning alone)
- **Threat intelligence feeds** — current attacker TTPs (**TTPs** — tactics, techniques, and procedures, describing how attackers operate)
- **Historical incident data** — e.g., the **Verizon DBIR** (Data Breach Investigations Report), an annual industry report on real breach trends

---

### Risk assessment sequence

1. Identify and value assets
2. Identify threats (threat modeling, intel, history)
3. Identify vulnerabilities (scanning, assessment)
4. Estimate likelihood of exploitation
5. Estimate impact (financial, reputational, operational, regulatory)
6. Calculate risk level (likelihood × impact)
7. Prioritize risks
8. Document in the risk register and present to management

---

### Risk communication

**Risk heat maps** — a two-dimensional matrix with likelihood on one axis and impact on the other, color-coded green (low) / yellow (medium) / red (high); gives executives an instant visual of where the worst risks cluster.

**Quantitative metrics** — express risk in dollar terms (e.g., **ALE** — Annual Loss Expectancy, the expected yearly financial loss from a given risk) so executives can compare security risk to market or credit risk in the same language.

Assessments can be **one-time** (project/acquisition), **recurring** (quarterly/annual), or **ad hoc** (triggered by a new threat or major environment change).

Regulatory mandates:
- **PCI DSS** — annual risk assessments (standard for organizations handling payment card data)
- **HIPAA** — periodic assessments of **ePHI** (electronic protected health information)
- **FISMA** — annual assessments for federal information systems

---

### Recap

- Risk = Threat × Vulnerability × Asset Value; the goal is *management*, not elimination
- **Risk appetite** = strategic limit set by leadership; **risk tolerance** = operational variance allowed around that limit
- The **risk register** tracks inherent risk, residual risk, owner, response, and status for every identified risk
- **STRIDE** names six threat categories; **PASTA** aligns business + technical; **attack trees** map attacker paths
- Regulatory bodies (PCI DSS, HIPAA, FISMA) mandate specific risk assessment frequencies

### Exam flags

- **STRIDE categories** — know all six by name and what each means; expect scenario questions asking which category a described attack falls into
- **Risk appetite vs. risk tolerance** — exam loves the distinction; be ready to identify which concept applies in a scenario
- **Inherent vs. residual risk** — know that inherent = before controls, residual = after controls, and that the risk register tracks both
