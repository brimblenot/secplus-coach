## Power Resiliency

Power is the foundation every IT system stands on — lose it and redundancy, backups, and geographic dispersion all become irrelevant. This lesson covers the full chain of power threats, protection technologies, and architecture decisions that keep systems available when the grid fails.

---

### Power threats and why they differ

Each threat type damages equipment or causes downtime differently, so each needs its own countermeasure.

- **Complete utility outage** — all power gone until the utility restores service (storms, grid overload, planned maintenance)
- **Brownout** — a *sustained* reduction in voltage below normal; causes processor errors, erratic behavior, and shortened component lifespan
- **Voltage sag** — a *brief* dip in voltage (milliseconds to seconds); distinct from a brownout by its short duration
- **Voltage surge** — a brief *increase* above normal voltage
- **Power spike** — a very large, very brief voltage increase (often from lightning); can destroy integrated circuits and capacitors by exceeding their voltage tolerance
- **EMP** (**Electromagnetic Pulse**) — a burst of electromagnetic energy that destroys unprotected electronics across a wide area; caused by high-altitude nuclear detonation, solar **CME** (coronal mass ejection — a large eruption of plasma from the sun), or dedicated weapons. Protection requires a **Faraday cage** — a purpose-built shielded enclosure that blocks electromagnetic fields. Relevant primarily to military and critical national infrastructure.

### UPS architecture: three tiers of protection

A **UPS** (Uninterruptible Power Supply) sits between utility power and connected equipment. It supplies battery-backed power during outages and conditions power quality to block sags, surges, and spikes.

| Architecture | How it works in normal operation | Transfer time on outage | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Offline / Standby** | Equipment runs directly from utility; UPS monitors and switches via a **transfer switch** (internal relay) when power drops | 2–10 ms — long enough to restart some systems | Workstations, tolerant equipment |
| **Line-Interactive** | An **autotransformer** (a voltage-regulating transformer) continuously boosts or trims voltage without touching the battery | Comparable to standby, but more reliable due to active electronics | Most server applications |
| **Online / Double-Conversion** | Utility AC → DC (charges battery) → AC via inverter; equipment *always* runs from the inverter | **Zero** — no transfer switch, no gap | Data centers, critical systems |

> 🔁 **Analogy:** A standby UPS is like a backup generator that takes a few seconds to start. A double-conversion UPS is like already running on the generator 24/7 — when the grid dies, nothing changes.

**Line-interactive advantage over standby:** the autotransformer handles sags and surges electrically, so the battery is used less often, extending battery life. Standby units offer only basic surge protection during normal operation and do not regulate voltage at all.

**Double-conversion trade-offs:** highest cost, more heat (needs extra cooling), and lower efficiency because power is converted twice.

---

### UPS sizing and battery considerations

- **Sizing** = total wattage or **VA** (volt-amperes — the product of voltage × current, used to rate UPS capacity) of all connected equipment + required runtime
- **Runtime goal:** if a generator is present, the UPS only needs to bridge the **1–2 minutes** until the generator stabilizes; standalone battery systems need much larger banks
- **VRLA batteries** (**Valve-Regulated Lead-Acid** — sealed, maintenance-free lead-acid cells): standard; service life **3–5 years**, then capacity degrades and replacement is required
- **Lithium-ion batteries**: higher energy density (more runtime per unit of space), **8–10 year** lifespan, wider operating temperature range — increasingly used in newer UPS designs

---

### UPS management features

- **NMC** (**Network Management Card**) — a card installed in the UPS that exposes status (load, battery charge, alarms) over the network via **SNMP** (Simple Network Management Protocol — a standard protocol for monitoring network devices)
- **Graceful shutdown** — when battery runtime runs low, the UPS sends shutdown commands to servers so they can flush caches and close files cleanly, preventing data corruption from abrupt power loss
- Monitor for: transfer to battery, battery low, overload, battery replacement needed

### Generators: extending runtime from minutes to days

**Diesel generators** are the most common data center choice:
- High energy density, bulk on-site storage, robust and well-understood
- ⚠️ Diesel degrades in **6–12 months**; needs biocide additives and **fuel polishing** (filtering and cleaning stored fuel) to extend life

**Natural gas generators** connect to the utility gas network — effectively unlimited fuel — but depend on that network, which can fail in the same events (e.g., earthquakes) that knock out electricity.

**ATS** (**Automatic Transfer Switch**) — monitors utility power and switches the building electrical system to generator power within seconds of an outage, then switches back automatically when utility power stabilizes. Without an ATS, a technician must be physically present to make the connection manually — unacceptable delay for production systems.

**Generator testing schedule:**

| Frequency | Test type |
|---|---|
| Monthly | No-load start — verify it starts and runs |
| Quarterly | Load test — verify it sustains actual IT load at correct voltage/frequency |
| Annually | Full simulation — ATS engages, all production equipment runs from generator |

### Redundant power distribution and data center tiers

**Dual power feeds** eliminate single points of failure: a server with two power supplies connects each supply to a separate **PDU** (**Power Distribution Unit** — a rack-mounted power strip that distributes power to equipment), separate circuit, separate panel, and ideally a separate utility feed from a different substation. Any single failure in the chain — feed, panel, PDU, or supply — does not interrupt the device.

**PDU types:**
- **Basic PDU** — heavy-duty power strip, no monitoring
- **Metered PDU** — measures total current draw for capacity planning
- **Monitored/switched PDU** — per-outlet metering and remote control of individual outlets

**Uptime Institute Tier classification** rates data center power redundancy:

| Tier | Power path | Availability | Approx. downtime/year |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | Single path, no redundancy | 99.671% | ~28.8 hours |
| II | Single path + redundant components | — | — |
| III | Multiple active paths; any single path handles full load | 99.982% | ~1.6 hours |
| IV | Fully fault-tolerant; multiple simultaneous active paths | 99.995% | ~26 minutes |

> 🔁 **Analogy:** Tier I is a one-lane road to your data center — one accident and everything stops. Tier IV is a full highway interchange with no single point that can block all traffic.

---

### Recap

- UPS types rank in protection: **Standby < Line-Interactive < Double-Conversion**; double-conversion has zero transfer time because equipment always runs from the inverter
- Diesel generators need fuel management (degradation at 6–12 months); natural gas avoids storage but depends on utility infrastructure
- The **ATS** automatically bridges generator to the building; the **UPS** bridges the gap until the generator is ready
- VRLA batteries last **3–5 years**; lithium-ion lasts **8–10 years** with higher density
- Uptime Institute **Tier III = 99.982%** availability; **Tier IV = 99.995%**

### Exam flags

- **UPS architecture comparison** — know what distinguishes each type, especially why double-conversion has zero transfer time and what the efficiency/heat trade-off is
- **ATS vs. UPS roles** — expect a scenario asking which component does what during a generator failover sequence
- **Generator fuel trade-offs** — diesel (storage, degradation) vs. natural gas (no storage, utility dependency) is a classic compare-and-contrast question
