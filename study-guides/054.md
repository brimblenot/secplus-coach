## Indicators of Compromise (IoC)

An **Indicator of Compromise (IoC)** — observable evidence (an artifact, pattern, or behavior) that gives high confidence a system, network, or account has been breached or is actively under attack — is the raw material of incident response. Recognizing, collecting, and acting on IoCs is what separates a team that catches breaches early from one that finds out months later.

---

### Account-based IoCs

These are often the *earliest* signals of compromise.

- **Unusual login times** — account that always authenticates 8 AM–6 PM suddenly logs in at 3 AM from an external IP
- **New geographic locations** — logins from countries with no business relationship to the org
- **Impossible travel** — same account authenticates from New York at 9 AM *and* London at 11 AM; physically impossible for one person, strongly suggesting credential theft
- **Multiple failed logins → success** — indicates password cracking or credential stuffing succeeded
- **Privilege escalation** — standard account suddenly gets admin rights outside normal IT provisioning
- **Dormant account activation** — account unused for months/years suddenly active; attacker using old credentials
- **New accounts with elevated privileges** created outside normal workflow — attacker establishing persistence

> Think of impossible travel like a loyalty card used at a coffee shop in DC and a coffee shop in Tokyo in the same hour — the card was clearly stolen.

---

### Network-based IoCs

- **Large outbound transfers** to previously unseen external IPs, especially off-hours → data exfiltration
- **Beaconing** — compromised host makes periodic, regular connections (e.g., every 60 seconds) to the same external IP; indicates a **C2 (command and control) server** — a remote attacker-controlled system issuing instructions to malware
- **Suspicious DNS queries** — newly registered domains, algorithmically generated domains, or domains with no legitimate reputation
- **Unusual ports** — e.g., port 4444 (used by **Metasploit** — a widely known open-source exploitation framework) or port 6666 suggest unauthorized attacker tools
- **Protocol anomalies** — HTTP on non-standard ports, DNS with abnormally long names or high **TXT record** (a DNS record type that can carry arbitrary text data) query rates → may indicate **tunneling** (hiding malicious traffic inside a legitimate protocol)
- **Tor exit node connections** — **Tor** is an anonymizing network whose exit nodes are publicly listed; connections to them from corporate hosts are a direct red flag
- **Lateral movement** — **SMB** (file-sharing protocol), **WMI** (Windows remote management interface), or **PsExec** (a Microsoft tool for running processes on remote systems) traffic *from workstations to other internal systems* outside normal admin patterns

> Beaconing is like a kidnap victim tapping SOS every 60 seconds — regular, predictable, and easy to spot once you're listening.

---

### Host-based IoCs

- **Unexpected child processes** — e.g., Microsoft Word spawning a **PowerShell** child process (PowerShell is Windows' scripting shell); Word has no legitimate reason to do this → exploitation indicator
- **New services or scheduled tasks** created outside IT activity → attacker persistence
- **Registry modifications** — additions to **Run/RunOnce keys** under `HKEY_CURRENT_USER` or `HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE` (Windows registry locations that automatically launch programs at startup) → malware surviving reboots
- **Unsigned executables** in system directories, or executables in `AppData`/`Temp` directories where legitimate system files don't live
- **Unexpected CPU/memory usage** by unknown processes → possible **cryptomining malware** (malware that hijacks system resources to mine cryptocurrency) or active exploitation
- **Security tools disabled** — antivirus stopping updates, firewall rules changed → malware actively evading detection
- **Unexpected reboots/crashes** on high-uptime servers → exploitation attempt may have destabilized the system

---

### File-based IoCs

- **File hashes** — **MD5**, **SHA-1**, **SHA-256** (cryptographic hash algorithms that produce a unique fixed-length fingerprint of a file); matching a file's hash against a threat intelligence database instantly identifies known malware
- **Masquerading file names** — `svchost32.exe` or `csrss_.exe` (note the underscore) mimicking real Windows process names
- **Unusual locations** — executables in `AppData` or `Temp`; legitimate system executables live in `System32`
- **Script files** (PowerShell, **VBScript** — a Windows scripting language, or batch files) appearing in user profile or temp directories

---

### Email-based IoCs

- **Look-alike domains** — subtle character substitutions mimicking legitimate domains
- **Macro-enabled Office documents** from unknown external senders — common malware delivery mechanism (**macros** are embedded scripts inside Office files that execute code)
- **Base64-encoded content** — **Base64** is an encoding scheme that converts binary data to text; attackers use it to obscure malicious payloads from content filters
- **Inbox forwarding rules** to an external address → classic **business email compromise (BEC)** — attacker quietly reads all email even after the password is reset

### Threat intelligence sharing

| Tool/Standard | What it is |
|---|---|
| **STIX** (Structured Threat Information eXpression) | Open standard *format* for representing IoCs, TTPs, and threat actor data in machine-readable form |
| **TAXII** (Trusted Automated eXchange of Intelligence Information) | The *protocol* that distributes STIX data between organizations |
| **ISACs** (Information Sharing and Analysis Centers) | Sector-specific orgs (finance, healthcare, energy, etc.) that share threat intel among peer organizations |
| **VirusTotal** | Aggregates file hash reputation and antivirus scan results; lets you instantly check if a hash is known malware |

> STIX is the standardized shipping container; TAXII is the truck that moves it between organizations.

---

### MITRE ATT&CK and IoAs

- **MITRE ATT&CK** — a publicly maintained taxonomy of attacker **TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures)** organized by intrusion stage; IoCs can be mapped to specific techniques to understand *what the attacker is trying to accomplish*
  - Example: a process injecting code into another process maps to **T1055 Process Injection** under the Defense Evasion tactic

- **IoA (Indicator of Attack)** — represents behavioral patterns of an *active, in-progress attack* before compromise is confirmed; based on attacker behavior, not specific artifacts
  - Key contrast: **IoCs** confirm a compromise *already happened*; **IoAs** flag an attack *in progress*, enabling response before the attacker completes their objective

---

### IoC management in a SIEM

**SIEM** — Security Information and Event Management system; collects and correlates log data across an environment to detect threats.

- IoCs are operationalized as **correlation rules** that trigger alerts when logs match known-malicious patterns
- Automated blocking on known-malicious IPs and file hashes
- **Threat hunting** — using IoCs to search *historical* log data for past compromises that weren't caught in real time
- **IoC currency matters**: IP addresses and domains change; malware gets new hashes; stale IoCs → **alert fatigue** (analysts overwhelmed by false positives, causing real alerts to be missed)
- Best practice: regularly age out, review, and refresh IoC data; calibrate detection thresholds

---

### Recap

- An **IoC** is observable evidence of a confirmed or likely breach; categories include account, network, host, file, and email indicators
- **Impossible travel**, **beaconing**, and **process injection child processes** are among the highest-confidence IoCs
- **STIX** is the format; **TAXII** is the transport protocol for sharing threat intel; **ISACs** are the sector-based communities that use them
- **IoAs** detect attacks *in progress* (behavior-based); **IoCs** confirm compromise *after* artifacts exist
- Stale IoCs cause alert fatigue — IoC databases require active maintenance to stay useful

---

### Exam flags

- **Impossible travel** as a specific account-based IoC — know why it's high-confidence (same account, two geographically impossible locations, same short time window)
- **STIX vs. TAXII** — expect a question asking which is the format and which is the transport protocol
- **IoC vs. IoA** — know the distinction: IoCs confirm past/current compromise from artifacts; IoAs detect active in-progress attack behavior *before* full compromise is confirmed
