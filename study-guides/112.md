## Risk Management Strategies

Once you've identified and analyzed risks, you have to decide what to do about each one. This lesson covers the four core response strategies, when to pick each one, and the supporting concepts — exemptions, transference mechanics, secondary risks — that appear directly on the Security+ exam.

---

### The four risk response strategies

**Risk mitigation** is reducing a risk by implementing controls that lower either the *likelihood* of exploitation or the *impact* if it succeeds.

- Likelihood controls: patching, **MFA** (multi-factor authentication — requiring a second proof of identity so a stolen password alone isn't enough), least-privilege access, network segmentation
- Impact controls: encryption, backups, incident response plans, cyber insurance
- Even after mitigation, **residual risk** — the risk that remains after controls are applied — persists and must be compared to the organization's **risk tolerance** (the maximum level of risk leadership is willing to accept)
- The threat landscape evolves, so mitigation is never "done"

> Think of mitigation like installing deadbolts AND a security camera: you make the break-in less likely *and* less damaging if it happens anyway.

---

**Risk acceptance** is a *conscious, documented* decision to live with a risk without adding new controls.

- Appropriate when: mitigation cost > expected loss, risk is below tolerance, or the risky system is being retired soon (classic example: skip patching a server being decommissioned in 30 days)
- **Neglect** — ignoring a risk due to inattention or ignorance — is NOT acceptance; the key difference is deliberate, documented decision-making
- Proper acceptance requires: written rationale, signature from the **risk owner** (an accountable senior manager), any **compensating controls** (partial offsets already in place), and a scheduled review date

> Risk acceptance is like signing a waiver before skydiving — you know the risk, you've decided it's okay, and your signature proves it wasn't an accident.

---

**Risk avoidance** eliminates the activity that creates the risk entirely — the most extreme option.

- Examples: stop storing payment card numbers directly (outsource to a payment processor, eliminating **PCI DSS** — Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard — scope); decline to enter a market with unacceptable legal exposure; shelve a technology with unfixable vulnerabilities until the vendor patches it
- Appropriate only when: no feasible mix of mitigation/acceptance/transference brings risk within tolerance *and* discontinuing the activity is operationally viable

> Avoidance is refusing to play a card game where the house always wins — sometimes the only smart move is not to play.

---

**Risk transference** shifts the *financial burden* of a risk to another party; the underlying risk still exists, it just changes who pays when it hits.

- **Cyber liability insurance** — the most common form — covers: forensic investigation, breach notification, credit monitoring, legal fees, regulatory fines, business interruption; average U.S. claim ≈ $300K, major breaches can reach tens of millions
- Premiums reflect risk profile: stronger controls = lower premiums
- **Contractual transference**: **hold-harmless clauses** (contract language releasing one party from liability) and **SLA** (Service Level Agreement) penalties shift financial responsibility to vendors
- Critical point: transference does NOT reduce breach probability, only financial impact
- Organizations retain *ultimate legal liability* for customer/employee data even when a third party processes it

> Cyber insurance is like car insurance — you still drive carefully, but someone else covers the repair bill if you crash.

### Policy exemptions vs. exceptions

Both are closely related to risk acceptance and both require formal documentation.

| | **Policy Exemption** | **Policy Exception** |
|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | Removes a system/user from a policy's scope entirely | Temporary deviation from a policy requirement |
| **When used** | Compliance is *technically impossible* (e.g., embedded OS that breaks if patched) | Compliance is possible but creates an operational conflict right now (e.g., patch crashes a critical app) |
| **Duration** | Typically ongoing while the condition holds | Temporary, with an expected resolution date |
| **Required** | Compensating controls (e.g., network isolation), documented approval, periodic review | Same — approval, documentation, review |

---

### Secondary risks

**Secondary risks** are new risks *created by* a risk treatment activity.

- Deploying backups → the backup infrastructure itself becomes an attack target
- Migrating to cloud to avoid on-prem failure risk → introduces cloud misconfiguration and supply chain risk through the cloud provider
- Must be identified and evaluated before finalizing any treatment decision

---

### Control selection criteria

When choosing a control, evaluate it across four dimensions:

| Criterion | What it measures |
|---|---|
| **Effectiveness** | How much it reduces likelihood, impact, or **ALE** (Annualized Loss Expectancy — the expected yearly cost of a risk) |
| **Cost** | Full lifecycle: purchase, implementation labor, licensing, maintenance, training |
| **Usability impact** | Friction that tempts users to create workarounds, undermining the control |
| **Regulatory compliance** | Whether it satisfies a mandatory requirement regardless of cost-benefit |

---

### Enterprise risk management and the risk register

**ERM** — Enterprise Risk Management — integrates cybersecurity risk with financial, operational, strategic, market, and compliance risks so boards allocate resources appropriately. **CISOs** (Chief Information Security Officers) present risk in financial language (expected loss, residual risk, risk appetite) to enable informed board-level decisions.

The **risk register** — the living document tracking all identified risks — must be:
- Updated when risks are closed, reclassified, or newly discovered
- Reviewed by risk owners quarterly; presented to executive leadership at least annually

---

### Recap

- **Four strategies**: mitigation (reduce), acceptance (document and live with it), avoidance (stop the activity), transference (shift financial burden)
- Acceptance ≠ neglect — acceptance requires documentation, owner sign-off, and a review date
- Exemption = compliance impossible; exception = compliance possible but temporarily conflicts with operations
- Cyber insurance transfers financial impact only — it does not make a breach less likely
- Secondary risks are new risks born from your own risk treatment choices

---

### Exam flags

- **Distinguishing all four risk response strategies** by scenario: the exam gives you a situation and asks which strategy is being used
- **Risk acceptance vs. neglect** and the required documentation elements (rationale, risk owner signature, review date)
- **Exemption vs. exception**: which applies when compliance is impossible vs. temporarily impractical
