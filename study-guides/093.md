## Email Security

Email was built decades ago with almost no security baked in — every defense we have today is a layer added on top of a fundamentally insecure foundation. This lesson covers how spoofing is stopped (SPF, DKIM, DMARC), how enterprise gateways filter threats, how encryption protects message content, and how attackers abuse email when all of that fails.

---

### Spoofing: why it's the core problem

**Spoofing** is when an attacker sends an email that falsely appears to come from a trusted source. **SMTP** — Simple Mail Transfer Protocol, the protocol that carries email between servers — does not verify that the sending server is actually allowed to send on behalf of the claimed domain. An attacker can write anything in the sender field. Three DNS-based standards work together to close this gap.

---

### SPF: who is allowed to send?

**SPF** — Sender Policy Framework — is a DNS TXT record published by a domain owner that explicitly lists which mail servers are authorized to send email on behalf of that domain.

- When a receiving server gets a message, it looks up the SPF record for the domain in the **envelope sender** (the behind-the-scenes "from" address used during SMTP delivery) and checks whether the connecting server's IP address is on the list.
- If the record ends with **`-all`** (hard fail), any server *not* on the list is unauthorized and SPF fails.
- **What SPF cannot do:** it doesn't protect against attackers who already have access to a legitimate mail server, and it doesn't catch **display-name spoofing** — where the visible name looks trustworthy but the actual sending address differs.
- **Forwarding problem:** when mail is forwarded, the forwarding server's IP isn't in the original SPF record, so SPF can fail on legitimate forwarded mail.

> *Think of SPF as a guest list at the door: only servers on the list get in. But if someone sneaks in through a side door (a legitimate server), or just lies about their name badge, the list doesn't help.*

---

### DKIM: cryptographic proof of authenticity and integrity

**DKIM** — DomainKeys Identified Mail — adds a **cryptographic signature** to each outgoing email so the receiver can verify both who sent it and that it wasn't tampered with in transit.

- The domain owner generates an **asymmetric key pair** — a matched set of mathematically linked public and private keys where only the private key can sign and only the public key can verify.
- The **private key** lives on the outgoing mail server and signs each message (hashes the headers + body, then encrypts that hash).
- The **public key** is published in DNS; receiving servers fetch it and verify the signature.
- A valid signature proves: (1) an authorized server signed it, and (2) the signed content was not modified in transit — this is **integrity** protection.
- **Unlike SPF**, the signature travels with the message itself, so it survives email forwarding.

> *DKIM is like a wax seal on an envelope: the seal proves it came from the right person, and any tampering breaks it — even if the envelope changes hands several times before you open it.*

---

### DMARC: policy + alignment + reporting

**DMARC** — Domain-based Message Authentication Reporting and Conformance — sits on top of SPF and DKIM and answers the question: *what should happen to mail that fails those checks?*

- Published as a DNS TXT record at `_dmarc.[domain]`.
- Adds **alignment**: the domain in the visible **From header** must match the domain that passed SPF or DKIM. This closes the loophole where an attacker sets up valid SPF for their *own* domain but makes the visible From address look like yours.

| DMARC Policy | What happens to failing mail |
|---|---|
| `none` | Deliver normally; send reports only |
| `quarantine` | Send to spam/junk folder |
| `reject` | Block delivery entirely ← gold standard |

**Reporting tags:**

- **RUA** — points to an address for **aggregate reports** (regular statistics: how much mail passed/failed SPF and DKIM)
- **RUF** — points to an address for **forensic reports** (details on individual failing messages, useful for spotting spoofing attempts)

> *DMARC is the bouncer who not only checks the guest list (SPF) and the wax seal (DKIM), but also makes sure the name on the invitation matches the name on the badge — and files an incident report when someone fails.*

### Secure Email Gateway (SEG)

A **SEG** — Secure Email Gateway — is the enterprise control point that all inbound (and outbound) mail passes through before reaching users' inboxes. It may be a physical appliance, a virtual appliance, or a cloud service.

**What an SEG does:**

- **Spam filtering** — reputation-based (blocklists, threat intel) + content analysis. **Bayesian filtering** — a statistical technique that learns from labeled examples of spam and legitimate mail to improve accuracy over time.
- **Malware scanning** — signature-based antivirus *plus* behavioral sandboxing for unknown threats (see below).
- **Phishing detection** — checks URLs, flags lookalike domains (visually similar characters or slight misspellings of trusted brands), inspects headers for spoofing signs.
- **DLP on outbound mail** — **DLP** (Data Loss Prevention) monitors outgoing messages for sensitive patterns (credit card numbers, SSNs, classified keywords) and blocks or quarantines violations.

---

### Email sandboxing

**Email sandboxing** is the technique of actually *executing* a suspicious attachment or URL in a fully isolated virtual environment — called **detonation** — to observe its real behavior, rather than just scanning it statically.

- Monitors: network connections (command-and-control callbacks), file system changes (dropped payloads), process creation (malicious child processes), registry modifications (persistence).
- Catches sophisticated malware engineered to evade signature-based detection.
- **Tradeoff:** detonation takes 2–5 minutes, introducing a delivery delay — an accepted cost for the protection against unknown threats.

> *Static scanning is like sniffing a package for known explosives. Sandboxing is like putting the package in a blast chamber and watching what happens when you open it — safely away from anyone who could get hurt.*

---

### URL rewriting and time-of-click analysis

**URL rewriting** addresses a specific attack: a link is benign at delivery time (passes scans), then the attacker swaps it to a phishing page *after* delivery but *before* the user clicks.

- The SEG replaces every URL in delivered mail with a **proxy URL**.
- When the user clicks, the proxy fetches the *current* content of the original URL in real time and re-analyzes it before deciding whether to let the user through.
- **Time-of-click analysis** catches threats that simple delivery-time scanning misses entirely.

### Email encryption

**Encryption in transit vs. end-to-end — these are different things:**

**TLS transport encryption** uses the **STARTTLS** mechanism — STARTTLS is a command that upgrades a plain SMTP connection to an encrypted one. It is **opportunistic**: both servers must support it or mail may fall back to plaintext — so encryption is not guaranteed.

**End-to-end encryption** encrypts the *message content itself*, so only the intended recipient can decrypt it — intermediate servers never see plaintext, regardless of transport security.

| Standard | Trust model | Key type | Native client support |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S/MIME** (Secure/Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions) | **X.509 certificates** — the same certificate authority hierarchy used by HTTPS; certificates are issued and validated by a central authority | Public/private key pair tied to a certificate | Outlook, Apple Mail (built-in) |
| **PGP** / **GPG** (Pretty Good Privacy / GNU Privacy Guard, its open-source implementation) | **Web of trust** — users vouch for each other's keys directly, with no central authority | Public/private key pair | Via plugins |

- **Signing:** sender signs with their *private* key → receiver verifies with sender's *public* key → proves origin.
- **Encrypting:** sender encrypts with recipient's *public* key → only recipient's *private* key decrypts it → ensures confidentiality.
- S/MIME requires both parties to have exchanged certificates first (typically by exchanging signed messages).

> *End-to-end encryption is like sealing a letter in a lockbox that only your recipient has the key to — even if it passes through ten post offices, no one in between can read it.*

---

### Email header analysis

Every email carries **headers** — metadata lines recording the message's path and authentication outcomes. This is a practical forensic skill.

- **Received headers** — each server that handled the message logs itself here; reading them in order traces the actual path from origin to destination.
- **X-Originating-IP** — when present, shows the IP of the user who originally submitted the message.
- **Authentication-Results headers** — show the pass/fail outcome of SPF, DKIM, and DMARC checks performed at each receiving server.
- An SPF fail in these headers means the sending server wasn't on the authorized list; a DKIM validation failure means the message was modified in transit or the signature was fraudulent.

### Business Email Compromise (BEC)

**BEC** — Business Email Compromise — is an attack where the threat actor either compromises a real email account or convincingly impersonates an executive to manipulate employees into authorizing wire transfers, changing payroll accounts, or sharing sensitive data.

- **Why it bypasses normal filters:** BEC emails contain no malicious links or attachments — they are just persuasively written requests. Reputation and signature-based tools have nothing to flag.

**Defenses:**

- **MFA** (Multi-Factor Authentication) on all email accounts — prevents credential-based account takeover.
- **Security awareness training** — teach employees to verify unusual financial requests via a *secondary channel* (e.g., phone call), never by replying to the email.
- **Financial approval workflows** — dual authorization required for wire transfers above a threshold.
- **Monitor for email forwarding rules** — attackers often set up auto-forward rules to silently copy mail to external addresses.
- **Anomaly detection** — alerts on unusual account access patterns.

> *BEC is the con artist who walks in wearing a suit and speaking the right language — no weapon, no disguise, just social engineering. The only defense is a second set of eyes that goes around the attacker entirely.*

---

### Recap

- **SPF** lists authorized sending IPs in DNS; **DKIM** cryptographically signs messages for authenticity and integrity; **DMARC** sets policy for failures and adds From-header alignment — all three together at `reject` is the gold standard.
- **DKIM survives forwarding; SPF does not**, because the forwarding server's IP isn't in the original SPF record.
- An **SEG** applies layered filtering: spam, malware (signature + sandbox), phishing, and outbound DLP.
- **S/MIME** uses X.509 certificates for end-to-end encryption; **PGP/GPG** uses a web-of-trust model — both encrypt content so intermediate servers never see plaintext.
- **BEC** carries no malicious payload and bypasses most filters; defenses rely on MFA, out-of-band verification, and dual-authorization workflows.
