## Threat Actors

Every security incident has someone (or something) behind it. This lesson breaks down *who* attacks organizations, *why*, and *how* — because understanding the attacker shapes every defensive decision you make.

---

### What defines a threat actor

A **threat actor** is any entity that causes an event harming the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of systems or data.

Every threat actor is characterized by five attributes:

- **Location** — inside the organization (insider) or outside (external)
- **Sophistication** — script-user vs. exploit-developer
- **Resources** — pocket money vs. nation-state budget
- **Motivation** — money, politics, revenge, curiosity, espionage
- **Intent** — exfiltrate data, disrupt services, surveil, destroy, or some mix

---

### Nation-state actors and APTs

A **nation-state actor** is a government — or an arm of one, such as a military cyber unit or intelligence agency — that conducts offensive cyber operations as an instrument of national policy. Motivations include espionage, disrupting critical infrastructure, undermining an adversary's economy, and influencing armed conflict.

- Government-level funding means they can hire elite developers, run long operations, and hit many targets at once
- **Advanced Persistent Threat (APT)** — a term describing the combination of high technical sophistication, persistence over months or years, and multi-stage campaigns; closely associated with nation-state actors

**Known APT groups:**

| Group | Also Known As | Attributed To | Notable Attack |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cozy Bear | APT29 | Russia's **SVR** (foreign intelligence service) | SolarWinds supply chain attack |
| Fancy Bear | APT28 | Russia's **GRU** (military intelligence directorate) | DNC breach, 2016 |
| Lazarus Group | — | North Korea's Reconnaissance General Bureau | Sony Pictures hack; WannaCry ransomware |
| APT41 | — | China | State espionage *and* financial cybercrime |

**How APTs operate:**
1. Initial access via spear phishing or zero-day exploits
2. Lateral movement using stolen credentials
3. Slow, careful data exfiltration to avoid detection
4. Multiple redundant backdoors for persistence

**Living off the Land (LotL)** — the APT technique of using built-in OS tools (e.g., **PowerShell** — Windows scripting environment; **WMI** — Windows Management Instrumentation, a system-management framework; **certutil** — a Windows certificate utility) instead of custom malware, because built-in tools rarely trigger antivirus alerts.

**Stuxnet** — a worm jointly attributed to the U.S. and Israel, engineered to sabotage Iranian nuclear centrifuges by altering motor speeds while reporting normal operation to supervisory systems — causing physical destruction while hiding the attack. It proved cyber operations can have real-world kinetic effects.

*Analogy: An APT is like a master burglar who moves into your attic, lives there for months, learns your schedule, copies your keys, and only takes things when you're asleep — never tripping a single alarm.*

### Script kiddies

A **script kiddie** is an unskilled attacker who runs pre-built exploit tools and scripts written by others, without understanding how they work. If the script fails, they cannot diagnose or adapt.

- Motivated by notoriety, thrill, or vague desire to disrupt
- Low resources, typically external, but dangerous in volume — they constantly probe any reachable service

*Analogy: A script kiddie is like someone rattling every door handle in a parking garage hoping one is unlocked — no skill, but eventually someone left their car open.*

---

### Hacktivists

A **hacktivist** is a politically or ideologically motivated attacker who uses hacking as a form of protest. ("Hacker" + "activist.")

- Common tactics: **DDoS** (flooding a service to take it offline), website defacement, data leaks
- **Anonymous** — collective behind Operation Payback (anti-piracy orgs) and Operation Tunisia (Arab Spring support)
- **LulzSec** — combined hacktivist goals with chaos and entertainment
- Mostly external; sophistication varies widely; generally lower resources than nation-states or organized crime

*Analogy: Hacktivists are the digital equivalent of spray-painting a protest slogan on a corporation's front wall — the goal is a public message, not profit.*

---

### Organized crime

**Organized crime** groups treat cybercrime like a business: structured, profit-driven, and resilient. Roles are divided — one team handles initial access, another manages infrastructure, another sells data.

- **Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS)** — a model where a core group builds and maintains the ransomware platform while **affiliates** (partner attackers) run the actual attacks and split ransom payments
- High sophistication: can buy **zero-day exploits** (vulnerabilities unknown to the vendor) and hire skilled developers
- If one attack path is blocked, they immediately pivot to another

*Analogy: A RaaS group is like a franchise — the parent company supplies the "product" and support system; local franchisees do the on-the-ground work and share the revenue.*

---

### Insider threats

An **insider threat** is a current or former employee, contractor, or business partner who abuses legitimate access to cause harm.

- **Malicious insiders** act intentionally — motivated by financial gain, revenge, ideology, or recruitment by an external adversary. They already have valid credentials and know exactly where valuable data lives, so perimeter controls don't stop them.
- **Unintentional insiders** cause harm through negligence — accidental data exposure, falling for phishing, or misconfiguring systems.

Key controls: hiring vetting, background checks, **separation of duties** (splitting critical tasks across multiple people so no one person has total control), **least privilege** (granting only the minimum access needed), and user activity monitoring.

*Analogy: A malicious insider is like a hotel employee who makes a copy of a guest's keycard — security at the front door means nothing.*

---

### Shadow IT

**Shadow IT** refers to cloud services, apps, devices, or infrastructure that employees deploy without IT's knowledge or approval — often using personal credit cards because official procurement feels too slow.

- Not managed: no patching, no access controls, no encryption standards, no monitoring
- If breached, the security team may not even know the data was stored there

*Analogy: Shadow IT is like a coworker storing company files in their personal Dropbox — convenient, but completely outside your security umbrella.*

---

### The hat color system

| Hat | Who They Are | Authorized? |
|---|---|---|
| **White hat** | Security professionals doing authorized pen tests and vulnerability assessments | Yes |
| **Black hat** | Malicious attackers operating outside the law | No |
| **Gray hat** | Probe without authorization but may not intend harm; may notify the org — or demand payment (which becomes extortion) | No, but intent varies |

---

### Recap

- **Nation-state/APT** = most dangerous: government-funded, LotL techniques, persistent multi-stage attacks
- **Script kiddies** = low skill, high volume; **hacktivists** = ideology-driven; **organized crime** = profit-driven with RaaS models
- **Insider threats** bypass perimeter entirely — requires internal controls like least privilege and separation of duties
- **Shadow IT** is an unmanaged internal attack surface created by well-meaning but unsecured employee behavior
- **Attribution** of attacks to a specific actor remains one of the hardest problems in cybersecurity

### Exam flags

- Nation-state actors and APTs: expect questions linking APT traits (sophistication, persistence, LotL) and specific named groups (Cozy Bear/APT29, Lazarus, APT41) to their motivations and example attacks
- Insider vs. external threat: know the distinction between malicious and unintentional insiders and which controls mitigate each
- Hat color definitions: white/black/gray hat distinctions, especially the gray hat's legal ambiguity
