## Security Policies

Security policies are the formal backbone of any organization's security program — they define what must be protected, who is responsible, and what behavior is required. This lesson maps the full policy landscape tested on Security+, from the hierarchy of documents down to specific policy types you must be able to distinguish.

---

### The policy hierarchy

Every security document fits into one of four tiers. Understanding which tier does what is a core exam skill.

- **Policy** — a high-level statement of management intent. Says *what* must happen, not how. Example: "All data must be encrypted."
- **Standard** — a specific, measurable requirement that operationalizes a policy. Example: "AES-256 must be used for all data at rest." Unlike a policy, a standard is precise enough for a technician to implement.
- **Guideline** — advisory best-practice recommendations. Not mandatory — an employee who picks a different approved tool is not in violation.
- **Procedure** — step-by-step instructions for a specific task. Example: every discrete click to enable BitLocker and save the recovery key.

| Tier | Mandatory? | Specificity | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Policy | Yes | Broadest intent | "Encrypt all data" |
| Standard | Yes | Precise & measurable | "Use AES-256 at rest" |
| Guideline | No | Advisory recommendation | "Consider password manager X" |
| Procedure | Yes | Step-by-step task | BitLocker setup checklist |

Think of it like building a house: the policy is the architect's vision ("a safe, comfortable home"), the standard is the building code (exact load tolerances), the guideline is the contractor's preferred brand of nails, and the procedure is the framing manual.

---

### Approval, communication, and maintenance

- Policies must be **approved at senior management level** — CISO, executive team, or (in regulated industries) the board of directors — before they carry organizational authority.
- Must be **communicated to all personnel** and **reviewed at least annually** to stay relevant as threats and technology evolve.

---

### Core policy types

**Information security policy** — the umbrella master document. It defines the scope of the entire security program, assigns high-level roles, and gives every other policy its authority.

**Acceptable use policy (AUP)** — **AUP** is the policy governing what users may and may not do with organizational technology (computers, phones, networks). Key provisions:
- Covers personal use of corporate devices, prohibited content, software installation, and social media use on corporate systems
- **No expectation of privacy** clause: employees are notified their activity may be monitored — this is both an ethical obligation and a legal protection
- Must be **acknowledged at hire** and **recertified annually**
- Also governs **BYOD** — **bring-your-own-device**, the use of personal devices for work

Think of the AUP as the employee rulebook you sign on day one: it's the first thing HR hands you and the first document referenced in a disciplinary hearing.

**Password policy** — sets complexity (minimum length, character mix), expiration intervals, reuse prohibitions, and reset procedures.

**Data classification policy** — defines sensitivity tiers (e.g., public → internal → confidential → restricted), labeling requirements, handling rules, and disposal requirements.

**Clean desk policy** — requires sensitive materials to be locked away and screens locked whenever an employee leaves their desk. Directly mitigates **shoulder surfing** — the act of an unauthorized person observing information on an unattended screen or paper document.

**Social media policy** — restricts what employees may post publicly; prevents disclosure of proprietary information, customer data, and internal details; governs use of the organization's name and logo.

**Remote access policy** — mandates **VPN** use (VPN — a Virtual Private Network that encrypts traffic over an untrusted network) for all connections to internal resources; sets minimum device security requirements; may explicitly prohibit public Wi-Fi without additional controls.

**Incident response policy** — defines what counts as a reportable incident, who to contact, and the escalation chain; answers the critical question: *what does an employee do first when they spot a potential incident?*

**Change management policy** — requires all system or configuration changes to pass through a formal request-and-approval process. Exists because **unauthorized changes** are among the most common causes of outages and security incidents; defines rollback planning requirements.

**Business continuity policy** — sets requirements for maintaining critical operations during disruptions; defines which processes are critical and required redundancy levels; links to the disaster recovery plan.

**Vendor and third-party policy** — sets security requirements suppliers must meet before accessing organizational systems or data; defines assessment, contractual obligations, and ongoing monitoring.

**BYOD policy** — requires enrollment in **MDM** (Mobile Device Management — software that enforces security settings on mobile devices), minimum OS version, device encryption, and **remote wipe** consent (the ability for the organization to erase its data from your personal device).

**Privacy policy** — external-facing notice of how the organization collects, uses, shares, and protects personal data. Required by **GDPR** (General Data Protection Regulation — EU privacy law) and **CCPA** (California Consumer Privacy Act — California privacy law).

---

### Exceptions and enforcement

- **Policy exception process** — allows a formal, temporary deviation from a policy when compliance isn't feasible. Requires: documented risk acceptance by an authorized manager, **compensating controls** (alternative measures that partially offset the elevated risk), a defined expiration date, and a review process.
- **Enforcement** gives policies real authority. Policies without enforcement are aspirational documents, not controls. Where possible, enforce technically — e.g., enforcing the password policy through **Group Policy** settings in **Active Directory** (AD — Microsoft's directory service for centrally managing users and computers on a Windows network) is far more reliable than relying on awareness alone.

---

### Recap

- The hierarchy runs **policy → standard → guideline → procedure**, from broad intent to step-by-step tasks; only guidelines are non-mandatory.
- The **AUP** applies to everyone, includes a no-expectation-of-privacy clause, and must be signed at hire and annually recertified.
- Policies require **senior management approval**, annual review, and consistent **enforcement** — paper-only policies provide no real security.
- The **information security policy** is the master document from which all other policies derive authority.
- **Exceptions** must be formally documented, time-limited, and paired with compensating controls.

### Exam flags

- **Policy vs. standard vs. guideline vs. procedure** — distinguishing which tier is mandatory and which is advisory is a high-frequency scenario question.
- **AUP provisions** — especially the no-expectation-of-privacy clause and the requirement for annual recertification.
- **BYOD policy requirements** — MDM enrollment, remote wipe consent, and encryption mandates are commonly tested.
