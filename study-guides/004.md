## Non-repudiation

Non-repudiation means a party cannot deny having performed an action — it produces cryptographic or auditable proof that is verifiable by a third party. This lesson explains exactly how that proof works and what technologies enforce it.

---

### What non-repudiation actually requires

**Non-repudiation** — a security property guaranteeing that a party cannot credibly deny sending a message, signing a document, or performing an action.

It depends on two assurances working together:

- **Proof of integrity** — the data received is exactly what was sent; nothing was modified in transit.
- **Proof of origin** — the data came from a specific, identifiable party who cannot later deny it.

Integrity alone is not enough. You also need to know *who* created it.

---

### Hashing: the foundation of integrity

A **cryptographic hash function** takes input of any size and produces a fixed-length **hash value** (also called a **message digest** — a fingerprint of the data).

- **SHA-256** — Secure Hash Algorithm producing a 256-bit output; current standard
- **SHA-3** — the next-generation standard algorithm
- **Avalanche effect** — any tiny change to the input produces a completely different hash
- Hashing alone does NOT identify who created the hash — that's why signatures are needed

> Think of a hash like a wax seal impression: if anyone tampers with the letter, the seal breaks and the mismatch is obvious — but the seal doesn't tell you who sent it.

---

### Digital signatures: how non-repudiation is actually proven

A **digital signature** uses **asymmetric key cryptography** — a mathematically linked **key pair**: a **private key** (kept secret by the owner) and a **public key** (freely distributed).

**How it works — step by step:**

1. Sender hashes the plaintext message → produces a hash value
2. Sender encrypts that hash with their **private key** → this is the **digital signature**
3. Sender transmits the plaintext + the digital signature
4. Recipient decrypts the signature with the sender's **public key** → recovers the original hash
5. Recipient independently hashes the received plaintext
6. If both hashes match → two things are confirmed simultaneously:
   - Data was not altered (any change would break the hash)
   - The signer holds the private key (only they could have encrypted it that way)

Because the private key is uniquely possessed by one party, that party cannot claim someone else signed it.

---

### Why HMAC doesn't achieve non-repudiation

**HMAC** — Hash-based Message Authentication Code — uses a **shared secret key** plus a hash function to verify integrity.

| Property | HMAC | Digital Signature |
|---|---|---|
| Integrity | ✅ | ✅ |
| Authentication | ✅ | ✅ |
| Non-repudiation | ❌ | ✅ |

Because both parties share the *same* key in HMAC, a third-party arbitrator cannot determine which party generated the code — either could have made it. A digital signature's private key belongs to exactly one party, so a third party *can* determine who signed.

---

### PKI and Certificate Authorities: anchoring identity

A **CA** (Certificate Authority) — a trusted third party that verifies identities and issues **digital certificates** (documents that bind a public key to an identified entity, signed by the CA).

**PKI** — Public Key Infrastructure — the full system of CAs, certificates, and policies that makes digital signatures trustworthy.

- Without a CA, you can't be sure the public key you're using actually belongs to who you think
- A CA's signature on Alice's certificate means: "we verified Alice owns this key"
- This **chain of trust** is what makes a digital signature legally meaningful

> A CA is like a notary public: they verify identity and stamp a document, so a third party later trusts it came from the right person.

---

### S/MIME: non-repudiation in email

**S/MIME** — Secure/Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions — a standard for applying digital signatures and encryption to email.

- Sender's email client hashes the message, signs with private key, attaches signature + certificate
- Recipient's client verifies automatically
- Most common practical implementation of non-repudiation in enterprise environments

---

### ECDSA: an efficient signature algorithm

**ECDSA** — Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm — produces digital signatures using elliptic curve math, providing the same security strength as **RSA** (a traditional asymmetric algorithm) but with much shorter key lengths, making it faster and computationally lighter.

- Used in **TLS** (Transport Layer Security — the protocol that encrypts web/network traffic), code signing, and modern cryptographic protocols
- Forgery is infeasible due to the mathematical hardness of the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem

---

### Timestamps and audit logs: supporting evidence

**TSA** — Time Stamping Authority — a service that applies a cryptographically signed timestamp to a document, proving *when* a signature was created.

- A digital signature proves *who* signed, not *when* — timestamps fill that gap
- Critical in legal disputes where timing matters (e.g., a contract signed after a deadline)

**Audit logs** — system-level records of user actions (logins, file access, database changes).

- Must be tamper-resistant: append-only storage, cryptographic signing, or real-time export to a **SIEM** (Security Information and Event Management — a platform that aggregates and analyzes security event data)
- If logs can be deleted or altered, their non-repudiation value is destroyed

---

### Proof of origin vs. proof of delivery

| Type | What it proves | Who it protects against denial |
|---|---|---|
| **Proof of origin** | The sender sent it | Sender cannot deny sending |
| **Proof of delivery** | The recipient received it | Recipient cannot deny receiving |

These are handled separately. Read receipts are weak proof of delivery; server-side logging and cryptographic acknowledgments are stronger.

---

### Compliance context

Non-repudiation is a legal requirement in regulated industries:

- **HIPAA Security Rule** — requires audit controls on systems holding protected health information
- **SEC Rule 17a-4 / FINRA rules** — require financial records preserved in a non-alterable format

Failure means regulatory penalties, inability to defend lawsuits, and broken forensic investigations.

---

### Recap

- Non-repudiation = **proof of integrity** + **proof of origin**, together
- **Digital signatures** (not HMAC) achieve non-repudiation because a private key belongs to exactly one party
- A **CA** within PKI certifies that a public key belongs to a specific identity — without this, signatures aren't trustworthy
- **Timestamps (TSA)** prove *when*; digital signatures prove *who*
- **Audit logs** must be tamper-resistant or they provide no non-repudiation value

---

### Exam flags

- **HMAC vs. digital signatures** — expect a scenario asking which mechanism provides non-repudiation; the answer is always digital signatures, because HMAC's shared key means neither party can be singled out
- **The signing process order** — hash first, then encrypt the hash with the private key; know each step and what it proves
- **Role of CAs** — why a digital signature without PKI/CA backing cannot prove identity to a third party
