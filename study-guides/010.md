## Change Management (Domain 1)

Change management is the formal process for planning, approving, implementing, and documenting every modification to IT systems before it touches production. It matters because uncontrolled changes are a leading cause of security incidents, outages, and compliance failures.

---

### Why change management exists

Every change carries risk — a firewall rule edit might expose an internal service; a patch might break a dependent application. **Change management** provides structure to evaluate those risks *before* they hit production, test changes safely, schedule them at low-impact times, and create a full audit trail afterward.

> Think of it like a surgical checklist: even experienced surgeons follow one because the cost of skipping a step outweighs the few seconds it saves.

---

### The core process flow

1. **RFC (Request for Change)** — a formal document submitted when someone needs to change a system. It captures: reason for the change, what will be changed, affected systems/services, the requester, and the designated **owner** (the person responsible for the system being changed).
2. **CAB review** — the governance body evaluates the RFC.
3. **Scheduling** — approved changes are slotted into a **maintenance window** (a scheduled low-traffic period, typically nights/weekends) and communicated to stakeholders in advance.
4. **Implementation + testing** — the change is applied and verified; dependent systems are checked.
5. **Documentation + closure** — the change record is closed, producing an **audit trail**.

---

### The Change Advisory Board (CAB)

The **CAB** — **Change Advisory Board** — is the cross-functional group that approves, rejects, or conditions every non-routine change request. Members typically include IT ops, security, network engineering, app dev, affected business units, and senior management.

The CAB evaluates four dimensions:

| Dimension | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Technical impact | Which systems are directly or indirectly affected? |
| Business impact | Does this hurt revenue, productivity, or customer experience? |
| Security impact | Does this open attack surface, remove controls, or create compliance gaps? |
| Test results | Was this validated in a non-production environment first? |

> The CAB is like a building permit board: your contractor (IT team) can't break ground until the board confirms the blueprint won't damage neighboring structures.

---

### Change categories

| Type | Risk level | Approval path | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | Low, routine | Pre-approved template; no full CAB review needed | Adding a standard user account; replacing a failed disk |
| **Normal** | Moderate–high | Full CAB review required | Firewall rule change, application upgrade |
| **Emergency** | Urgent | Expedited approval (e.g., **CISO** — Chief Information Security Officer — and CTO), then *retrospective* CAB review | Patching an actively exploited zero-day within hours |

---

### Ownership and stakeholders

- The **owner** is the person responsible for a system — often a business unit manager, not the technician who performs the work. Owners ensure testing happens and sign off on **UAT (User Acceptance Testing)** — verifying the system works as expected after the change.
- **Stakeholder identification** must go beyond the obvious. A change to shipping label software can ripple into accounting (billing data), customer service (delivery tracking), and revenue recognition — missing stakeholders means missing objections and preparation gaps.

---

### Impact analysis and test results

**Impact analysis** is the CAB's structured evaluation of what could go wrong. Risks are rated high / medium / low, driving approval requirements and testing obligations.

**Test results** — evidence that the change was validated in a **non-production environment** (dedicated test, **staging/pre-production**, or **sandboxed** environment) — are a required component of any significant RFC.

---

### Rollback plans

A **rollback plan** (also called a **backout plan**) documents the exact steps to undo a change and restore the prior state if something breaks. It must specify who executes each step and how long it takes.

- **Easy to roll back:** uninstalling a software patch and verifying original files
- **Hard or impossible to roll back:** database schema changes that already processed new data; cryptographic key changes after old keys are invalidated

> Always back up the affected system *before* touching production — the backup is the rollback of last resort.

---

### Change freezes and unauthorized changes

A **change freeze** is a defined period when no non-emergency changes are allowed. Example: retail organizations freeze changes from Thanksgiving through New Year to protect peak-revenue stability.

**Unauthorized changes** — including **shadow IT** (business units deploying systems without IT knowledge) — skip security review, lack documentation, and create compliance failures. They are detected via:
- **FIM (File Integrity Monitoring)** — a tool that alerts when files are modified unexpectedly
- **Configuration management tools** that compare live system state against a known-good baseline
- **SIEM (Security Information and Event Management)** — a platform that aggregates logs and alerts on config changes outside approved windows

---

### Configuration management and the CMDB

**Configuration management** maintains accurate records of every system's intended state. These records live in a **CMDB (Configuration Management Database)**. Every approved change updates the CMDB.

**Configuration drift** occurs when a system's actual state diverges from what the CMDB says it should be — caused by unauthorized changes, partial implementations, or software that self-modifies. Drift is a red flag requiring investigation.

> The CMDB is the "source of truth" map of your environment; drift means the territory no longer matches the map.

---

### Recap

- **RFC → CAB review → maintenance window → implement → document** is the mandatory sequence for normal changes
- Change types (standard, normal, emergency) determine how much CAB oversight is required
- Every change needs **impact analysis**, **test results in non-production**, and a **rollback plan** before touching production
- **Configuration drift** signals something changed outside the formal process
- **Change freezes** protect high-stakes periods; **shadow IT** is the most common source of undocumented risk

### Exam flags

- **Emergency change process** — know that it uses expedited approval *then* retrospective CAB review, not no review at all
- **Standard vs. normal vs. emergency change** — be able to match a scenario to the correct category and its approval path
- **Rollback plan requirements** — know which change types are hard/impossible to reverse and why that makes the backout plan critical
