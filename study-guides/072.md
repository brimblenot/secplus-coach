## Capacity Planning (Domain 3)

Capacity planning is the practice of measuring current resource use and projecting future needs so that systems, people, and infrastructure can handle workloads — including peaks and growth — without failing. In security, this matters directly: an overwhelmed SIEM drops logs, an understaffed SOC misses alerts, and a saturated IDS creates blind spots for attackers.

---

### The two failure modes to avoid

**Under-provisioning** means acquiring too few resources, causing exhaustion, outages, and security gaps.
**Over-provisioning** means buying more than needed — idle capacity that wastes budget.

The goal: provision *comfortably above expected peak demand* without excessive idle resources. Get there by measuring utilization, projecting growth, spotting bottlenecks, and acting *before* limits are hit.

---

### People capacity: the slowest resource to scale

**Security Operations Center (SOC)** staffing is tiered:

| Tier | Role | What they do |
|------|------|--------------|
| Tier 1 | Alert triage | Review alerts, decide what to escalate |
| Tier 2 | Investigation | Correlate events across sources, confirm incidents |
| Tier 3 | Threat hunting / forensics | Proactively search for undetected threats; analyze confirmed incidents |

- Recruiting + onboarding takes **months** — you cannot staff up reactively.
- Plan based on **projected** demand, not today's headcount.
- **MSSP** — Managed Security Service Provider — a vendor that delivers analysts, tooling, and 24/7 monitoring as a service, letting you supplement internal staff without the hiring burden.
- **Succession planning**: if one person holds unique expertise and leaves, that's a capacity gap too.

*Think of Tier-1 analysts like an emergency dispatch center: if call volume exceeds dispatcher capacity, some emergencies just go unanswered.*

---

### Technology capacity: compute, network, and security tools

**Elastic scaling** — a cloud capability (AWS, Azure, GCP auto-scaling groups) that automatically adds or removes compute instances as demand rises and falls, so you provision for the average, not the worst-case peak.

**Network utilization rule of thumb**: links above ~**70% sustained utilization** develop rising latency and packet loss, degrading both app performance and security tool effectiveness.

**NetFlow / IPFIX / sFlow** — network telemetry protocols that record traffic flow metadata (who talked to whom, how much); used by flow analysis tools to spot bandwidth trends and bottlenecks.

**IDS/IPS** — Intrusion Detection / Prevention System — a security appliance that inspects network traffic for attack signatures. Critical capacity rule: it must process traffic at **line rate** (as fast as traffic arrives). If it can't keep up, it either introduces latency or **drops packets**, creating blind spots where attacks pass undetected.

---

### SIEM capacity: the data-volume challenge

**SIEM** — Security Information and Event Management — a platform that ingests, parses, correlates, stores, and makes searchable log data from across the environment.

- Licensing is often **volume-based** (cost grows with data ingested).
- During major incidents, log volumes **spike dramatically** — SIEM must handle peak, not just average, ingestion.
- **Log retention** mandated by regulations: commonly **1–7+ years**.
- **Tiered storage**: recent logs in fast, expensive storage for quick searches; older logs moved to cheaper archive storage while remaining accessible.

*A SIEM is like a courtroom stenographer: if they can't type fast enough, parts of the testimony vanish forever — and regulators don't accept "we ran out of space" as an excuse.*

---

### Infrastructure capacity: power, cooling, and addresses

**PUE** — Power Usage Effectiveness — ratio of *total* data center power to *IT equipment* power only.
- **PUE of 1.0** = theoretical perfection (zero overhead).
- **Lower PUE = more efficient** facility.
- Power and cooling are the **hardest and most expensive** to expand — plan these with the longest lead times.

**IP address space planning** ensures the network addressing scheme can grow without forcing a disruptive full renumbering.

---

### Security-specific capacity concerns

- **DDoS mitigation**: if attack traffic exceeds your mitigation infrastructure's capacity, the mitigation fails. **Cloud-based DDoS services** absorb attacks across a global network, offering effectively unlimited capacity — but it must be explicitly provisioned and tested beforehand.
- **Patch deployment**: if you can patch 500 systems per change window and have 10,000 vulnerable systems, that's 20 windows of exposure — a real vulnerability window attackers can exploit.
- **EDR agents** — Endpoint Detection and Response software running on every endpoint — collectively consume CPU and RAM on those hosts; older systems may be resource-constrained.
- **Cloud service quotas**: providers cap virtual machines, storage volumes, and IPs per account by default. Review quotas against growth projections and submit **quota increase requests before limits are hit in production**.

---

### Recap

- **Under-provisioning creates security gaps**; over-provisioning wastes budget — the goal is comfortable headroom above projected peak demand.
- **People are the slowest resource to scale**: SOC staffing must be planned months ahead; MSSPs can supplement internal capacity.
- **IDS/IPS must run at line rate** — a device that can't keep up drops packets and goes blind.
- **SIEM** faces volume-based licensing, spike ingestion during incidents, and multi-year retention mandates solved by tiered storage.
- **PUE measures data center power efficiency**; lower is better; power/cooling have the longest planning horizons.

### Exam flags

- **SOC tier definitions** (Tier 1 triage → Tier 2 investigation → Tier 3 threat hunting/forensics) and why staffing can't be reactive.
- **IDS/IPS line-rate requirement** and what happens when it fails (packet drops = blind spots).
- **SIEM capacity triad**: volume-based licensing, peak-ingestion sizing, and tiered log retention storage.
