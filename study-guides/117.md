## Privacy (Domain 5)

Privacy is about individuals' right to control their own personal data — not just keeping it safe, but ensuring it's collected, used, and shared on their terms. This lesson covers the core principles, data categories, individual rights, and program components you need for the exam.

---

### Privacy vs. security

**Privacy** is the right of individuals to control how their personal information is collected, used, shared, and retained. **Security** protects information from unauthorized access, modification, and disclosure.

- Security is a *necessary condition* for privacy, but not sufficient on its own
- An org can have strong security and still violate privacy — e.g., retaining data longer than needed, or sharing it without consent

> Think of security as locking the filing cabinet, and privacy as the rules about who's allowed to open it and why.

---

### Core privacy principles

These appear in GDPR, the OECD Guidelines, and the Fair Information Practice Principles — all expressing the same ideas:

| Principle | What it requires |
|---|---|
| **Lawfulness, fairness, transparency** | Legal basis for processing; no surprises; plain-language notice |
| **Purpose limitation** | Collect for a specific reason; don't reuse for incompatible purposes |
| **Data minimization** | Collect only what's actually necessary — not "might be useful someday" |
| **Accuracy** | Keep data current; correct or remove inaccurate records |
| **Storage limitation** | Delete or anonymize once the purpose is fulfilled |
| **Integrity and confidentiality** | Implement technical/organizational security controls |
| **Accountability** | The **data controller** — the entity that decides how personal data is processed — must demonstrate compliance |

> Purpose limitation in action: using a customer's email address (collected for order confirmations) to send marketing is a violation — that's a new, incompatible purpose.

### Personal data categories

| Category | Definition | Key law |
|---|---|---|
| **PII** (Personally Identifiable Information) | Any info that identifies or can identify a specific individual | General |
| **Direct identifiers** | Uniquely ID an individual alone: SSN, passport, biometrics | General |
| **Quasi-identifiers** | ID an individual only in combination (zip + birthdate + gender) | General |
| **PHI** (Protected Health Information) | Health-related PII: diagnoses, prescriptions, insurance records | HIPAA (US) |
| **Special category data** | Highest-sensitivity PII under GDPR: race, religion, genetics, biometrics, health, sexual orientation | GDPR |

Special category data requires **explicit consent** or a specific legal exception to process — a much higher bar than ordinary PII.

> Quasi-identifiers are like puzzle pieces — harmless alone, but combine three of them and you can often pinpoint exactly who someone is.

### Privacy by Design — seven principles

**Privacy by Design** is a framework requiring privacy to be built into systems from the start, not bolted on later. The seven principles:

1. **Proactive, not reactive** — prevent privacy harms before they happen
2. **Privacy as the default** — maximum protection automatically, no user action required
3. **Privacy embedded into design** — part of the architecture, not an add-on
4. **Full functionality** — privacy, security, and usability coexist (not a trade-off)
5. **End-to-end security** — protection across the entire data lifecycle, collection through deletion
6. **Visibility and transparency** — processing is verifiable by users, auditors, regulators
7. **Respect for user privacy** — user-centric; individuals can exercise their rights

> Privacy by Design is like building a house with insulation in the walls — far cheaper and more effective than trying to add it after construction is complete.

### GDPR data subject rights

**GDPR** — General Data Protection Regulation, the EU's comprehensive data protection law — grants individuals these rights:

| Right | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Right of access (DSAR)** | **Data Subject Access Request** — receive a copy of all data held + processing info |
| **Right to rectification** | Request correction of inaccurate/incomplete data |
| **Right to erasure** | "Right to be forgotten" — delete data when no longer needed or consent withdrawn |
| **Right to restriction** | Pause processing while accuracy/lawfulness dispute is resolved |
| **Right to data portability** | Receive data in machine-readable format for transfer to another controller |
| **Right to object** | Object to direct marketing (must be honored) or legitimate-interest processing |
| **Automated decision-making rights** | No purely automated decisions with significant legal effects without human review option |

**Response timeframes:**
- **GDPR:** 30 calendar days (up to 2-month extension for complex requests, with notice)
- **CCPA** — California Consumer Privacy Act, California's equivalent law — **45 days**

---

### Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA / DPIA)

A **Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA)**, called a **Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)** under GDPR, is a systematic evaluation of privacy risks *before* a new data processing activity begins.

- Required when processing poses **high risk**: large-scale sensitive data, public surveillance, or extensive automated profiling
- Assesses: nature/scope/purpose of processing; necessity; risks to individuals; mitigations
- If **high residual risk remains** after mitigation → must consult the **supervisory authority** before proceeding

> A DPIA is like an environmental impact study before construction — you map the damage you might cause and fix it before breaking ground.

---

### Consent requirements

For **consent** to be a valid legal basis for processing under GDPR, it must be:

- **Freely given** — no penalty for refusing
- **Specific** — separate consent per purpose, no bundling
- **Informed** — individual understands what they're agreeing to
- **Unambiguous** — requires a clear affirmative action; silence, pre-ticked boxes, or inactivity do NOT count

Organizations must keep records of consent and provide an easy withdrawal mechanism.

---

### Cross-border data transfers

GDPR restricts EU personal data from leaving the **EEA** — European Economic Area — without an approved safeguard:

| Mechanism | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Adequacy decision** | European Commission certifies a non-EU country's laws are equivalent to GDPR; data flows freely |
| **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)** | Model contract terms approved by the Commission, signed between EU exporter and non-EU importer |
| **Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs)** | Intra-group rules for multinational companies moving data between their own entities across borders |

---

### Recap

- Privacy ≠ security: security is necessary but not sufficient — misuse of properly secured data is still a privacy violation
- GDPR's seven principles (purpose limitation, minimization, storage limitation, etc.) are the conceptual backbone of all data protection law
- Special category data and PHI require stricter legal bases than ordinary PII
- GDPR DSAR response = **30 days**; CCPA = **45 days**
- Privacy by Design means privacy is proactive, default, and embedded — not patched in afterward

### Exam flags

- **GDPR data subject rights and response timeframes** (30-day DSAR, right to erasure triggers, automated decision-making rules) — highly likely to appear
- **Distinguishing PII / PHI / special category data** and when heightened protection applies
- **Consent validity requirements** (freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous — and what invalidates it)
