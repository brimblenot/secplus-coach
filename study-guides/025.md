## Other Social Engineering Attacks

Social engineering goes far beyond phishing. This lesson covers the full range of manipulation techniques — from fake domains to AI-generated voices — and the psychological levers that make them work. Understanding these is essential for both the exam and real security program design.

---

### Typosquatting and bitsquatting

**Typosquatting** (also called **URL hijacking**) is the practice of registering domain names that are common misspellings or near-identical variations of a legitimate site, so users who mistype a URL land on the attacker's page instead.

- The fake page may harvest credentials, serve malware via **drive-by download** — malware that installs automatically just by visiting a page — or display ads for revenue
- Attackers register *hundreds* of variants; even a tiny fraction of mistyped traffic is profitable
- Examples: `gooogle.com`, `paypa1.com` (numeral 1 replacing lowercase L)

**Bitsquatting** is a variant targeting domains that differ from a real domain by exactly one flipped bit — exploiting the rare case where memory corruption changes a character in a stored URL, capturing traffic from high-volume servers making millions of DNS lookups.

> Think of typosquatting like opening a fake storefront one door down from a famous restaurant, betting that distracted customers will walk in by mistake.

### Influence campaigns

An **influence campaign** is a coordinated effort — often nation-state sponsored — to manipulate public opinion or spread disinformation using fake online accounts and social media amplification.

- Attackers create **sockpuppets** — fake accounts designed to look like independent real users
- Sockpuppets post, like, and share each other's content, tricking algorithms into amplifying it to real audiences
- Once real users engage, traditional media may pick it up, turning manufactured content into mainstream narrative
- Used to undermine democratic institutions, deepen social divisions, or create confusion before/during cyberattacks

**Brand impersonation in search results** is related: attackers create many sites using well-known brand names so they appear in search results, then lure visitors into downloading malware or submitting credentials.

> Sockpuppets are like a handful of paid audience members applauding loudly — the real crowd starts clapping because they assume the show must be good.

### Invoice scams and Business Email Compromise

An **invoice scam** targets **accounts payable** — the department that pays vendors — by submitting fraudulent invoices for goods or services never provided, betting that high invoice volume means some slip through unapproved.

**Business Email Compromise (BEC)** — an attack where the attacker impersonates a trusted person via email — supercharges invoice scams: the attacker poses as a known vendor and requests a change to payment routing details, redirecting future *legitimate* payments to attacker-controlled accounts.

> It's like slipping a fake bill into a thick stack of real ones on a busy accountant's desk — most get rubber-stamped without a second look.

### Psychological principles exploited by social engineers

These are the levers attackers pull. Know all seven:

| Principle | What it exploits | Example attacker move |
|---|---|---|
| **Authority** | People comply with perceived authority figures | Impersonating an executive or law enforcement |
| **Urgency / Scarcity** | Time pressure degrades decision quality | "Your account locks in 1 hour — act now" |
| **Social proof (Consensus)** | People copy what others appear to do | "Other departments already submitted this" |
| **Intimidation** | Fear of consequences overrides resistance | Threatening termination or legal action |
| **Familiarity / Liking** | We comply more readily with people we like | Building rapport before making a request |
| **Reciprocity** | We feel obligated to return favors | Offering help first, then asking for something |
| **Fear** | Broad anxiety about loss, embarrassment, or blame | Fake alerts claiming the user caused a breach |

### Hoaxes and diversion theft

A **hoax** is false information spread to make targets harm themselves without any real attack tool being deployed — for example, a fake virus warning telling users to delete a legitimate system file.

**Diversion theft** is physically misdirecting a legitimate delivery — a package, document, or payment instrument — to a location the attacker controls, by social engineering the delivery company, sender, or recipient.

---

### Deepfake attacks

A **deepfake** is AI-generated audio or video that realistically impersonates a specific person.

- **Audio deepfakes** clone a voice; documented cases show fake CEO voices authorizing wire transfers over the phone
- **Video deepfakes** can fabricate a convincing video call appearance, making impersonation even harder to detect
- Quality is improving rapidly, increasing the threat over time

> An audio deepfake is like a perfect voice actor who studied hours of someone's recordings — the target has no reason to doubt they're talking to the real person.

---

### Defenses

- **Security awareness training** — teach employees to recognize psychological triggers and create a safe reporting culture
- **Verification procedures** — confirm sensitive requests via a *separate known channel*, never just calling the number the caller provided
- **MFA** — even if credentials are stolen socially, the attacker can't authenticate without the second factor
- **Multi-person financial approval workflows** — require multiple sign-offs on wire transfers and payment detail changes to counter BEC/invoice scams

---

### Recap

- Typosquatting registers misspelled domains; bitsquatting targets single-bit memory errors in URLs
- Influence campaigns use sockpuppet networks to manufacture apparent popularity and manipulate real audiences
- Invoice scams + BEC redirect legitimate payments by impersonating vendors and changing routing details
- Seven psychological levers: authority, urgency/scarcity, social proof, intimidation, familiarity/liking, reciprocity, fear
- Deepfakes use AI-generated voice or video to impersonate real people in fraud scenarios

### Exam flags

- **Psychological principles** — expect scenario questions where you identify which principle (urgency, authority, reciprocity, etc.) the attacker is using
- **Typosquatting vs. bitsquatting** — know both definitions and what distinguishes them (user typo vs. memory bit-flip)
- **BEC + invoice scam combination** — know how impersonating a vendor to change payment routing details ties these two techniques together
