## Business Impact Analysis (BIA)

A **BIA** — Business Impact Analysis — is the foundational planning document that identifies which business processes are critical and quantifies the harm caused by disrupting them. It drives every downstream decision about backup frequency, recovery site investment, and system restore order.

---

### What the BIA actually answers

The BIA is not primarily a security document — it's a business resilience document that security teams help build. It answers four questions:

- Which processes are most critical to the organization's mission?
- What happens operationally and financially if those processes go down?
- How long can the business tolerate each disruption before suffering **irreversible harm**?
- What resources are needed to recover each critical process?

> 🏥 Think of it like a hospital triage system: before any disaster hits, you've already ranked which "patients" (processes) die first without care, so resources go to the right place fast.

---

### The five BIA steps

**Step 1 — Identify critical processes:** Work with business unit leaders to find what directly supports revenue, compliance, or essential services (e.g., payment processing, patient care, order entry).

**Step 2 — Map resource dependencies:** For each critical process, list every IT system, person, facility, and third-party service it relies on. One broken dependency can kill the whole process.

**Step 3 — Calculate MTD:** **MTD** — Maximum Tolerable Downtime (also called **MTPD**, Maximum Tolerable Period of Disruption) — is how long a process can be offline before the organization suffers *irreversible* harm: regulatory violations, permanent customer loss, or financial failure. Process owners define this; financial modeling validates it.

**Step 4 — Analyze escalating impact:** Model what happens at 1 hour, 4 hours, 24 hours, 72 hours, 1 week. Different categories of harm kick in at different thresholds.

**Step 5 — Set RTO and RPO:** These targets then drive all continuity and disaster recovery planning.

---

### RTO — how fast you must recover

**RTO** — Recovery Time Objective — is the maximum acceptable time between a disruption and full restoration of a system or process. It's a *planning target*, not a guarantee.

Tighter RTOs require more expensive infrastructure:

| RTO Target | Recovery Approach | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| < 1 hour | **Active-active** (both instances always running) or **hot standby** with synchronous replication + automated failover | Highest |
| 4–24 hours | **Warm standby** with asynchronous replication + manual failover | Moderate |
| > 24 hours | **Cold site** with tape restoration | Lowest |

> Don't apply one aggressive RTO to every system — that's extremely expensive. Set RTOs by criticality.

> 🏎️ RTO is like a pit-stop time limit: a Formula 1 team (hot standby) can swap tires in 2 seconds; a weekend driver (cold site) might take 30 minutes.

---

### RPO — how much data loss is acceptable

**RPO** — Recovery Point Objective — is the maximum acceptable data loss, measured in time. An RPO of 4 hours means backups must run *at least* every 4 hours so no more than 4 hours of transactions are lost in a disaster.

- **RPO = 0:** Zero data loss tolerated → requires **synchronous replication** — every write to the primary is simultaneously written to a secondary site. Most expensive option; reserved for critical transactional systems.
- **Larger RPO:** Periodic backups are sufficient; cost drops significantly.

> 📷 RPO is your last save point in a video game — if you save every 4 hours, you can lose up to 4 hours of progress when the game crashes.

---

### MTBF and MTTR — reliability metrics

**MTBF** — Mean Time Between Failures — is the average time between successive failures of a repairable component. Published by hardware manufacturers for drives, servers, and power supplies.

- Higher MTBF = more reliable hardware
- Can be calculated empirically: total uptime ÷ number of failures

**MTTR** — Mean Time to Repair — is the average time to restore service after a failure, including diagnosis, parts procurement, repair, reconfiguration, and testing.

- MTTR is *reducible* through investment: on-site spare parts, vendor support contracts with guaranteed response times, and documented procedures all lower it
- Organizations invest in lower MTTR to meet RTO targets they couldn't otherwise hit

| Metric | Measures | Improved By |
|---|---|---|
| MTBF | Reliability (how often things break) | Buying higher-quality hardware |
| MTTR | Recovery speed (how fast you fix it) | Spare parts, support contracts, runbooks |

---

### Impact categories beyond financial loss

A BIA captures five types of harm:

- **Financial:** Lost revenue, emergency overtime, regulatory fines, legal costs, insurance deductibles
- **Reputational:** Customer loss, brand damage, negative media — hardest to quantify, often the biggest long-term harm
- **Operational:** Inability to manufacture, deliver services, or process orders
- **Legal/regulatory:** Contract failures, compliance violations, mandatory breach reporting
- **Life and safety:** For healthcare, utilities, or transportation — disruption endangers the public

---

### BIA documentation and maintenance

Key outputs: prioritized process list with RTOs/RPOs, multi-horizon impact analysis, dependency maps, restore sequence, and resource requirements.

**Review frequency:** At minimum annually, and after any major change to processes, infrastructure, or org structure.

---

### Recap

- The BIA identifies critical processes, maps dependencies, and quantifies harm at multiple downtime durations — it drives all continuity planning
- **MTD** = point of irreversible harm; **RTO** = target restore time; **RPO** = maximum tolerable data loss in time
- Tighter RTO → more expensive architecture (active-active → warm standby → cold site)
- **MTBF** measures reliability; **MTTR** measures repair speed — both can be managed through investment
- Impact categories include financial, reputational, operational, legal/regulatory, and life/safety

### Exam flags

- **RTO vs. RPO distinction:** Know that RTO governs *time to restore service* and RPO governs *acceptable data loss*, and how each maps to specific technical solutions (synchronous replication for RPO = 0; active-active for aggressive RTO)
- **MTD as the ceiling:** MTD defines the absolute limit above which harm becomes irreversible — RTO must always be set *below* MTD
- **MTBF vs. MTTR:** Know what each measures and how organizations reduce MTTR to meet RTO requirements
