## Security Controls

Security controls are the mechanisms, policies, procedures, and safeguards organizations use to protect systems, data, people, and physical assets. This lesson maps out the **four categories** and **six functional types** of controls — the framework Security+ uses to evaluate every defensive measure you'll encounter on the exam.

---

### The four categories of security controls

**Technical controls** (also called logical controls) use technology to enforce security policy.
- Examples: firewalls, antivirus/antimalware, OS access policies, encryption, IDS/IPS
- **SIEM** — Security Information and Event Management system; aggregates logs from across an environment, correlates events, and alerts analysts to potential incidents
- *Think of technical controls as the locks, alarms, and cameras built into the system itself.*

**Managerial controls** (also called administrative controls) are implemented through documentation, policies, and procedures — not technology or people directly.
- Examples: overall security policy, onboarding access policies, risk assessments, SOPs, governance documents
- These set the rules that every other control is designed to enforce.
- *Think of managerial controls as the employee handbook: it defines the rules but doesn't physically stop anyone.*

**Operational controls** rely on people to carry out security processes.
- Examples: security guards, security awareness training (e.g., lunch-and-learn sessions), awareness posters, property patrols, incident response teams
- If a human is directly performing the security function, it's operational.
- *Think of operational controls as the staff on the floor — they only work as well as the people executing them.*

**Physical controls** limit physical access to buildings, rooms, systems, and devices.
- Examples: perimeter fencing, door locks, badge readers, guard shacks, bollards, security lighting
- **Bollards** — short, sturdy posts installed to prevent vehicle ramming attacks
- Usually the first layer an attacker encounters.
- *Think of physical controls as the building's skeleton — walls, doors, fences.*

---

### The six functional types of security controls

Each type answers the question: *What does this control actually DO?*

| Type | What it does | Example per category |
|---|---|---|
| **Preventive** | Stops an incident before it happens | Firewall rule (technical); background-check policy (managerial); guard checking IDs (operational); server-room door lock (physical) |
| **Deterrent** | Discourages attackers without necessarily blocking them | Login warning banner (technical); termination policy for unauthorized access (managerial); staffed reception desk (operational); "Trespassers prosecuted" sign (physical) |
| **Detective** | Identifies and logs events after or as they occur — does not prevent, but ensures incidents are discovered | SIEM log correlation (technical); weekly login-report review policy (managerial); property patrol (operational); motion detectors (physical) |
| **Corrective** | Applied *after* detection to reverse damage or restore operations | Restoring from a clean backup after ransomware (technical); incident-escalation policy (managerial); contacting law enforcement (operational); fire extinguisher (physical) |
| **Compensating** | Alternative control used when the primary control can't be implemented (cost, time, technical limits) | Firewall rule blocking exploit traffic while awaiting a patch (technical); separation of duties (managerial); multiple simultaneous guards (operational); backup power generator (physical) |
| **Directive** | Instructs people to behave securely — weakest type because it depends on voluntary compliance, not enforcement | File-storage policy directing use of encrypted folders (technical); compliance procedures (managerial); phishing-awareness training (operational); "Authorized Personnel Only" sign (physical) |

**Key contrast — deterrent vs. preventive:** A deterrent *raises the perceived cost* of an attack to discourage the attempt; a preventive control *mechanically blocks* the action. A warning sign deters; a locked door prevents.

**Key contrast — directive vs. preventive (physical example):** An "Authorized Personnel Only" sign is directive — it gives an instruction but doesn't stop anyone. A key-card lock on the same door is preventive — it physically enforces access.

---

### One control, multiple types

A single control can fit multiple types simultaneously.
- A **CCTV camera** is both **detective** (records activity) and **deterrent** (visible cameras discourage attackers) — and it spans technical and physical categories.
- The exam may present a control and ask you to identify *all* applicable types, not just one.

---

### Defense-in-depth

**Defense-in-depth** is the design philosophy of layering controls from all four categories and multiple types so that if one control fails, others remain to detect, correct, or compensate. No single control is assumed to be sufficient.

---

### Recap
- There are **4 categories**: technical, managerial, operational, physical
- There are **6 types**: preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating, directive
- These form a **4×6 matrix** — a control can occupy multiple cells at once
- **Directive controls are the weakest** — they instruct but don't enforce
- **Defense-in-depth** = layering controls so failure of one doesn't mean total compromise

### Exam flags
- Given a scenario, **identify both the category AND the type** of a described control — the exam tests the full matrix, not just one dimension
- **Compensating controls** are a high-frequency scenario topic: recognize them when a patch or primary control is unavailable and a workaround is deployed
- **Directive vs. preventive** is a common distractor: a policy or sign that *tells* people what to do is directive, not preventive
