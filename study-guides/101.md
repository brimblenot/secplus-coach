## Incident Response

Incident response (IR) is the structured, pre-planned process an organization follows when a security breach or cyberattack occurs. Getting it right depends almost entirely on work done *before* an incident happens — planning, tooling, and practice determine outcomes more than anything done in the moment.

---

### Core terminology

Three terms that sound interchangeable but are not:

- **Event** — any observable occurrence on a system or network (a login, a firewall block, a file creation). Routine; extremely high volume.
- **Alert** — an event or group of events that a security tool has flagged as potentially significant, requiring review. May be a **true positive** (real threat) or **false positive** (noise).
- **Incident** — a confirmed security event with negative impact on the **CIA triad** (confidentiality, integrity, or availability). Only confirmed incidents trigger the full IR process.

> Think of it like a hospital triage system: events are every patient who walks in, alerts are patients the triage nurse flags, and incidents are the ones confirmed to need a doctor *right now*.

---

### The PICERL lifecycle

**PICERL** — the six-phase incident response model defined by **NIST SP 800-61** (NIST Special Publication 800-61, the federal guide for computer security incident handling):

| Phase | Core objective |
|---|---|
| **Preparation** | Build capability *before* incidents occur |
| **Identification** | Detect and confirm an incident is happening |
| **Containment** | Limit spread; preserve evidence |
| **Eradication** | Remove the threat completely |
| **Recovery** | Restore clean, verified systems |
| **Lessons Learned** | Improve before the next incident |

---

### Preparation

The most important phase — its quality determines how well every other phase goes.

Key preparation activities:
- Write the **incident response plan** and **playbooks** (pre-defined step-by-step procedures for specific attack types)
- Train the **CSIRT** — **Computer Security Incident Response Team** — in roles and responsibilities
- Configure logging and monitoring for visibility
- Define **out-of-band communication channels** — channels that bypass potentially compromised infrastructure (e.g., personal cell phones, a separate email system)
- Assemble an **incident go-bag**: forensic workstations, portable storage, **write blockers** (hardware devices that allow reading evidence drives without altering them), and licensed forensic software
- Establish contacts with forensic vendors and law enforcement

> Like a firefighter's gear staged by the door — the moment the alarm sounds, you grab it and go; there's no time to shop for equipment.

---

### Identification and detection

Detection sources include **SIEM** (Security Information and Event Management — a platform that aggregates and correlates logs from across the environment) alerts, **EDR** (Endpoint Detection and Response — software that monitors endpoints for malicious behavior) detections, user reports, threat intelligence, partner/law enforcement tips, or the attacker's own ransom note.

**Triage** during this phase:
- Confirm true positive vs. false positive
- Assess initial scope and severity
- Determine which systems are affected, what data may be at risk, and how long access may have existed

---

### Containment

Two stages:

- **Short-term containment** — isolate affected systems from the network immediately. **Do not power off** — powering off destroys volatile memory, which may hold running attacker tools, encryption keys, or active network connections.
- **Long-term containment** — patch the exploited vulnerability, harden adjacent systems, segment the network, add monitoring for reentry attempts.

Key tension: move fast enough to limit damage, but don't tip off the attacker (who may still have access) prematurely — they might accelerate activity or destroy evidence.

---

### Eradication

Remove everything the attacker left behind:
- All **malware** — malicious software installed by the attacker
- All **persistence mechanisms**: backdoor accounts, scheduled tasks, **registry run keys** (Windows registry entries that auto-launch programs at startup), and **web shells** (attacker-uploaded scripts that provide remote access through a web server)
- Patch all exploited vulnerabilities; reset all compromised credentials

Incomplete eradication = reinfection. **Threat hunting** — proactively searching for attacker activity across the environment — is valuable here to find affected systems not yet identified.

---

### Recovery

- Restore from **known-good backups** that predate the incident
- Rebuild from scratch with hardened baselines when backup integrity is uncertain
- Restore services in **business-priority order**
- Monitor restored systems closely for renewed attacker activity
- Verify the root cause is fixed before reconnecting systems

---

### Lessons learned

Held within days of resolution while memory is fresh. Reviews the full timeline, what worked, what failed, gaps in detection, and playbook adequacy. Produces **action items** to improve controls, tools, and training.

> Think of it as a sports film session — watch the tape, find the breakdowns, fix them before the next game.

---

### Severity classification

| Severity | Examples | Response timeline |
|---|---|---|
| **Critical** | Active breach, ransomware encrypting production, confirmed regulated-data exfiltration | Immediate; full mobilization |
| **High** | Suspected breach, privileged account compromise | Within hours |
| **Medium** | Malware confined to one endpoint, policy violation with security impact | Within 24 hours |
| **Low** | Failed attack attempt, minor policy violation | Within days |

---

### Communication and legal considerations

- **Incident war room** — physical or virtual hub centralizing responder coordination
- Restrict information to **need-to-know** — broadcasting the breach could tip off an insider or an attacker still in the environment
- Involve **legal counsel** early; **attorney-client privilege** can protect sensitive incident communications
- Track **regulatory notification deadlines**:

| Regulation | Notification requirement |
|---|---|
| **GDPR** (EU data privacy law) | Supervisory authority within **72 hours** of awareness |
| **HIPAA** (US healthcare data law) | Up to **60 days** for breach notification |
| **SEC** cybersecurity rules (US public company disclosure) | **4 business days** after determining materiality |

---

### Playbooks

A **playbook** is a pre-written, tested, step-by-step procedure for a specific incident type. Eliminating in-the-moment procedural decisions under stress is the whole point.

Examples:
- **Ransomware playbook** — isolate hosts, find patient zero, preserve forensic evidence, assess backup integrity, engage external IR help
- **Phishing playbook** — analyze email headers, quarantine messages from all mailboxes, identify every recipient who clicked, force password resets, block indicators
- **Privilege escalation playbook** — identify accounts with new elevated privileges, investigate how escalation occurred, remediate the vulnerability, verify no backdoors remain

---

### Tabletop exercises and the CSIRT

A **tabletop exercise** is a discussion-based scenario walkthrough that practices IR decision-making and communication without activating real systems — no live infrastructure is touched.

**CSIRT roles:**
- **Incident commander** — coordinates overall response
- **Technical analysts** — conduct investigation
- **Forensics specialists** — preserve and analyze evidence
- **Communications coordinator** — manages internal and external messaging
- **Legal representation** — handles privilege and regulatory obligations

---

### Recap

- **Event → Alert → Incident**: only confirmed CIA-impact events are incidents and trigger full IR
- **PICERL** is the NIST 800-61 lifecycle; Preparation is the most important phase
- During Containment, **never power off** — volatile memory holds critical forensic evidence
- Eradication must be complete; missed persistence mechanisms cause reinfection
- Regulatory notification deadlines: **GDPR = 72 hours**, **HIPAA = 60 days**, **SEC = 4 business days**

### Exam flags

- **PICERL phase objectives and order** — expect scenario questions asking which phase a described action belongs to
- **Regulatory notification timeframes** — GDPR 72 hours, HIPAA 60 days, SEC 4 days are high-frequency exam targets
- **Volatile evidence / no power-off rule** — frequently tested as a scenario: what do you do first when you reach a compromised system?
