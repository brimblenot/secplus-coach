## Risk Analysis

Risk analysis is how security professionals decide *which risks matter most* and *how much to spend fixing them*. This lesson covers the two core methodologies — qualitative and quantitative — plus the specific formulas and risk types the Security+ exam tests.

---

### Qualitative risk analysis

**Qualitative risk analysis** is a method that rates risks using descriptive, subjective categories rather than dollar amounts — it tells you *how bad* a risk is relative to others, not exactly what it will cost.

- Rates two dimensions: **likelihood** (how probable is the threat?) and **impact** (how severe are the consequences?)
- Typical scales: Rare → Almost Certain; Negligible → Catastrophic — often mapped 1–5
- **Risk score** = likelihood rating × impact rating (e.g., 4 × 5 = 20)
- **Heat map** — a color-coded 2D matrix (likelihood on one axis, impact on the other) where red = high risk, yellow/orange = medium, green = low; lets executives grasp the risk picture at a glance

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Fast, simple, needs less data | Subjective — two analysts may rate the same risk differently |
| Works without precise financial data | Results aren't consistent across organizations |
| Easy for non-technical audiences | Cannot calculate ROI for controls (no dollar values) |

> Think of a heat map like a weather radar: you don't need exact wind-speed numbers to know the red blob is the storm you evacuate for.

### Quantitative risk analysis — core formulas

**Quantitative risk analysis** assigns monetary values to risks so you can calculate expected financial loss and compare it against the cost of controls — producing a true return-on-investment figure.

**Asset Value (AV)** — the full monetary worth of an asset to the organization: not just replacement cost, but revenue generated, data stored, regulatory fines from compromise, and all related costs.

**Exposure Factor (EF)** — the *percentage* of AV that would be lost if a specific threat occurred, expressed as a decimal (0.0–1.0). A complete server destruction = EF of 1.0; a ransomware attack that destroys 40% of recoverable data = EF of 0.4.

**Single Loss Expectancy (SLE)** — the expected dollar loss from *one* occurrence of a threat against one asset.
> **SLE = AV × EF**
> Example: Server worth $50,000, EF = 1.0 → SLE = $50,000

**Annualized Rate of Occurrence (ARO)** — how many times per year a specific threat is expected to happen. ARO of 0.1 = once every 10 years; ARO of 2.0 = twice per year. Geography and industry matter: hurricane ARO is far higher in Florida than Montana.

**Annualized Loss Expectancy (ALE)** — the expected total dollar loss from a risk *over a full year*.
> **ALE = SLE × ARO**
> Example: Laptop (AV = $1,000, EF = 1.0, SLE = $1,000) stolen 7×/year (ARO = 7) → ALE = $7,000/year

**Control ROI check:** If a laptop-tracking solution costs $2,000/year and drops ALE from $7,000 to $2,000, the reduction is $5,000 — net benefit = $5,000 − $2,000 = **$3,000/year**. If a control costs *more* than the ALE reduction it produces, the math doesn't justify it.

| Term | Formula | What it answers |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| SLE | AV × EF | Cost of one bad event |
| ALE | SLE × ARO | Expected annual cost of this risk |
| Control ROI | ALE reduction − control cost | Is the control worth buying? |

> Think of ALE like car insurance math: the insurer multiplies the chance of a crash (ARO) by the average payout (SLE) to decide what to charge you.

### Types of risk

- **Inherent risk** — risk *before* any controls exist; your raw, natural exposure
- **Residual risk** — risk *after* controls are applied; what you're left living with
- **Control risk** — the risk that a control itself fails (misconfiguration, bypass, outage)
- **Detection risk** — the risk that a problem goes *unnoticed*, letting a threat persist or escalate

The goal: use controls to bring inherent risk down to a residual risk level within the organization's **risk tolerance** — the threshold of acceptable exposure.

---

### Data sources for quantitative analysis

- **Marsh / AON** — insurance actuarial data on real-world financial losses by incident type
- **Verizon DBIR / VERIS** — empirical breach frequency and characteristics data across industries; **VERIS** = Vocabulary for Event Recording and Incident Sharing, the underlying data schema
- **Ponemon Institute** — annual research on breach costs: per-record cost, notification, business interruption
- **FAIR** (Factor Analysis of Information Risk) — a structured quantitative framework for measuring information risk in financial terms, designed for executive and financial audiences

---

### Recap

- Qualitative uses subjective scales + heat maps; fast but no dollar values or ROI
- Quantitative chains AV → EF → SLE → ARO → ALE to produce justifiable control spend
- ALE is the key metric: if control cost > ALE reduction, the investment isn't financially justified
- Inherent risk is pre-control; residual risk is post-control; control risk and detection risk can undermine both
- Risk assessments should be reviewed periodically (typically quarterly) and after significant environmental changes

### Exam flags

- **ALE formula chain** — expect a scenario where you must calculate SLE or ALE from given numbers, or evaluate whether a control is cost-justified
- **Qualitative vs. quantitative trade-offs** — know which method fits which situation and why qualitative cannot produce ROI calculations
- **Risk type definitions** — inherent, residual, control, and detection risk are all distinct terms likely to appear in scenario questions
