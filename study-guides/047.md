## Wireless Attacks

Wireless networks are uniquely vulnerable because the transmission medium — radio frequency — is physically accessible to anyone within range, no physical building access required. This lesson covers the major attack types and why each one works.

---

### Evil twin attacks

An **evil twin** is a rogue wireless access point that broadcasts the same **SSID** (Service Set Identifier — the human-readable network name) as a legitimate network, tricking clients into connecting to the attacker instead.

- If the attacker's signal is stronger, clients will prefer it automatically
- Advanced versions also spoof the **BSSID** (Basic Service Set Identifier — the access point's MAC address), making the two networks indistinguishable
- Once connected, the attacker runs a **man-in-the-middle** — intercepting credentials, session tokens, and unencrypted traffic
- Often paired with a deauth attack (see below) to forcibly kick clients off the real AP
- Best defense: **EAP-TLS** within **WPA2-Enterprise** — the client checks the AP's certificate, and a rogue AP can't forge a valid one

> Think of an evil twin like a fake ATM placed next to a real one with identical branding — you insert your card thinking it's the bank's machine, but everything you do goes to the attacker.

---

### Deauthentication attacks

A **deauthentication attack** exploits the original 802.11 standard's failure to authenticate management frames. **Deauthentication frames** — control messages telling a client to disconnect — were sent in cleartext with no verification.

- An attacker spoofs the legitimate AP's MAC address and broadcasts a deauth frame
- All targeted clients disconnect and cannot reconnect while the attacker keeps sending frames
- Used standalone as a **denial-of-service** or as the setup step for an evil twin
- **802.11w** — the Management Frame Protection (MFP) amendment — fixes this by encrypting and authenticating sensitive management frames under WPA2/WPA3
- Caveat: beacon, probe, and association frames *cannot* be protected by 802.11w because encryption hasn't been established yet at that stage

> It's like a fake fire alarm pull — the attacker impersonates building authority, everyone evacuates (disconnects), and is now in the parking lot (the evil twin) instead of the real office.

---

### WPA2 KRACK

**KRACK** (Key Reinstallation Attack), disclosed in 2017, exploited a flaw in the **WPA2 four-way handshake** — the process by which a client and AP negotiate session encryption keys.

- The standard allows handshake messages to be *retransmitted* if confirmations aren't received
- An attacker manipulates retransmission to force the client to reinstall an already-used key while resetting the **nonce** (a number-used-once counter that must never repeat)
- Reusing a nonce in **AES-CCMP** (the WPA2 cipher) breaks its security guarantees — allowing traffic decryption, packet replay, and in some cases data injection
- Affected *all* WPA2 implementations regardless of vendor or cipher suite
- Fixed via OS/firmware patches; **WPA3** was developed in part to address the underlying architectural weakness

> Imagine a combination lock that resets to zero every time you retry the code — KRACK forced that reset so the attacker could predict the combination.

---

### WPA3 and Dragonblood

**WPA3** replaced the WPA2 **PSK** (Pre-Shared Key) exchange with **SAE** (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals), also called the **Dragonfly** handshake.

- SAE provides **forward secrecy** — past sessions can't be decrypted even if the passphrase is later compromised
- Resistant to offline dictionary attacks by design
- **Dragonblood** — a set of vulnerabilities disclosed shortly after WPA3 launched — used timing and cache **side-channel attacks** against specific SAE implementations to enable offline dictionary attacks
- Partially mitigated by updates to the WPA3 spec, illustrating that even new standards need continuous review

---

### WPS PIN brute-force and Pixie Dust

**WPS** (Wi-Fi Protected Setup) lets users join a network via button-press or an 8-digit PIN instead of a full passphrase — convenient but critically flawed.

- The AP validates the PIN in **two independent halves** (first 4 digits checked separately from last 4; final digit is a checksum)
- This reduces the brute-force space from ~100 million to ~**11,000 combinations** — crackable in hours with tools like **Reaver**
- A successful attack recovers the full WPA2 passphrase
- **Pixie Dust** goes further: it exploits weak random number generation in some APs to recover the PIN in *seconds* without iterative brute force
- **Defense: disable WPS entirely**

> Splitting the PIN into two halves is like a bank vault with two separate 4-digit locks — you only need to crack each one independently, cutting the combinations from millions to thousands.

### Wireless eavesdropping

**Wireless eavesdropping** captures and analyzes over-the-air traffic to extract sensitive data. How dangerous it is depends entirely on the encryption in use.

| Protocol | Status | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **WEP** (Wired Equivalent Privacy — original 802.11 encryption) | Completely broken | 24-bit **IV** (Initialization Vector — a value that must be unique per packet) reused so often that statistical analysis recovers the key in minutes; tools like **aircrack-ng** need only thousands of packets |
| **WPA2 (CCMP/AES)** | Resistant to passive sniffing, but not immune | Captured four-way handshake can be cracked offline with **hashcat** — GPU acceleration makes short/common passphrases vulnerable |

> WEP is like a combination lock where the combination is printed on the lock itself after enough people turn the dial — the math leaks the key.

### War driving and war chalking

**War driving** is scanning for wireless networks while mobile — using a wireless adapter in **monitor mode** (passive capture without associating) to record SSIDs, signal strength, encryption type, and GPS location.

- **War chalking** was the practice of marking physical locations with symbols indicating a nearby network's presence and type
- Still a valid reconnaissance technique for identifying target networks

---

### Rogue access points

A **rogue access point** is an unauthorized AP plugged into a corporate network — often by an employee wanting better coverage, sometimes by an attacker with physical access.

- Bypasses perimeter security controls; anyone within radio range gets a path into the internal network
- Detected by a **WIDS** (Wireless Intrusion Detection System — a system that monitors the RF environment), which compares observed BSSIDs against an approved inventory

---

### Wireless jamming

**Wireless jamming** floods the target frequency with RF interference, degrading the signal-to-noise ratio until communication fails.

- Affects all devices in range on a given frequency band
- Illegal in most jurisdictions
- Located physically via **triangulation** using a directional antenna (radio operators call this a **fox hunt**)

---

### Bluetooth and NFC attacks

**Bluetooth** has its own attack taxonomy distinct from Wi-Fi:

| Attack | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Bluejacking** | Sends unsolicited messages to nearby devices via the Bluetooth discovery process |
| **Bluesnarfing** | Unauthorized access to a device's data (contacts, messages, calendar) by exploiting vulnerabilities in protocols like the **OPP** (Object Push Profile) |
| **Bluebugging** | Full remote control of a device — calls, messages, data access |
| **BlueBorne** (2017 vulnerability set) | Exploited Bluetooth stack flaws in multiple OSes; required *no pairing or user interaction* |

**NFC** (Near-Field Communication — a short-range wireless protocol used for contactless payments) has its own attack:

- **NFC relay attack** — an attacker captures an NFC transaction at one location and replays it at another, completing a payment with a victim's card without ever physically holding it

> An NFC relay is like someone holding a phone up to your wallet in a crowd, then using a partner across town to tap a reader at the same moment — your card "appears" to be in two places at once.

---

### Recap

- **Evil twin** spoofs SSID/BSSID and MitMs traffic; defeated by **EAP-TLS** certificate validation
- **Deauth attacks** exploit unsigned 802.11 management frames; **802.11w** (MFP) is the fix
- **KRACK** forced nonce reuse in WPA2's handshake; patched but inspired WPA3's **SAE**
- **WPS PIN** is crackable in hours (~11,000 combos) due to split-half validation; **disable WPS**
- **WEP** is completely broken via IV reuse; **WPA2** handshakes are vulnerable to offline dictionary attacks

---

### Exam flags

- **Evil twin + deauth combo** — know that deauth forces clients onto the rogue AP, and that EAP-TLS certificate verification is the specific countermeasure
- **WPS PIN flaw** — know the split-half math (11,000 not 100 million) and that a successful attack yields the full WPA2 passphrase
- **802.11w / Management Frame Protection** — know what it protects (deauth/disassoc frames) and what it *cannot* protect (beacons, probes, association frames)
