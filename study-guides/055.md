## Segmentation and Access Control

Network segmentation and access control are two of the most foundational ideas in defensive security architecture. This lesson covers how networks are divided into isolated zones, how traffic between those zones is controlled, and how access control models determine who can reach what — and why.

---

### Physical segmentation

**Physical segmentation** means dividing a network using genuinely separate hardware — distinct cables, switches, and ports for each segment. Traffic between segments can only flow through an explicitly configured device such as a firewall or router.

- Highest level of isolation available
- Most expensive: dedicated hardware per segment
- Used where sensitivity is extreme (classified government networks, industrial control systems)

Think of physical segmentation like separate buildings with no doors between them — you can only get from one to the other by going through a guarded checkpoint.

---

### VLANs and VLAN attacks

A **VLAN** — Virtual Local Area Network — provides logical segmentation at **Layer 2** (the data-link layer of the OSI model, which handles MAC addressing and switching within a local network) without requiring separate physical hardware. Managed switches assign each port to a VLAN, creating separate **broadcast domains** — isolated groups within which traffic circulates freely. Traffic between VLANs must cross a Layer 3 router or firewall.

**VLAN hopping** attacks try to bypass VLAN boundaries without traversing a router:

| Attack | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Double-tagging** | Crafts a frame with two **802.1Q tags** (the standard header a switch uses to mark which VLAN a frame belongs to); the first switch strips the outer tag, and the inner tag sends the frame to the target VLAN on the second switch |
| **Native VLAN attack** | Exploits the fact that traffic on the **native VLAN** (VLAN 1 by default) is sent untagged on trunk links — an attacker on the native VLAN can reach other VLANs through certain trunk configurations |

**Defenses:**

- Change the native VLAN away from the default VLAN 1 to an unused VLAN
- Disable **DTP** — Dynamic Trunking Protocol, a protocol that lets switches automatically negotiate trunk links — on access ports
- Apply **VLAN ACLs** — access control lists that filter traffic inside and between VLANs

### DMZ architecture

A **DMZ** — Demilitarized Zone — is a network segment that sits between the untrusted internet and the trusted internal network, hosting public-facing servers (web, email, public DNS).

- Firewall rules allow inbound internet connections to DMZ servers on specific ports
- Outbound connections from DMZ servers to the internal network are restricted
- A **dual-firewall DMZ** uses two separate firewalls: one between the internet and the DMZ, and a second between the DMZ and the internal network — so an attacker who compromises a public server still must defeat a second firewall to reach internal systems

A DMZ is like a reception lobby: visitors from outside can enter it, but a locked interior door separates them from the private offices.

---

### Microsegmentation and east-west vs. north-south traffic

**Microsegmentation** isolates individual workloads, applications, or containers from each other — not just broad network zones. Each server or container can communicate only with the specific other workloads it has a legitimate reason to reach.

- **East-west traffic** flows between servers and services inside the data center — lateral, internal paths
- **North-south traffic** flows between the data center and external networks (internet, remote users)
- Traditional perimeter security focused on north-south and left east-west largely uncontrolled, giving attackers room to move laterally after an initial compromise
- Microsegmentation dramatically reduces the **blast radius** — the scope of damage — after a single host is compromised
- Implemented in **software-defined networking** — networks where routing and policy are controlled by software rather than static hardware configuration — and cloud platforms
- **Zero trust** architectures depend on microsegmentation because they eliminate the idea of an implicitly trusted internal network

If traditional segmentation is dividing an office building into floors, microsegmentation is locking every individual office door — so even a person already inside the building can only reach exactly the rooms they need.

---

### Air gaps

An **air gap** is a system or network with absolutely no electronic connection to any external network — the ultimate physical isolation.

- Used for the most sensitive systems: nuclear facilities, classified national security networks, some industrial control systems
- Data transfer only via approved physical media under strict procedures
- **Stuxnet** — a malware worm discovered around 2010 — demonstrated the limits of air gaps: it spread into an Iranian nuclear facility's air-gapped network through infected USB drives, showing that air gaps block network-based attacks but not supply chain compromise or insider threats using physical media

---

### Jump servers

A **jump server** (also called a **bastion host**) is a hardened, minimally configured system that acts as the single controlled access point into a protected network segment.

- Administrators must authenticate to the jump server first; all sessions are logged
- All administrative traffic enters the protected segment through this one chokepoint
- Subject to heightened patching and monitoring

A jump server is like a secure airlock: you can't walk straight into the clean room — you must pass through the airlock first, where every entry is recorded.

### Access control models

**Access control** defines the rules by which subjects (users, processes) are granted or denied access to objects (files, databases, systems). The model determines who makes access decisions and what factors into them.

| Model | Who decides access | Key property |
|---|---|---|
| **MAC** — Mandatory Access Control | The OS/security kernel, based on classification **labels** assigned to both subjects and objects | Absolute: a Secret-cleared user cannot access Top Secret data regardless of any other factor; users cannot share their access |
| **DAC** — Discretionary Access Control | The resource **owner** | Flexible: file owner grants/denies permissions; used in Windows NTFS and standard Unix permissions; risk: a compromised account can share its access |
| **RBAC** — Role-Based Access Control | Permissions are assigned to organizational **roles** (accountant, developer); users are assigned to roles | Simplifies administration at scale — adding a new employee means assigning a role, not configuring every permission individually |
| **ABAC** — Attribute-Based Access Control | Decisions combine **user attributes** (department, clearance), **resource attributes** (data classification), and **environmental attributes** (time of day, device posture) | Most flexible and expressive; can enforce complex policies; most complex to implement |
| **Rule-based** | Static rules applied regardless of user identity | Example: firewall ACLs — a rule permitting port 80 traffic applies to all matching packets with no reference to who sent them |

---

### Need-to-know, separation of duties, and least privilege

**Need-to-know** — users access only the specific information required for their current task, even if their clearance level would technically permit broader access.

**Separation of duties** — no single person can complete a critical transaction alone. Dividing the steps between two or more people reduces fraud, error, and insider threat. **Dual control** (also called **two-person integrity**) is a specific implementation requiring two authorized individuals to both be present and act — for example, to open a safe containing cryptographic material.

**Least privilege** — every account, service account, and process receives only the minimum permissions needed for its function. Accounts with excess permissions give an attacker more capability if those accounts are compromised.

**PAM** — Privileged Access Management — encompasses specialized controls for high-risk administrative accounts:

- **Just-in-time access**: elevated permissions granted only when needed, revoked immediately after
- **Session recording**: all privileged sessions logged for audit
- **Password vaulting**: credentials for privileged accounts stored and automatically rotated

---

### Recap

- Segmentation — physical, VLAN, DMZ, microsegmentation, air gap — limits **lateral movement** after a compromise; it doesn't prevent initial compromise
- VLAN hopping (double-tagging, native VLAN attack) is defeated by changing the native VLAN, disabling DTP, and using VLAN ACLs
- MAC is label-enforced and absolute; DAC is owner-controlled; RBAC uses roles; ABAC uses combined attributes; rule-based uses static rules independent of identity
- Least privilege, need-to-know, and separation of duties are complementary principles that together minimize what any one compromised account or insider can do
- Air gaps block network-based attacks but not physical-media threats — Stuxnet is the canonical example
