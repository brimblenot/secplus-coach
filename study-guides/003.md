## CIA Triad

Every security control, policy, and architecture ultimately protects one thing: information. The CIA Triad gives you a three-part framework for describing *what* you're protecting and *why* — and it shows up on the exam constantly.

---

### What the CIA Triad is

The **CIA Triad** is a foundational security model whose three letters stand for **Confidentiality**, **Integrity**, and **Availability** — the three core objectives every security program is built to protect. It is drawn as a triangle because all three sides must stay balanced; weakening any one weakens your overall security posture.

- Also called the **AIC Triad** to avoid confusion with the U.S. Central Intelligence Agency — same concept, different order.
- Its adversarial mirror is the **DAD Triad** — **Disclosure**, **Alteration**, **Destruction** — one DAD outcome maps to each CIA pillar (see table below).

| CIA Pillar | DAD Threat | Example attack |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Disclosure | Data breach, eavesdropping |
| Integrity | Alteration | Man-in-the-middle, SQL injection |
| Availability | Destruction | Ransomware, DDoS |

---

### Confidentiality

**Confidentiality** is the property that ensures information is accessible only to authorized parties — preventing unauthorized disclosure of sensitive data.

Key mechanisms:

- **Encryption** transforms readable **plaintext** into scrambled **ciphertext** — unreadable without the decryption key. Protects data **in transit** (crossing a network) and **at rest** (sitting on storage). Algorithms include **AES** (Advanced Encryption Standard — symmetric) and **RSA** (asymmetric).
- **Access controls** — lists, role-based permissions, group membership — restrict who can read or modify data. The **need-to-know principle** says grant access only to what someone needs for their specific job; a marketing employee shouldn't see payroll data.
- **Data classification** labels data by sensitivity so protection matches risk:

| Level | Who can see it | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Public | Anyone | Press releases |
| Internal/Private | Employees only | Internal memos |
| Confidential/Sensitive | Business-need only | Trade secrets |
| Regulated | Legally governed | **HIPAA** — health data; **PCI DSS** — payment card data |

- **MFA** (**Multi-Factor Authentication**) — requiring something you *know* + something you *have* or *are* — stops attackers who steal a password alone.
- **Steganography** — hiding secret data *inside* another file (e.g., embedding a message in an image) — conceals the existence of sensitive information, not just its contents.

> Think of encryption as a locked safe for your data: even if a thief grabs the safe, they can't read what's inside without the combination.

---

### Integrity

**Integrity** is the property that ensures data has not been altered — accidentally or maliciously — during storage or transmission.

Key mechanisms:

- **Hashing** — a hash function takes any input and produces a fixed-length **hash value** (also called a **message digest**). Any change to the input, even one bit, produces a completely different hash. Compare hashes before and after transmission to detect tampering. Common algorithms: **SHA-256**, **SHA-3**. **MD5** is now cryptographically weak and should not be used for security.
- **Digital signatures** combine hashing with asymmetric cryptography. The sender hashes their message, then encrypts that hash with their **private key** — that encrypted hash *is* the signature. The recipient decrypts it with the sender's **public key** and compares hashes. If they match: data is unaltered and the sender is authenticated. Provides **non-repudiation** (see below).
- **FIM** (**File Integrity Monitoring**) — continuously watches the hash values of critical system files and alerts admins to unexpected changes, useful for detecting malware that modifies OS files.
- **Change control** — a managerial process requiring documentation and approval before any system change — prevents unauthorized or accidental modifications.

**Non-repudiation** is the ability to prove, beyond doubt, that a specific entity performed a specific action so they cannot later deny it. Digital signatures provide this because only the holder of a private key could have produced that signature — critical in legal and financial contexts.

> A digital signature is like a wax seal on a letter: it proves who sent it and shows instantly if anyone tampered with the envelope.

---

### Availability

**Availability** is the property that ensures systems and data are accessible to authorized users whenever needed — the pillar users feel most directly.

Key mechanisms:

- **Redundancy** — duplicate components so one failure doesn't stop service. **RAID** (**Redundant Array of Independent Disks**) adds disk-level redundancy: RAID 1 mirrors data across two drives; RAID 5 uses striping with parity (needs 3+ drives, tolerates 1 failure); RAID 6 tolerates 2 simultaneous failures.
- **Fault tolerance** — the ability to keep operating when components fail. **High availability clusters** — groups of servers where a failed node's workload is automatically taken over by another, often invisibly to users. **Load balancers** — devices that spread traffic across multiple servers, improving both performance and resilience.
- **Backups** — regular, *tested* copies of data so ransomware or corruption doesn't end operations. Defined by two objectives: **RTO** (**Recovery Time Objective**) — the maximum acceptable time to restore a system; **RPO** (**Recovery Point Objective**) — the maximum acceptable data loss measured in time (how old can the restore point be?).
- **DDoS** (**Distributed Denial of Service**) mitigation — an attacker floods a system with traffic until legitimate users can't reach it; defenses include traffic scrubbing and rate limiting.
- **UPS** (**Uninterruptible Power Supply**) — a battery device that keeps systems online through power outages — a physical availability control.
- **SLA** (**Service Level Agreement**) — a contractual uptime guarantee, e.g., 99.9% ≈ 8.7 hours downtime/year; 99.99% ≈ 52 minutes/year.

> Availability is like a 24-hour pharmacy: redundancy and backups are the extra stock and the second pharmacist on call — so the store never closes, even when something breaks.

---

### The tension between pillars

Strengthening one pillar can weaken another. Strong authentication boosts confidentiality but adds friction, reducing effective availability. Redundant systems improve availability but expand the attack surface. Security professionals must deliberately balance all three based on the organization's mission and **risk** — defined as the probability that a threat exploits a vulnerability to violate a CIA pillar.

---

### Recap

- **CIA** = Confidentiality (no unauthorized access), Integrity (no unauthorized alteration), Availability (always accessible to authorized users).
- **DAD Triad** maps directly: Disclosure attacks C, Alteration attacks I, Destruction attacks A.
- Encryption and access controls protect **confidentiality**; hashing and digital signatures protect **integrity**; redundancy and backups protect **availability**.
- **Non-repudiation** (from digital signatures) proves *who* did something and prevents denial — tied to integrity.
- **RTO** = how fast you recover; **RPO** = how much data loss is acceptable — both drive backup architecture.

### Exam flags

- Scenario asks which CIA pillar is violated: map the attack to DAD (Disclosure → C, Alteration → I, Destruction → A).
- Distinguishing RTO vs. RPO definitions — exam frequently tests both terms in the same question.
- Recognizing that digital signatures provide both **integrity** *and* **non-repudiation** simultaneously.
