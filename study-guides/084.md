## Penetration Testing

Penetration testing is how organizations find and prove security weaknesses before real attackers do. This lesson covers the full pen test lifecycle — authorization, knowledge types, phases, tools, reporting, and team structures — all of which are directly exam-relevant.

---

### Written authorization comes first

**Written authorization** is the mandatory legal document that permits testing to begin. Without it, testing is illegal under computer fraud and abuse laws — intent doesn't matter.

- Must name: who authorized it, which systems are **in scope**, and the permitted time window
- Both the testing firm and individual testers face legal exposure without it

> Think of it like a search warrant: a locksmith who opens your door without one is still a burglar in the eyes of the law.

---

### Knowledge types define the threat model

The three testing types differ by how much information testers receive upfront.

| Type | Prior knowledge given | Simulates | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Black box** | None — org name or IP range only | External attacker with zero insider info | Most realistic; slowest and most expensive |
| **Gray box** | Partial — network diagrams, a low-privilege account, or tech stack info | Compromised employee account or informed contractor | Balances realism and efficiency |
| **White box** (crystal box) | Full — source code, architecture docs, admin credentials | Insider threat or attacker with extended access | Most thorough; least realistic externally |

---

### The engagement follows defined phases

**Planning and reconnaissance** — scope, rules of engagement, and legal authorization are locked in before any technical activity.

- **Passive reconnaissance** — gathering public information *without touching target systems*, so it is completely undetectable. Examples: **WHOIS** lookups (domain registration data), **DNS** queries (subdomains, mail servers), **certificate transparency logs** (reveal internal hostnames from TLS certs), LinkedIn (employee roles), **Shodan** (internet-connected devices), job postings (technology stack clues).
- **Active reconnaissance** — directly interacting with target systems; detectable by firewalls, **IDS** (intrusion detection systems), and scanners. Examples: port scanning, service enumeration, web spidering, vulnerability scanning.

**Exploitation** — actually leveraging vulnerabilities to gain unauthorized access, proving they are practically exploitable, not just theoretical.

**Post-exploitation** — demonstrating full impact after a foothold is established:
- **Privilege escalation** — moving from limited access to admin/root control
- **Lateral movement** — pivoting from the initial compromise point to other network systems
- Accessing sensitive data; establishing **persistence** (mechanisms that survive initial-entry remediation)

**Reporting** — documenting all findings for both technical teams and leadership (covered below).

---

### Rules of Engagement govern every boundary

The **Rules of Engagement (RoE)** is the pre-engagement document that formally defines what testers can and cannot do.

- **Scope** — which IP ranges/systems are in-scope vs. explicitly excluded (fragile or third-party systems often excluded to prevent outages)
- **Timing restrictions** — off-hours to protect operations, or business-hours so the **SOC** (Security Operations Center) can observe attacker behavior in real time
- **Notification contacts** — SOC, system owners, management, and emergency contacts
- **Limitations** — prohibited activities such as denial-of-service testing, physical social engineering, or touching production data
- **Evidence handling** — how discovered credentials or customer data must be stored and destroyed post-engagement

---

### Social engineering tests the human layer

**Social engineering** probes people rather than technology.

- **Phishing simulation** — crafted emails measuring click rates, attachment opens, and credential submissions on fake login pages; results expose training gaps
- **Physical social engineering** — tailgating, impersonating employees/contractors/delivery personnel to test physical access controls
- **Vishing** — **voice phishing** via telephone; tests whether employees disclose sensitive info or reset credentials for an impersonator

> Vishing is phishing with a phone call instead of an email — same manipulation, different channel.

### Core tools and what each one does

| Tool | Category | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Nmap** | Network scanning | Port scanning, service version detection, OS fingerprinting, scripted checks via **Nmap Scripting Engine** |
| **Metasploit Framework** | Exploitation | Open-source exploit platform; modules, payload generation, post-exploitation via **Meterpreter** agent |
| **Burp Suite** | Web app testing | Intercepting proxy, automated scanner, intruder (automated payloads), repeater (manual request editing) |
| **Hashcat** | Password cracking | GPU-accelerated *offline* cracking — dictionary, rules-based, brute force |
| **Hydra** | Password spraying | *Online* attacks against live services: SSH, FTP, HTTP, SMB |
| **Mimikatz** | Windows post-exploitation | Extracts **NTLM** hashes and plaintext credentials from **LSASS** (Windows process that manages authentication) memory; enables **pass-the-hash** and **pass-the-ticket** attacks |
| **BloodHound** | AD mapping | Maps **Active Directory** (Microsoft's directory service) relationships to reveal paths from compromised user to domain admin |
| **Cobalt Strike** | C2 framework | Commercial **command-and-control** platform for advanced post-exploitation, lateral movement, and persistent access simulation |

### The report turns findings into decisions

The **penetration test report** is the deliverable that makes technical work actionable.

- **Executive summary** — jargon-free; overall risk level, top findings, business impact, remediation priorities (written for leadership)
- **Technical findings** — per-vulnerability: description, **CVE** identifier (a standardized vulnerability ID), affected systems, reproduction steps, screenshots/command output, remediation guidance
- **Risk ratings** — Critical / High / Medium / Low based on likelihood and business impact
- The report itself must be protected: it is a step-by-step attack guide for the organization's own systems

---

### Red, blue, purple, and white team roles

| Team | Role |
|---|---|
| **Red team** | Attackers — extended adversarial simulation (weeks or months), mimicking sophisticated persistent threats |
| **Blue team** | Defenders — the org's own SOC; detects and responds; may or may not know a red team exercise is active |
| **Purple team** | Collaborative mode — red shares techniques with blue in real time so blue can build detection rules and response playbooks against actual attacks |
| **White team** | Administrators — sets RoE, arbitrates disputes, collects performance metrics for both sides |

> Purple team is like a sparring session where the coach calls out every move as it happens — blue learns faster because they see exactly what they're defending against.

---

### Bug bounty programs extend coverage continuously

A **bug bounty program** invites external security researchers to test defined systems and pays them for valid vulnerability reports. It complements periodic penetration tests by providing continuous, crowd-sourced testing between formal engagements.

---

### Recap

- **Written authorization** is a legal prerequisite — no authorization = illegal, period
- Black/gray/white box differ by **knowledge given**: none / partial / full, trading realism for efficiency
- **Passive recon** is undetectable; **active recon** can trigger IDS/firewall alerts
- Post-exploitation proves real impact: privilege escalation → lateral movement → persistence
- **Purple team** = red + blue collaborate; **white team** = administrators/arbitrators

### Exam flags

- Be ready to distinguish **vulnerability scanning** (identifies, no exploitation) vs. **penetration testing** (actually exploits to prove impact)
- Know all three **knowledge-level types** (black/gray/white box) and what threat scenario each simulates
- Recognize what each major tool does: especially **Mimikatz** (credential extraction from LSASS), **BloodHound** (AD attack path mapping), and **Burp Suite** (web app proxy/testing)
