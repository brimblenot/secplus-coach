## Third-Party Risk Assessment

Third-party risk management is about securing your organization not just from the outside, but from the trusted doors you've already opened. This lesson covers how attackers exploit vendor relationships, how to categorize and assess third-party risk, and what contractual and monitoring controls your organization must have.

---

### Why third-party risk matters

**Third-party risk** is the exposure an organization faces because external entities — vendors, contractors, suppliers, managed service providers — have access to its systems, data, or networks. The risk is real because attackers target the weakest link, not the strongest one.

Three landmark incidents show this in practice:

- **Target (2013):** Attackers stole credentials from an HVAC vendor — **HVAC** meaning heating, ventilation, and air conditioning — that had remote network access for billing purposes. That access was the path into Target's point-of-sale systems, exposing ~40 million credit cards. Target's own defenses were never breached directly.
- **SolarWinds (2020):** Attackers compromised SolarWinds' development environment and injected malicious code into an update for **Orion** — SolarWinds' network monitoring platform. SolarWinds digitally signed and distributed the poisoned update normally. ~18,000 customers installed it, including US federal agencies.
- **Kaseya VSA (2021):** **Kaseya VSA** — a remote management platform used by managed service providers — was exploited to push ransomware to ~1,500 organizations through a single vendor compromise.

> Think of a vendor connection like a side door on a bank: the vault is well-armored, but if the cleaning crew has a key and leaves theirs under a mat, attackers walk right in.

### Categories of third-party risk

| Risk Type | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Data access risk** | Vendor can read/handle sensitive organizational data |
| **System access risk** | Vendor has remote access to networks or systems |
| **Availability risk** | Organization depends on vendor uptime to function |
| **Concentration risk** | Over-reliance on one vendor = single point of failure |
| **Security posture risk** | Vendor's own weak security exposes the organization |
| **Supply chain risk** | Vendor's vendors can also be compromised upstream |
| **Geographic/geopolitical risk** | Vendor operates under adversarial or unstable jurisdictions |

### The vendor risk management lifecycle

**Vendor identification and inventory** — building a complete catalog of every vendor and what they can access — is the foundation. Most organizations discover they have far more vendor relationships than they realized, each approved in isolation.

**Tiering and criticality assessment** classifies vendors by risk level based on what they access and how sensitive that access is:

| Tier | Risk Level | Access Profile | Assessment Intensity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | High | Sensitive data or broad system access | Most rigorous, ongoing monitoring |
| Tier 2 | Moderate | Moderate access and sensitivity | Substantive but less intensive |
| Tier 3 | Low | Limited access, low-sensitivity data | Basic questionnaire review |

### Vendor assessment methods

**Security questionnaires** are the most common starting point — the vendor self-reports on their security controls.

- **SIG** — **Standardized Information Gathering** questionnaire, used heavily in financial services; covers access control, cryptography, incident management, cloud, and more.
- **VSAQ** — **Vendor Security Assessment Questionnaire**, used primarily in the tech sector.
- Custom questionnaires may supplement either. **Key limitation:** all questionnaires rely on vendor self-reporting, which may be inaccurate or overly optimistic.

**SOC 2 Type 2** — **System and Organization Controls 2** — is an independent audit report produced by an external auditing firm. A Type 2 report covers 6–12 months of actual control operation, not just design. When reviewing one, check:
- **Scope** — does it cover the specific systems you'll use?
- **Time period** — is it less than 12 months old?
- **Exceptions** — any auditor-noted failures?
- **Trust service categories** — Security must be included at minimum.

**ISO 27001 certification** means the vendor's **ISMS** — **Information Security Management System** — has been certified by an accredited third-party body through an initial audit and annual surveillance audits. It signals a structured, risk-managed security program.

**Right-to-audit clause** — a contractual provision giving your organization the right to directly assess the vendor's controls via questionnaire, document review, or on-site inspection. Critical for Tier 1 vendors; it goes beyond what vendors voluntarily disclose.

> A SOC 2 report is like a restaurant's health inspection certificate — independently verified, dated, and you can read what the inspector flagged.

### Contractual security provisions

| Document | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **SLA** — Service Level Agreement | Uptime guarantees and penalties |
| **NDA** — Non-Disclosure Agreement | Protects shared confidential information |
| **DPA** — Data Processing Agreement | Required under **GDPR** (EU data protection law); specifies data handling, breach notification, deletion, sub-processors |
| **MSA** — Master Service Agreement | Overarching relationship terms |
| **SOW** — Statement of Work | Deliverables and timelines per project |
| **Security addendum** | Encryption, patching, incident notification, audit rights |

---

### Ongoing monitoring and termination

A single assessment at onboarding is not enough — vendor security posture changes over time.

- **BitSight / SecurityScorecard** — continuous monitoring services that collect external signals (open vulnerabilities, botnet activity, patching cadence) and translate them into a security rating.
- Annual reassessment questionnaires and updated SOC 2 reports track changes.
- Monitor public news for vendor breaches.
- **Secure termination:** when a vendor relationship ends, revoke all access, retrieve or destroy organizational data they hold, and document the offboarding.

> Monitoring a vendor's security rating over time is like checking your contractor's license annually — you trusted them at hire, but circumstances change.

---

### Recap

- Attackers exploit vendor trust relationships to bypass strong perimeter defenses — Target, SolarWinds, and Kaseya are canonical examples.
- Vendors are tiered by sensitivity of access (Tier 1 = highest risk, most scrutiny).
- Questionnaires (SIG, VSAQ) rely on self-reporting; SOC 2 Type 2 provides independent audit evidence for a defined time period.
- Contracts must include SLA, NDA, DPA (required by GDPR), and a right-to-audit clause for high-risk vendors.
- Monitoring is continuous, not one-time — and vendor termination must include access revocation and data destruction.

### Exam flags

- **SOC 2 Type 2 review criteria** — scope, recency (<12 months), exceptions, and required trust service categories are all high-probability details.
- **Supply chain attack scenarios** — expect a scenario question where the breach path runs through a vendor (HVAC, software update, MSP platform) and you must identify the risk type or control gap.
- **Contractual provisions** — know which document (SLA vs. DPA vs. right-to-audit) addresses which obligation, especially DPA under GDPR.
