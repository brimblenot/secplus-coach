## Penetration Tests (Domain 5)

Penetration testing isn't just a technical exercise — it's a governed, authorized, legally bounded process that fits inside compliance frameworks and organizational risk programs. This lesson covers how pen tests are scoped, authorized, reported, and compared to other assessment types.

---

### Assessment types: how pen tests compare

Four distinct assessment types exist, each with a different depth, cost, and purpose.

**Vulnerability assessment** — an automated and manual process that *identifies and ranks* vulnerabilities but never exploits them.
- Less disruptive, cheaper, done frequently (monthly baseline checks, compliance demos)
- Tells you what *might* be exploitable, not what *is*

**Penetration test** — actively exploits identified vulnerabilities to prove real-world impact.
- More skilled testers, higher cost, more disruptive → appropriate for comprehensive *annual* assessment
- Moves beyond theoretical risk to *demonstrated* impact

**Red team exercise** — an extended adversary simulation (weeks or months) testing people, processes, and technology together — detection/response, physical security, and social engineering included.
- Appropriate only for *mature* programs that have already addressed obvious technical gaps

**Bug bounty program** — ongoing crowdsourced vulnerability discovery by independent researchers paid per valid finding.
- Provides continuous outside-in perspective *between* formal assessments

| Type | Exploits? | Frequency | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vulnerability assessment | No | High (monthly) | Low |
| Penetration test | Yes | Annual | High |
| Red team exercise | Yes + social/physical | One-off, long | Very high |
| Bug bounty | Researcher's choice | Continuous | Variable |

---

### Compliance frameworks that require pen testing

- **PCI DSS** — requires annual pen testing of external *and* internal surfaces, plus after significant infrastructure changes; must test both network and application layers, from both inside and outside; must include **segmentation testing** — verifying that network segments isolating the cardholder data environment actually prevent an attacker on one segment from reaching card data on another
- **NIST SP 800-53 CA-8** — **CA-8** is the specific control identifier requiring pen testing for systems at moderate or high impact levels
- **ISO 27001** — requires technical compliance verification including pen testing as part of a certified **ISMS** (Information Security Management System — the overall framework of policies and controls an organization uses to manage information security)
- **SOC 2** — pen test evidence from independent third parties strengthens the report's credibility

---

### Authorization: the most legally critical step

**Authorization** — written permission, from someone with actual authority over the in-scope systems, obtained *before* any testing begins.

- Testing without authorization violates the **Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA)** — the U.S. federal law criminalizing unauthorized access to computer systems — and equivalent laws globally
- For internal systems: authority rests with the CISO, CIO, or senior executive responsible for those systems
- For **cloud-hosted systems**: the cloud provider has its own pen testing policies; advance notification and approval are required

Think of authorization like a search warrant — even a highly skilled investigator commits a crime if they search without one.

---

### Rules of engagement: scoping the test

The **rules of engagement (ROE)** — the signed document defining exactly what is and is not permitted during the test — must specify:

- **In-scope systems**: explicitly identified by IP ranges, domain names, or application URLs
- **Out-of-scope systems**: explicitly excluded — testers must never assume a system is implicitly in scope. Commonly excluded: live production databases with customer data, third-party systems without that third party's consent, life-safety systems, systems excluded for operational continuity
- **Testing hours**: restricted to off-peak, or business hours permitted?
- **Permitted techniques**: is social engineering allowed? Physical access testing? Denial-of-service testing?
- **Emergency contacts**: escalation chain to stop the test immediately if critical production is impacted or an active compromise is discovered
- **Evidence handling**: how captured credentials, PII, and proof-of-concept code must be stored, protected, and destroyed

---

### SOC coordination: to notify or not

If the **SOC** (Security Operations Center — the team that monitors for and responds to attacks) is *not* told a test is underway, they may treat it as a real attack and trigger incident response, potentially interfering with the test or causing harm.

- **Notify SOC** → tests detection capability but not real-world response to surprise
- **Don't notify SOC** → tests both detection and response, but requires tight coordination to prevent defensive actions from causing unintended damage

---

### Pen test reports: two audiences

- **Executive summary**: business-risk language only — overall rating, top findings, business impact; *no technical jargon*
- **Technical findings section**: per-vulnerability writeups including affected systems, reproduction steps, screenshots/command output, **CVE** identifiers (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures — the standardized naming system for publicly known vulnerabilities), **CVSS** scores (Common Vulnerability Scoring System — a 0–10 numeric rating of severity), proof-of-concept code, risk rating, and specific remediation guidance
- Reports should include **retesting provisions** — a plan for the firm to verify remediations and issue a verification addendum

**PCI DSS report requirements specifically**: tester must be independent; report must document scope, methodology, tester qualifications, all findings, and remediation status. **QSA** (Qualified Security Assessor — a PCI-certified firm authorized to validate compliance) -approved firms provide highest credibility.

---

### Credentials and responsible disclosure

- **CREST** — a UK-originated international certification framework for penetration testers across multiple specializations
- **OSCP** (Offensive Security Certified Professional) — a hands-on certification requiring candidates to actually compromise target systems within a time limit, proving real skill rather than memorization

**Responsible disclosure** — when a test uncovers a vulnerability in a *vendor's product*, the tester notifies the vendor privately and gives ~90 days to patch before any public disclosure, balancing user protection with the vendor's need for time to fix the issue properly.

---

### Recap

- Pen tests *actively exploit* vulnerabilities; vulnerability assessments do not — both serve different cadences and costs
- Authorization must be signed before testing begins; unauthorized testing is a federal crime (CFAA)
- Rules of engagement must explicitly name in-scope *and* out-of-scope systems — no implicit assumptions
- PCI DSS adds segmentation testing as a unique requirement; NIST SP 800-53 CA-8 mandates pen testing at moderate/high impact levels
- Reports serve two audiences: executive summary (business risk) and technical findings (reproduction steps, CVEs, remediation)

### Exam flags

- **PCI DSS segmentation testing** — know that it verifies isolation of the cardholder data environment and is a PCI-specific pen test requirement
- **Rules of engagement elements** — authorization authority, in/out-of-scope definitions, permitted techniques, emergency contacts, and evidence handling are all high-frequency scenario questions
- **Responsible disclosure timeline** — ~90 days to the vendor before public disclosure; know the rationale
