## Access Controls

Access control governs what authenticated users can do with resources. Authentication answers *who you are*; authorization answers *what you're allowed to do*. This lesson covers the major access control models and the organizational controls that support them — all high-frequency Security+ material.

---

### Least privilege: the universal baseline

**Least privilege** is the principle that every user, service, and process gets only the minimum permissions needed to do their job — nothing extra.

- Limits blast radius: a compromised low-privilege account can't do admin-level damage
- Permissions *creep upward* over time as roles change, so **periodic access reviews** are required to claw back unneeded access

> Think of least privilege like a keycard that only opens the doors on your floor — not the whole building.

---

### Mandatory Access Control (MAC)

**MAC** — Mandatory Access Control — is a model where the *system* enforces access based on security labels; individual users have no say in granting or revoking access.

- Admins assign labels to **subjects** (users/processes) and **objects** (resources)
- Users cannot share access, change their own label, or override decisions
- Used in high-security government/military environments
- **SELinux** — Security-Enhanced Linux, a kernel-level framework — implements MAC on Linux by confining each process to only what its policy explicitly allows

Two classic MAC models:

| Model | Goal | Rule 1 | Rule 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bell-LaPadula** | Confidentiality | No-Read-Up (can't read above your clearance) | No-Write-Down (can't write to lower classification — prevents leaking secrets down) |
| **Biba** | Integrity | No-Write-Up (can't write to higher-integrity objects) | No-Read-Down (can't read lower-integrity data — prevents contaminating your work with unreliable info) |

> MAC is like a military vault where the lock combination is set by command — the person *storing* files there can't hand out copies.

---

### Discretionary Access Control (DAC)

**DAC** — Discretionary Access Control — lets the *resource owner* decide who gets access. It's the most common model in everyday computing.

- Windows **NTFS** permissions and Linux **rwx bits** (read/write/execute flags on a file) both implement DAC
- Weakness: relies entirely on users making good security decisions — over-sharing is a policy violation no system will catch

> DAC is like owning a Google Doc: you decide who to share it with, and nothing stops you from clicking "Anyone with the link."

---

### Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

**RBAC** — Role-Based Access Control — assigns permissions to *roles*, then assigns users to roles, rather than granting permissions to individuals directly.

- Changing a user's job function = reassigning their role; all permissions update automatically
- Far easier to manage at enterprise scale (fewer roles than users)
- Risk: **role explosion** — too many fine-grained roles can make RBAC as complex as per-user permissions; keep the role set minimal
- Used in enterprise apps, databases, and cloud platforms: **AWS IAM** (Identity and Access Management), **Azure RBAC** (Owner/Contributor/Reader built-in roles), **GCP IAM**

> RBAC is like a hotel key card programmed by job title: all front-desk staff get the same doors without individually programming each card.

---

### Attribute-Based Access Control (ABAC)

**ABAC** — Attribute-Based Access Control — makes access decisions by evaluating a combination of attributes from the subject, resource, and environment.

- **Subject attributes**: department, job title, clearance level
- **Resource attributes**: classification, sensitivity, data type
- **Environment attributes**: time of day, network location, device type
- Example policy: "A physician may access a patient record only if the patient is assigned to their practice *and* they are on the hospital's internal network *during business hours*"
- Implemented with **XACML** — eXtensible Access Control Markup Language, a policy language for expressing complex Boolean (true/false logic combining multiple conditions) attribute rules
- Cloud IAM increasingly uses ABAC via tag-based conditions

> ABAC is like a nightclub with a velvet rope that checks your age *and* your outfit *and* whether it's past 10 PM — all three must pass.

---

### Rule-based access control

**Rule-based access control** applies explicit rules to resources regardless of who the user is — identity is irrelevant, only the rule matters.

- **Firewall ACLs** (Access Control Lists — rules that permit or deny network traffic) are the classic example: "Allow TCP port 443" applies to all matching traffic
- Time-based restrictions (e.g., training-room network only accessible 8 AM–6 PM) are rule-based
- Physical card-access systems that allow specific cards through specific doors at specific times are rule-based

> A rule-based firewall is like a bouncer with a single instruction: "No one in after midnight" — they don't care who you are.

---

### Policy-Based Access Control (PBAC)

**PBAC** — Policy-Based Access Control — centralizes access policy in one engine, often blending RBAC and ABAC, so decisions are consistent and auditable across many applications.

- XACML implementations and cloud identity governance platforms are PBAC examples

---

### Organizational access controls

These controls address the *human* side of access management.

**Need-to-know** — even a user with sufficient clearance for a classification level only gets access to the specific information their job requires. A Top Secret clearance doesn't open every Top Secret file.

**Separation of duties** — high-risk tasks are split across multiple people so no single person can complete the whole process alone. Prevents fraud (requires collusion) and catches errors.
- Example: one person enters a payment, a *different* person approves it; the developer who writes code can't deploy it without separate review.

**Dual control** (the two-person rule) — two authorized individuals must *simultaneously* act for a sensitive operation to proceed.
- Classic example: nuclear launch requires two officers turning keys at separate stations at the same time.
- **Root CA** (Certificate Authority — the top-level entity that signs other certificates) key ceremonies use dual control with witnesses.

**Job rotation** — employees cycle through different roles periodically.
- Cross-trains staff, disrupts long-running fraud (rotating out breaks schemes that need continuous presence), and surfaces security gaps when a new person takes over.

**Mandatory vacation** — employees must be away for a set period each year, forcing colleagues to cover their work.
- Specifically designed to expose fraud that requires the perpetrator to be present to hide it; many financial frauds unravel the moment the fraudster can't be there.

---

### Recap

- **MAC** uses admin-assigned labels; users can't override decisions — Bell-LaPadula protects confidentiality, Biba protects integrity
- **DAC** gives owners discretion — convenient but relies on users making good choices (NTFS/Linux permissions)
- **RBAC** assigns permissions to roles, not individuals — scalable but watch for role explosion
- **ABAC** combines subject + resource + environment attributes for the most granular control; uses XACML
- Organizational controls (separation of duties, dual control, mandatory vacation) address insider threat and fraud — not just technical access

---

### Exam flags

- **Bell-LaPadula vs. Biba**: know which protects confidentiality vs. integrity and which direction each "No-Read/No-Write" rule applies
- **Separation of duties vs. dual control**: both split responsibility, but dual control requires *simultaneous* action; separation of duties splits *sequential* steps between different people
- **ABAC vs. RBAC**: ABAC can enforce time-of-day, location, and device conditions that RBAC cannot — questions often present a scenario requiring that extra granularity
