Beyond the conventional IT infrastructure of servers, routers, and switches lies a broad landscape of specialized computing environments — embedded systems, industrial control systems, Internet of Things devices, and operational technology — each carrying distinct security challenges that traditional IT security practices do not fully address. The Security+ exam expects candidates to understand the unique characteristics of these environments, the specific threats they face, and the appropriate compensating controls that reduce risk when standard patching and endpoint protection are not feasible.

The Internet of Things encompasses billions of network-connected devices designed to collect data, automate physical processes, or extend digital control into the physical world. IoT devices include smart thermostats, security cameras, video doorbells, industrial sensors, medical monitoring equipment, smart lighting systems, smart meters, wearable health monitors, connected automobiles, and an enormous variety of consumer and industrial appliances. What unifies these devices from a security perspective is a set of common characteristics that make them particularly difficult to secure. Most IoT devices have severely constrained computational resources — limited CPU, memory, and power — which means they cannot run the cryptographic operations, endpoint detection software, or full operating systems that are standard on conventional computers. Firmware on IoT devices is frequently never updated, either because the manufacturer does not issue updates, users do not apply available updates, or the update mechanism itself is inadequate or absent. Many IoT devices ship with default administrative credentials — often well-known username and password combinations like admin/admin or admin/password — that are either hardcoded into the firmware and cannot be changed or are simply never changed by users. The deployment lifecycle of IoT devices is often measured in decades rather than years: an industrial sensor installed in a manufacturing plant in 2010 may still be operational in 2030 without ever having received a security update. IoT devices use a diverse array of communication protocols — Zigbee, Z-Wave, Bluetooth Low Energy, LoRaWAN, MQTT, and proprietary protocols — many of which were designed for efficiency and low power consumption rather than security. A single compromised IoT device can serve as a foothold for an attacker to move laterally into the broader network. The Mirai botnet, which emerged in 2016, demonstrated this risk at scale: it compromised hundreds of thousands of IoT devices with default credentials and used them to launch the largest distributed denial of service attacks recorded at that time, targeting DNS infrastructure and disrupting internet access across major regions.

The appropriate security strategy for IoT devices begins with network segmentation. IoT devices should be placed on dedicated, isolated network segments — separate VLANs with strict firewall rules permitting only the specific communications those devices require to fulfill their function. An industrial temperature sensor that reports to an on-site controller does not need internet access or access to the corporate finance systems. Granting IoT devices network access beyond their minimum operational requirements vastly expands the damage a compromised device can cause. Organizations should inventory all IoT devices, disable unnecessary features and services, change default credentials wherever possible, keep firmware updated when updates are available, and monitor IoT network segments for unusual traffic patterns that may indicate compromise.

SCADA — Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition — systems form the backbone of industrial control infrastructure for critical sectors including electric power generation and distribution, water and wastewater treatment, oil and gas pipelines, manufacturing, and transportation. SCADA systems collect real-time data from sensors and field devices across large geographic areas and provide centralized monitoring and control capabilities to operators in control rooms. The consequences of a successful cyberattack against SCADA infrastructure extend far beyond data loss: manipulation of a power grid can cause widespread blackouts, disruption of water treatment systems can endanger public health, and sabotage of industrial manufacturing processes can cause physical equipment damage or injuries to workers.

SCADA systems were originally designed and deployed before cybersecurity was a significant consideration. They prioritized reliability, availability, and real-time determinism over security. Many SCADA systems run on specialized proprietary hardware using protocols — including Modbus, DNP3, and EtherNet/IP — that were designed for device communication on isolated networks without any authentication or encryption. The integration of these systems with corporate IT networks and, increasingly, with internet-connected services for remote monitoring and management has dramatically increased their exposure to cyber threats. The Stuxnet worm, discovered in 2010, was a landmark event that demonstrated the potential for sophisticated cyberweapons to cause physical destruction through SCADA systems. Stuxnet targeted Iranian nuclear enrichment centrifuges by exploiting vulnerabilities in Siemens programmable logic controllers and subtly manipulating centrifuge rotation speeds while reporting normal readings to operators. The damage was physical and real, demonstrating that cyberattacks against industrial control systems can achieve effects traditionally associated only with kinetic weapons.

Industrial Control Systems, or ICS, is the broader term encompassing SCADA as well as other control system types. Distributed Control Systems, or DCS, are used for continuous industrial processes like chemical manufacturing and refining, where control is distributed across multiple controllers closer to the field devices. Programmable Logic Controllers, or PLCs, are specialized industrial computers that directly control individual pieces of equipment — motors, valves, pumps — based on programmed logic. PLCs are deeply embedded in manufacturing, energy, and building automation systems.

Operational Technology, or OT, is a broad term for hardware and software that monitors and controls physical equipment, processes, and infrastructure. The distinction between OT and IT is conceptually important for security. IT environments prioritize confidentiality, integrity, and availability, in roughly that order — an unauthorized disclosure of data is often the primary concern. OT environments prioritize availability and safety above all else — a centrifuge or power turbine must not go offline unexpectedly, and a safety malfunction can injure or kill people. This difference in priorities fundamentally shapes the security practices appropriate for each environment. IT's standard patch-on-a-monthly-cycle approach is frequently impossible in OT environments where systems may run continuously without planned downtime for years. Deploying antivirus software on an OT workstation controlling a turbine may be unacceptable if it interferes with real-time control cycles. These constraints require security professionals to adapt their approach.

Recommended security practices for ICS and SCADA environments include network segmentation as the highest priority. Where physically and operationally feasible, OT networks should be air-gapped — completely isolated with no network connection to IT networks or the internet. Where air gaps are not achievable because of legitimate operational needs for data exchange, strict firewall policies permitting only whitelisted protocols, source addresses, and destination addresses on the minimal necessary ports provide a practical alternative. An industrial DMZ, placed between the IT network and the OT network and hosting the specific historian servers and data aggregation systems that need to exchange information, limits direct connectivity between the two environments. Unidirectional security gateways, also called data diodes, are specialized hardware devices that physically enforce one-way data flow — data can pass from the OT network to the IT network for monitoring and reporting, but no traffic can flow back from the IT network into the OT environment, making network-based attacks from IT to OT impossible by physical design. Patch management in OT requires vendor-approved patches tested in a simulated environment before deployment, acceptance of longer patch cycles than IT, and thorough assessment of whether patches might introduce instability into real-time control processes. Network monitoring specifically designed to understand OT protocols — recognizing normal command patterns in Modbus or DNP3 traffic and alerting on anomalous commands — is more appropriate than standard IT network monitoring that would not understand the context of industrial communications.

Embedded systems are computing devices where hardware and software are tightly integrated into a purpose-built unit designed to perform a specific, dedicated function. Traffic signal controllers, smart meters, medical implants and bedside monitors, avionics control computers, point-of-sale terminals, industrial robotics controllers, and home appliances with digital controls are all embedded systems. Embedded systems are designed to do one thing efficiently and reliably, not to host general-purpose applications. They typically run proprietary operating systems or stripped-down versions of standard operating systems, with no user-accessible interface beyond the specific controls needed for their function. Firmware is often stored in read-only memory and may be impossible to update without physical access to the device or specialized tools from the manufacturer. The security implications are significant: vulnerabilities discovered in embedded system firmware may go unpatched for the lifetime of the device.

Real-Time Operating Systems, or RTOS, are a specialized category of operating systems designed for applications where timing determinism is critical. A standard non-real-time operating system like Windows or Linux schedules processes flexibly and may delay a low-priority process to serve a higher-priority one — this is acceptable for general computing but catastrophic in systems where a control loop must execute within microseconds. RTOS platforms guarantee that critical tasks will complete within defined time constraints, regardless of other system activity. Automotive braking systems, medical infusion pumps, avionics flight control systems, and industrial robot controllers rely on RTOS to ensure that safety-critical control actions execute precisely when required. The security challenge is that the determinism requirements of an RTOS may make it impossible to add security monitoring or patching mechanisms that could interfere with timing guarantees. These systems tend to be highly self-contained, with limited external interfaces, which provides some security through reduced attack surface, but this also means that security updates are difficult or impossible to deploy remotely.

Connected vehicles represent an emerging frontier of embedded system security concerns. Modern automobiles contain dozens of electronic control units communicating over the Controller Area Network bus, which was designed without authentication — any device on the CAN bus can send commands to any other. In 2015, security researchers remotely exploited a vulnerability in the Uconnect telematics system of a Jeep Cherokee to take control of steering, braking, and acceleration over the internet, demonstrating the real-world consequences of insecure vehicle systems. OBD-II diagnostic ports in vehicles provide a physical access point that can be exploited by malicious devices. Autonomous vehicles, air traffic control systems, railway signaling systems, and other safety-critical transportation infrastructure all present similar challenges where cybersecurity vulnerabilities can translate directly into physical safety risks.

Drones, or Unmanned Aerial Vehicles, introduce additional security considerations. Drone control signals can potentially be intercepted or spoofed, allowing an attacker to take control of a drone or force it to land. GPS spoofing — transmitting false GPS signals to override a drone's navigation system — can cause drones to fly to unintended locations. Drones equipped with cameras can be used for reconnaissance and surveillance of physical facilities, enabling adversaries to gather intelligence about security postures. Weaponized drones have been used in conflict and criminal activities. Counter-drone measures include radio frequency detection systems, jamming devices used within legal constraints, and physical barriers. Organizations operating in sensitive environments must consider drone threats as part of their physical security planning.

The decommissioning and disposal of physical infrastructure requires careful attention to data destruction. Storage media that reaches end of life must be sanitized in a manner appropriate to the sensitivity of the data it contains. For magnetic hard drives, degaussing uses a powerful magnetic field to destroy the magnetic domains that store data, rendering the media unrecoverable. Physical destruction methods — shredding, disintegrating, or melting the media — ensure that data on both magnetic drives and solid-state drives cannot be recovered. Cryptographic erasure is particularly effective for encrypted storage: by destroying the encryption key — deleting it from all key stores — the data stored on the drive becomes permanently unrecoverable even if the drive itself is physically intact, because the data is only ciphertext without the key. NIST Special Publication 800-88, Guidelines for Media Sanitization, provides a comprehensive framework for selecting the appropriate sanitization method based on media type and data sensitivity. Organizations should obtain a certificate of destruction from vendors who perform physical destruction or degaussing, providing documented evidence that sanitization occurred.

Legacy and end-of-life infrastructure presents persistent security risk. Hardware and software past their vendor-defined support lifecycle no longer receive security patches, leaving known vulnerabilities permanently unaddressed. Compensating controls are necessary when replacement is not immediately feasible: network isolation limits exposure by preventing the legacy system from communicating with systems or networks beyond what is strictly necessary for its function. Enhanced monitoring can detect exploitation attempts or anomalous behavior. Application-layer controls — such as a WAF in front of a legacy web server — can provide a protective layer even when the underlying system cannot be patched. A documented and funded replacement roadmap ensures that legacy systems are tracked and eventually migrated to supported alternatives.

On-premises versus cloud infrastructure trade-offs extend beyond cost to security posture. On-premises infrastructure gives organizations complete control over hardware, software, configuration, and physical security, but requires substantial internal expertise and operational overhead. Cloud infrastructure offloads operational burden and provides access to sophisticated managed security services, but introduces shared responsibility complexity and data sovereignty considerations for organizations subject to regulations requiring data to remain within specific geographic jurisdictions. Latency requirements for real-time control applications — industrial systems, financial trading platforms, healthcare monitoring — may mandate on-premises or edge deployment rather than reliance on cloud infrastructure with variable network round-trip times.

Edge computing processes data at or near its source rather than sending it to a centralized data center or cloud. By performing analysis locally on devices or at nearby edge servers, edge computing reduces bandwidth consumption, minimizes latency for time-sensitive decisions, and enables operation in environments where connectivity to central infrastructure is intermittent. However, edge computing nodes are often deployed in physically uncontrolled environments — manufacturing floors, remote substations, retail locations — making physical security more challenging and increasing the difficulty of maintaining consistent security patch levels across a large fleet of distributed edge devices.
