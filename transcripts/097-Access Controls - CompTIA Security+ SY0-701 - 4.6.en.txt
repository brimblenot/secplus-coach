Access control is the process of determining what authenticated users are permitted to do with which resources. Where authentication answers the question of who is attempting to access a system, authorization answers the question of what that authenticated user is allowed to do. These are distinct functions even though they work together: a user may successfully authenticate and still be denied access to a specific resource if their authorization level is insufficient. Access control models provide the conceptual frameworks for implementing authorization decisions, and understanding the characteristics, strengths, and appropriate use cases for each model is fundamental to security practice and the Security+ examination.

The principle of least privilege applies across all access control models and should be considered a universal security requirement rather than a feature of any specific model. Least privilege means that each user, service, and process receives only the minimum permissions necessary to perform their legitimate function, and no additional access beyond that minimum. This principle limits the damage that can result from either an attacker compromising an account or a user making an error. If a standard user account is compromised, the attacker operates with the limited permissions of that account rather than with administrative access. Least privilege requires ongoing effort: permissions tend to accumulate over time as users take on new responsibilities and are granted new access without old access being revoked, so periodic access reviews are essential to maintain appropriate privilege levels.

Mandatory Access Control, or MAC, is the most restrictive access control model and is used primarily in highly secure government and military environments. In MAC, security labels are assigned by a system administrator to both subjects, which are the users or processes requesting access, and objects, which are the resources being accessed. Access decisions are made automatically based on whether the subject's label permits access to the object's label. Users cannot grant others access to resources they own, cannot change their own labels, and cannot override the system's access decisions. SELinux, the Security-Enhanced Linux framework, implements MAC on Linux systems by enforcing mandatory access control policies at the kernel level, confining processes to only the operations their policy explicitly permits. Two classic MAC models define how labels interact with confidentiality and integrity goals. The Bell-LaPadula model is designed to enforce confidentiality and is based on two rules: No-Read-Up, which prevents subjects from reading objects with a higher classification level than their own clearance, and No-Write-Down, which prevents subjects from writing data to objects with a lower classification level, preventing the downgrading of sensitive information to lower classification levels where it becomes accessible to users without appropriate clearance. The Biba model is designed to enforce integrity and inverts these rules: No-Write-Up prevents subjects from writing to higher-integrity objects, and No-Read-Down prevents subjects from reading lower-integrity objects that could contaminate their own data with unreliable information.

Discretionary Access Control, or DAC, is the most common access control model in commercial computing environments and is based on owner discretion. The owner of a resource has complete control over who can access it and what they can do with it. NTFS file permissions on Windows implement DAC: when a user creates a file, they are the owner and can grant or deny access to other users at will. Linux file permissions use a DAC model where the file owner controls the read, write, and execute permissions granted to the owner, the group, and all other users. The fundamental weakness of DAC is that it relies entirely on individual users to make appropriate security decisions for every resource they create or own. Users who share files too broadly, either through misunderstanding or convenience, can inadvertently expose sensitive data without violating any system-enforced policy.

Role-Based Access Control, or RBAC, shifts the basis for access decisions from individual identity to the user's role within the organization. Rather than assigning permissions directly to individual users, permissions are assigned to roles, and users are assigned to the roles appropriate to their job function. A user who is a member of the "accounts payable clerk" role receives all the permissions associated with that role automatically. When a user changes job functions, reassigning their role is sufficient to update their access. RBAC is significantly easier to manage than per-user DAC at enterprise scale because there are far fewer roles than individual users. The risk of role explosion, where too many fine-grained roles are created to handle every edge case, can make RBAC nearly as complex to manage as per-user permissions and should be guarded against by maintaining a well-designed, minimal set of roles. RBAC is widely used in enterprise applications, database management systems, and cloud IAM platforms. AWS IAM policies and roles, Azure RBAC with its built-in roles of Owner, Contributor, and Reader plus custom role definitions, and GCP IAM all implement role-based authorization.

Attribute-Based Access Control, or ABAC, is the most flexible and granular access control model, making access decisions based on a collection of attributes rather than a single dimension. ABAC considers attributes of the subject, such as the user's department, job title, clearance level, and organizational unit; attributes of the resource, such as its classification, owner, data type, and sensitivity level; and attributes of the environment, such as the current time of day, the user's network location, and the type of device being used. By combining multiple attributes in policy rules, ABAC can express very precise access conditions that cannot be represented in simpler models. An example ABAC policy might state that a user may access patient records only if the user's role attribute is physician, the patient's record has an attribute indicating the patient is assigned to that physician's practice, and the access is occurring from a network location attribute matching the hospital's internal network during normal business hours. ABAC is implemented using policy languages such as XACML, the eXtensible Access Control Markup Language, which allows policy writers to express complex Boolean conditions combining attributes. Cloud IAM systems increasingly incorporate ABAC concepts through tag-based conditions and context-aware policies.

Rule-based access control defines access decisions through explicit rules associated with resources, applying regardless of user identity. Firewall access control lists are the most common example of rule-based access control: a rule that allows TCP port 443 traffic to a web server applies to all traffic matching the rule regardless of who the source user is. Time-based access restrictions are a form of rule-based control: a training room network that is only accessible between 8 AM and 6 PM has a time rule governing access regardless of the individual user. Card access systems that allow specific physical access cards through specific doors at specific times implement rule-based physical access control.

Policy-based access control, or PBAC, centralizes access control policy management, often combining elements of RBAC and ABAC in a unified policy engine. XACML implementations are a form of PBAC, as are cloud identity governance platforms that define policy centrally and evaluate it dynamically across many applications. PBAC provides better consistency and auditability than distributed access decisions made independently by each application.

Beyond the access control models themselves, several important organizational controls address the human dimension of access management. Need-to-know is a complementary principle to least privilege, stating that even a user with sufficient clearance for a classification level should only receive access to specific information that is necessary for their particular job assignment. Having a Top Secret clearance does not automatically grant access to all Top Secret information; access is further restricted to what the user actually needs for their work. Separation of duties distributes the tasks involved in critical, high-risk processes among multiple people so that no single person can complete the entire process alone. This prevents both fraud, by requiring collusion between multiple people, and errors, by providing an independent check. Classic examples include requiring that one person enters a payment and a different person approves it, and that the developer who writes code cannot deploy it to production without a separate review and approval step. Dual control, also called the two-person rule, requires two authorized individuals to simultaneously and independently take action for a high-risk operation to proceed. The two-key system for nuclear weapons launch, requiring two officers to simultaneously turn keys at separate locations, is the classic example. Root CA key ceremonies for certificate authority operations use dual control with multiple witnesses to protect the most sensitive cryptographic keys. Job rotation regularly moves employees through different roles and system access levels, which serves multiple security purposes: it cross-trains staff, makes it harder for any individual to sustain a long-term fraud scheme because rotating out of a role disrupts any scheme that depended on continuous access, and often reveals inappropriate configurations or security gaps when a new person assumes responsibility for a system. Mandatory vacation requires employees to be away from their systems for a period each year, during which their colleagues must handle their responsibilities. This is specifically intended to detect fraud schemes that require the fraudster to be continuously present to cover their tracks; many financial frauds have been discovered when the perpetrator was forced to take vacation and the cover-up fell apart.
