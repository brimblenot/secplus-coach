Security standards provide specific, measurable requirements that translate the high-level intent of security policies into actionable technical and operational controls. While a policy might state that all data must be protected, a standard specifies exactly how that protection must be implemented, which algorithms are required, and what configurations must be maintained. Standards are more prescriptive and technology-specific than policies, and they form the backbone of a consistent, auditable security program. Organizations derive their internal standards from either their own analysis of risk and requirements or from established industry frameworks and external standards that have been developed and refined by expert bodies over many years.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework, commonly abbreviated as the NIST CSF, is a voluntary framework developed by the National Institute of Standards and Technology to help organizations in critical infrastructure and beyond manage and reduce cybersecurity risk. The framework is organized around five core functions that represent the full lifecycle of cybersecurity risk management. The Identify function covers asset management, business environment understanding, governance, risk assessment, and risk management strategy, establishing the foundation of what the organization must protect and why. The Protect function addresses access control, awareness and training, data security, information protection processes and procedures, maintenance, and protective technology, representing the controls the organization implements to limit the impact of cybersecurity events. The Detect function encompasses anomalies and events monitoring, security continuous monitoring, and detection processes that enable the organization to identify cybersecurity events when they occur. The Respond function covers response planning, communications, analysis, mitigation, and improvements that the organization takes when a cybersecurity event is detected. The Recover function addresses recovery planning, improvements, and communications that restore capabilities and services impaired by a cybersecurity incident. The CSF also defines four implementation tiers ranging from Tier 1, called Partial, where risk management practices are not formalized, through Tier 4, called Adaptive, where the organization continuously improves its cybersecurity practices based on lessons learned and predictive threat intelligence. Organizations use framework profiles to align their cybersecurity activities with their business requirements, risk tolerances, and available resources.

NIST Special Publication 800-53, titled Security and Privacy Controls for Information Systems and Organizations, is a comprehensive control catalog primarily developed for United States federal agencies but widely adopted in the private sector. The publication organizes security controls into twenty control families identified by two-letter prefixes: AC for Access Control, AU for Audit and Accountability, CA for Assessment Authorization and Monitoring, CM for Configuration Management, IA for Identification and Authentication, IR for Incident Response, RA for Risk Assessment, SA for System and Services Acquisition, SC for System and Communications Protection, SI for System and Information Integrity, and many others. The publication defines three impact baselines: Low, Moderate, and High, corresponding to the potential impact of a security breach on organizational operations. Each baseline specifies which controls are required, with higher-impact systems requiring more extensive control implementation.

NIST Special Publication 800-171, titled Protecting Controlled Unclassified Information in Nonfederal Systems and Organizations, addresses the security requirements that contractors and other nonfederal entities must meet when handling Controlled Unclassified Information on behalf of the federal government. The publication defines 110 security requirements organized across 14 requirement families and serves as the technical foundation for the Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification program.

The Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification, abbreviated CMMC, is the Department of Defense's framework for ensuring that defense industrial base contractors adequately protect sensitive government information. The CMMC program defines three levels of cybersecurity maturity. Level 1, called Foundational, requires implementation of 17 basic cyber hygiene practices that address the most fundamental security requirements. Level 2, called Advanced, requires implementation of all 110 security requirements from NIST SP 800-171, representing a comprehensive security program appropriate for contractors handling Controlled Unclassified Information. Level 3, called Expert, adds 24 additional security practices beyond NIST 800-171 for contractors working on the most sensitive defense programs. Contractors seeking Level 2 or Level 3 certification must undergo third-party assessments conducted by CMMC Third Party Assessment Organizations, rather than relying on self-attestation.

ISO 27001 is the international standard for Information Security Management Systems published by the International Organization for Standardization. The standard defines requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an information security management system within an organization. ISO 27001 takes a risk-based approach, requiring organizations to systematically identify their information security risks and implement controls proportionate to those risks. The standard is recognized globally, and ISO 27001 certification demonstrates to customers, partners, and regulators that the organization has implemented a structured, independently audited security management program. ISO 27001 is paired with ISO 27002, which is a best practice guide providing detailed implementation guidance for the controls referenced in ISO 27001. The combined framework covers fourteen control domains including access control, cryptography, physical and environmental security, operations security, communications security, system acquisition development and maintenance, supplier relationships, information security incident management, and compliance.

SOC 2, which stands for Service Organization Control 2, is an auditing standard developed by the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants that applies to service providers storing, processing, or transmitting customer data. The standard is built around five Trust Service Criteria: Security, which addresses protection against unauthorized access; Availability, which addresses whether systems are available for operation and use; Processing Integrity, which addresses whether system processing is complete, valid, accurate, timely, and authorized; Confidentiality, which addresses whether information designated as confidential is protected; and Privacy, which addresses the collection, use, retention, disclosure, and disposal of personal information. A Type 1 SOC 2 report assesses the design of controls at a single point in time, demonstrating that appropriate controls exist. A Type 2 SOC 2 report covers a period typically ranging from six to twelve months and assesses both the design and the operational effectiveness of controls over that period, making it significantly more valuable as evidence of sustained security performance. Enterprise customers routinely require SOC 2 Type 2 reports from SaaS vendors before sharing sensitive data with them.

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard, known as PCI DSS, is a security standard mandated for any organization that stores, processes, or transmits payment cardholder data. PCI DSS is enforced contractually by the payment card brands rather than by law, but the consequences of noncompliance or a breach are severe and include significant fines and loss of the ability to process card payments. The standard is organized into six categories containing twelve core requirements. Smaller merchants may satisfy compliance requirements through a self-assessment questionnaire while larger merchants must undergo assessments conducted by qualified security assessors. PCI DSS requires quarterly vulnerability scans performed by approved scanning vendors and annual penetration testing. The standard also requires segmentation testing to verify that the cardholder data environment is properly isolated from other network segments.

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, known as HIPAA, is a United States law that establishes national standards for protecting protected health information. The Privacy Rule governs the permissible uses and disclosures of protected health information. The Security Rule requires covered entities and business associates to implement administrative, physical, and technical safeguards to protect electronic protected health information. The Breach Notification Rule requires covered entities to notify the Department of Health and Human Services and affected individuals within sixty days of discovering a breach of unsecured protected health information.

The General Data Protection Regulation, known as GDPR, is a comprehensive data protection law enacted by the European Union that applies to any organization processing the personal data of individuals in the European Union regardless of where the organization is located. Key principles of the GDPR include requiring a lawful basis for all data processing, data minimization, purpose limitation, storage limitation, accuracy, security, and accountability. The regulation requires organizations to notify supervisory authorities within seventy-two hours of discovering a personal data breach. It establishes individual rights including the right to access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability, and objection. Organizations that violate the GDPR face significant fines of up to four percent of annual global revenue or twenty million euros, whichever is greater.

The CIS Controls, developed by the Center for Internet Security, is a prioritized set of eighteen controls that address the most common and impactful attack vectors. The controls are organized into three Implementation Groups that allow organizations of different sizes and risk profiles to apply controls proportionate to their capabilities. Implementation Group 1 covers the most essential controls suitable for organizations with limited cybersecurity expertise, while Implementation Group 3 covers all controls appropriate for sophisticated organizations facing the most significant threats.

When applying standards in an organization, the process typically begins with a gap assessment that compares the organization's current security controls and practices against the requirements of the chosen standard. The gap assessment identifies control deficiencies and areas of partial compliance that require attention. Remediation planning converts the gap findings into a prioritized action plan with assigned owners, timelines, and resource requirements. Once controls are implemented, the organization must collect and maintain evidence of their effectiveness to support internal review and external audit or certification processes.
