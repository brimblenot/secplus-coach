Risk management is the systematic process of identifying, assessing, and responding to risks in a way that enables informed decisions about how to allocate security resources to protect organizational assets. A fundamental principle of risk management is that the goal is not to eliminate all risk, because doing so is impossible and would be prohibitively expensive even if it were possible. The real goal is to understand what risks exist, quantify their potential impact, and manage them to levels that are acceptable given the organization's objectives, risk appetite, and available resources. Every organization of any size engages in some form of risk management, whether formally structured or not, because every decision about whether to implement a security control is implicitly a risk management decision.

The core concepts of risk management form a vocabulary that appears throughout the Security+ exam and in professional security practice. A threat is any circumstance or event with the potential to cause harm to an organization's assets. Threats come from many sources including malicious actors such as cybercriminals, nation-state actors, hacktivists, and malicious insiders; natural events such as floods, hurricanes, earthquakes, and fires; accidental human errors such as misconfiguration or inadvertent data disclosure; and technical failures such as hardware malfunctions or software bugs. A vulnerability is a weakness in a system, application, process, or control that could be exploited by a threat to cause harm. Common vulnerabilities include unpatched software, weak password policies, misconfigured systems, lack of multifactor authentication, and insufficient logging. Risk is the combination of the likelihood that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and the impact that exploitation would have on the organization. A useful conceptual formula expresses risk as the product of threat, vulnerability, and asset value, though in practice risk assessment methodologies vary in how they quantify these factors. An asset is anything of value that the organization needs to protect, including information and data, hardware and software systems, people, facilities, intellectual property, and organizational reputation.

Risk appetite is the amount and type of risk that an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives. Risk appetite is set at the board or senior management level and reflects the organization's fundamental strategic posture toward uncertainty. A financial institution that handles customer deposits may have an extremely low risk appetite for data breach because even a small breach could trigger regulatory action and severely damage customer trust. A technology startup in an early growth phase may have a higher risk appetite for certain categories of risk in exchange for moving quickly to capture market opportunity. Risk appetite shapes security investment decisions by establishing which risks the organization is willing to tolerate without additional controls and which risks require mitigation regardless of cost.

Risk tolerance is a related but distinct concept that describes the acceptable variation around the risk appetite. If the risk appetite establishes the strategic boundary, the risk tolerance describes the operational threshold above which a specific risk becomes unacceptable and must be addressed. A practical analogy illustrates the distinction: the posted speed limit on a highway represents the risk appetite, the officially sanctioned maximum speed that balances safety against the need for transportation efficiency. In practice, law enforcement does not typically ticket drivers who are one mile per hour over the speed limit because there is a practical tolerance band. However, driving significantly above the speed limit exceeds the risk tolerance and triggers enforcement action. Similarly, an organization might have a risk appetite that calls for critical vulnerabilities to be patched within fourteen days but have a risk tolerance that allows for an exception if a patch breaks a critical application and must be tested before deployment.

The risk register is the central document that catalogs all identified risks within the organization or a specific project. Each entry in the risk register describes the nature of the risk, identifies the risk owner who is accountable for managing it, identifies the relevant threat and vulnerability, assigns a likelihood score indicating how probable the risk is, assigns an impact score indicating how severe the consequences would be if the risk were realized, calculates an inherent risk score representing the risk level before any controls are applied, describes the controls currently in place, calculates a residual risk score representing the risk remaining after current controls are accounted for, identifies the risk response strategy selected, establishes a target residual risk level, specifies a due date for completing any planned remediation activities, and tracks the current status of risk reduction efforts. The risk register serves as the authoritative source of truth for the organization's risk posture and is regularly reviewed and updated by risk owners and the security team.

Risk identification is the process of discovering and cataloging the risks that the organization faces. Threat modeling is a structured technique for systematically identifying threats to specific systems or applications. The STRIDE framework categorizes threats into six types: Spoofing of identity, Tampering with data, Repudiation of actions, Information disclosure, Denial of service, and Elevation of privilege. Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis, known as PASTA, is a risk-centric threat modeling framework that aligns business objectives with technical requirements. Attack trees model the ways an attacker might achieve a specific goal, working from the root goal downward through the prerequisite conditions. Vulnerability assessments using automated scanning tools identify technical weaknesses in systems and applications. Penetration testing goes further by attempting to actively exploit identified vulnerabilities to demonstrate real-world risk. Threat intelligence feeds provide information about current attacker tactics, techniques, and procedures that help organizations understand which threats are most active and most likely to be directed at their industry. Historical incident data from the organization's own experience and from industry sources such as the Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report provides empirical evidence about which threats materialize in practice and what their consequences are.

The risk assessment process follows a logical sequence. First, assets are identified and valued. Second, threats to those assets are identified using threat modeling, intelligence, and historical data. Third, vulnerabilities that could be exploited by those threats are identified through scanning, assessment, and analysis. Fourth, the likelihood of each threat exploiting each relevant vulnerability is estimated. Fifth, the potential impact of each risk scenario is estimated in terms of financial loss, reputational damage, operational disruption, and regulatory consequences. Sixth, a risk level is calculated by combining likelihood and impact. Seventh, risks are prioritized based on their calculated levels. Eighth, the complete risk picture is documented in the risk register and presented to management for decision-making.

Risk communication is the discipline of translating technical risk assessments into language and formats that resonate with executive and board audiences who may not have technical backgrounds. Risk heat maps are among the most effective communication tools, using a two-dimensional matrix with likelihood on one axis and impact on the other, color-coded with green for low risk, yellow for medium risk, and red for high risk, to provide an immediate visual sense of where the most significant risks lie. Quantitative risk metrics expressed in dollar terms of expected annual loss allow executives to understand security risks in the same language they use for other business risks such as market risk, credit risk, and operational risk. Regular risk reporting to the board or executive team, including trend analysis showing how the risk profile is changing over time, supports informed governance decisions about security investment and risk acceptance.

Risk assessments may be conducted on a one-time basis in connection with a specific project or acquisition, on a recurring schedule such as quarterly or annually, or on an ad hoc basis in response to a specific triggering event such as a new threat reported at an industry conference or a significant change to the organizational environment. Some regulatory frameworks mandate specific risk assessment frequencies: PCI DSS requires annual risk assessments, HIPAA requires periodic risk assessments of electronic protected health information, and FISMA requires annual risk assessments for federal information systems.
