Organizations formalize their relationships with vendors, partners, government agencies, and other external entities through a variety of agreement types. Each type of agreement serves a distinct purpose and carries different legal weight, specificity, and enforceability. The Security+ exam tests knowledge of these agreement types and the security provisions that belong in each one. Understanding when each agreement type is appropriate and what it must contain is an important competency for security professionals who participate in vendor and partner relationship management.

A Service Level Agreement, abbreviated SLA, defines the expected performance levels for a service and the remedies available to the customer when those levels are not met. SLAs are standard components of cloud service contracts, managed service provider agreements, internet service provider contracts, and other ongoing service relationships. The most common SLA metric is uptime percentage, which defines the proportion of time the service must be available. Uptime of 99.9 percent translates to approximately 8.7 hours of permitted downtime per year. Uptime of 99.99 percent translates to approximately 52 minutes of permitted downtime per year. Uptime of 99.999 percent, sometimes called five nines, translates to approximately five minutes per year. The gap between these levels is significant: the difference between 99.9 percent and 99.99 percent availability is nearly eight hours of additional permitted downtime per year.

SLAs also define response time commitments, specifying how quickly the provider must acknowledge an incident and how quickly they must resolve incidents of each severity level. For example, an SLA might require acknowledgment within one hour for all incidents and resolution within four hours for Severity 1 incidents that cause complete service unavailability. Mean time to repair commitments, throughput guarantees for bandwidth or transaction processing, and data recovery time commitments may also appear in SLAs. Penalties for non-compliance typically take the form of service credits that reduce future billing. For the most serious failures, the SLA may include termination rights allowing the customer to exit the contract without penalty if sustained underperformance occurs. Security-specific SLA provisions include breach notification timeframes specifying how quickly the provider must notify the customer of a security incident affecting their data, audit rights allowing the customer to verify compliance with security requirements, and requirements for specific security controls. Cloud provider SLAs frequently contain exclusions for scheduled maintenance windows, events caused by the customer's own actions, or force majeure events such as natural disasters.

A Memorandum of Understanding, abbreviated MOU, is a non-binding document that expresses mutual intent and understanding between two parties. The MOU describes the general principles and intentions of both parties without creating detailed legal obligations. Organizations use MOUs for exploratory partnerships and preliminary agreements before formal contract negotiation begins. Inter-organizational collaborations that do not require legally enforceable commitments, joint research initiatives, and preliminary arrangements for information sharing may be documented in MOUs. Because an MOU is not a contract and does not create legally actionable obligations, neither party can typically sue the other for failing to honor its terms. The MOU serves primarily as a statement of good faith and shared direction.

A Memorandum of Agreement, abbreviated MOA, is a step above the MOU in formality and specificity. An MOA is a binding agreement between two parties that commits them to specific actions, responsibilities, or activities. MOAs are commonly used in government contexts, including agreements between federal agencies and between government agencies and contractors. An MOA defines the specific deliverables each party will provide, the responsibilities each party will fulfill, the timeline for activities, and the resources each party will contribute. Unlike a full contract, an MOA may be less formal in its legal drafting, but it carries the intent of both parties to fulfill their commitments. MOAs are used for inter-agency information sharing arrangements, joint projects, and shared infrastructure agreements where both parties have specific obligations.

A Master Service Agreement, abbreviated MSA, is a comprehensive legal contract that establishes the foundational terms governing an ongoing business relationship. The MSA sets the framework that will apply to all work performed under the relationship, avoiding the need to renegotiate fundamental terms for each new project. Typical MSA provisions include the general terms of service, the billing and payment process, liability limitations and indemnification, dispute resolution procedures, intellectual property ownership, confidentiality obligations, and the security requirements that apply to all work under the agreement. Once an MSA is in place, individual projects or work engagements are documented in Statements of Work or work orders that reference the MSA and add project-specific details without repeating all the foundational terms. The MSA and Statement of Work combination is the standard structure for relationships with consulting firms, managed service providers, and technology vendors that perform ongoing or recurring work.

A Statement of Work, abbreviated SOW, documents the specific details of a particular work engagement or project within the framework of an MSA. A SOW typically defines the scope of the work to be performed, the deliverables expected and their specifications, the timeline and milestones, the location or environment where work will be performed, the resources and access the client will provide to the vendor, and the acceptance criteria that determine when the work is considered complete. The SOW is essential because it creates a shared, written record of expectations that can be referenced if questions arise about whether a particular item was included in the engagement.

A Non-Disclosure Agreement, abbreviated NDA, creates a contractual obligation to maintain the confidentiality of information shared between parties. NDAs are signed before sharing sensitive technical, business, security, or financial information with external parties. The key elements of an NDA include the definition of confidential information, specifying precisely what categories of information are subject to the confidentiality obligation; the duration of the obligation, which commonly ranges from two to five years for most business information but may be indefinite for trade secrets; the permitted uses of the information, specifying who within the receiving organization may access it and for what purposes; the obligations to protect the information, typically requiring the same degree of care the receiving party uses for its own confidential information; and the remedies for breach, which typically include both injunctive relief to stop ongoing disclosure and liquidated damages to compensate for harm. NDAs may be unilateral, also called one-way, where only one party has confidentiality obligations, as when an employee signs an NDA protecting the employer's information; bilateral, also called mutual, where both parties have obligations, as when two companies share sensitive information with each other during due diligence; or multilateral, where three or more parties all have obligations, as in a consortium arrangement. Employees routinely sign NDAs at the time of hire covering all proprietary information they encounter during their employment.

A Data Processing Agreement, abbreviated DPA, is specifically required by the General Data Protection Regulation when a data controller shares personal data with a data processor. GDPR Article 28 requires that every controller-processor relationship be governed by a contract that specifies several mandatory provisions. The DPA must specify the nature and purpose of the processing, the types of personal data involved, the categories of data subjects whose data will be processed, the duration of the processing, and the obligations and rights of the controller. The DPA must require the processor to process personal data only on documented instructions from the controller, to implement appropriate technical and organizational security measures, to notify the controller promptly when it becomes aware of a data breach, to support the controller in fulfilling data subject rights requests, to delete or return all personal data at the end of the relationship, and to provide all information necessary to demonstrate compliance with GDPR. Sub-processor requirements must also be included, obligating the processor to obtain controller approval before engaging sub-processors and to impose equivalent obligations on any sub-processors it engages.

Interconnection Security Agreements, abbreviated ISA, are specific to the technical connections established between the networks of different organizations or agencies. NIST Special Publication 800-47 provides guidance on ISAs for federal information system interconnections. An ISA documents the technical details of the network connection including protocols, ports, and systems involved; the security controls protecting each end of the connection; the types of data transmitted across the connection; the responsible parties on each end; incident response contact information; and the schedule for reviewing and reauthorizing the connection. ISAs are required between government agencies, between agencies and contractors accessing agency systems, and for critical infrastructure interconnections.

Beyond the primary agreement types, security-specific contract clauses appear in various agreement types and are important to understand. Requirements for specific encryption standards, patch management obligations, access control configurations, and security monitoring may be embedded as contractual obligations. Breach notification clauses specify the maximum time the vendor has to notify the organization after discovering a security incident involving the organization's data. Liability and indemnification provisions determine who bears financial responsibility when a breach or other failure occurs. Cyber liability insurance requirements obligate vendors to maintain specified coverage limits. Data ownership clauses confirm that data created during the contract belongs to the organization, not the vendor. Asset return and destruction requirements specify how the vendor must handle the organization's data and assets at contract termination.
