Impersonation is a broad social engineering technique in which an attacker pretends to be someone or something else in order to gain the trust, cooperation, or information of a target. The impersonated identity may be a colleague, an IT support technician, a government official, an executive, a vendor representative, or any entity that the target would be inclined to trust and comply with. Impersonation underpins many specific attack types including phishing, vishing, pretexting, and business email compromise. Understanding how impersonation works and the psychological levers it exploits is essential for both recognizing attacks and designing effective countermeasures.

Pretexting is the practice of creating a fabricated scenario — a pretext — to provide context and justification for the request being made. A well-constructed pretext is researched, plausible, and internally consistent. The attacker may impersonate an IT support technician calling to fix a problem that has been detected on the employee's workstation. The pretext here is the purported technical problem: "We've noticed some unusual activity from your system and need to verify your credentials to isolate the issue." In reality, no problem exists and no verification is needed — the credentials are the objective. Another common pretext involves impersonating a vendor representative visiting to service office equipment, a newly hired employee who needs access to a system to get started, or a government auditor requiring access to financial records. Effective pretexting requires the attacker to maintain the story consistently throughout the interaction, answer follow-up questions convincingly, and project confidence. The goal is to move the target's decision-making from "Is this person legitimate?" to "How do I help this person?" because once that shift occurs, most people naturally provide assistance.

A real-world illustration of how impersonation and pretexting work in practice is the style of scam calls that millions of people receive. One type says, "Hello, my name is Wendy and I'm from Microsoft Windows. This is an urgent checkup call for your computer as we have found several problems with it." Microsoft does not proactively call users to report problems, but the combination of a corporate name, a friendly tone, and an urgent framing is enough to deceive many recipients. Another type uses authority and legal threat: "This is an enforcement action executed by the US Treasury intending your serious attention." The grammatical errors in such messages are often a tell, but fear of legal consequences can override critical scrutiny. A third type uses flattery and financial incentive: "Congratulations on your excellent payment history. You now qualify for zero percent interest rates on all your credit card accounts." Each of these represents impersonation designed to elicit cooperation or information.

Elicitation is a more subtle form of impersonation-based information gathering. Rather than directly asking for sensitive information, the attacker engages the target in seemingly casual conversation and steers the discussion toward areas of interest, drawing out information the target would never consciously choose to reveal to a stranger. An attacker might pose as a fellow conference attendee and discuss industry topics, gradually moving toward questions about the company's security posture, upcoming product releases, or organizational structure. The target, believing they are having a professional networking conversation, may volunteer details about internal systems, project timelines, and key personnel without recognizing that they are being manipulated. Elicitation is particularly effective in competitive intelligence gathering and espionage contexts.

Shoulder surfing involves physically observing a target's screen, keyboard, or documents to capture sensitive information such as passwords, PINs, financial data, or confidential documents. This attack is most effective in public spaces where people use laptops or mobile devices: coffee shops, airports, aircraft, trains, and open-plan offices are all environments where shoulder surfing is feasible. Sophisticated shoulder surfing can use binoculars or long-range cameras to observe targets from a distance that the victim would not perceive as threatening. Defenses include privacy screens — polarized film filters that make the display readable only from directly in front, appearing dark or blank from any other angle — as well as situational awareness about who is positioned near the screen, locking the screen whenever stepping away, and keeping sensitive documents out of sight in public environments.

Dumpster diving is the practice of searching through an organization's discarded physical materials to recover sensitive information. Information valuable to an attacker might be found in printed documents containing confidential data, organizational charts revealing the internal structure and key roles, handwritten notes with passwords or access codes, old hard drives and USB drives that were not properly wiped before disposal, system manuals that reveal software and hardware configurations, and even calendar printouts that expose meeting schedules and travel plans. The defense against dumpster diving is a multi-layered physical document destruction policy: all documents should be shredded using cross-cut shredders rather than strip-cut shredders, since strip-cut shredded documents can be reassembled. Storage media must be physically destroyed or properly degaussed before disposal, not simply formatted. A clean desk policy — requiring employees to clear their desks of sensitive materials at the end of each workday — reduces the volume of material that could be recovered if someone accesses the physical space.

Tailgating and piggybacking exploit social norms to gain unauthorized physical access to secured areas. Tailgating occurs when an unauthorized person follows an authorized person through a secured door or gate without their knowledge, taking advantage of the fact that doors often remain open for a moment after the authorized person passes through. Most people are reluctant to confront someone they assume is a colleague, so the tailgater appears to belong and is rarely challenged. Piggybacking is similar but distinct: the authorized person knowingly allows the unauthorized individual to follow them through — perhaps holding the door open out of politeness — without verifying that the follower has authorization to be in that area. The defense against tailgating and piggybacking begins with access control vestibules, also called mantraps: these are small enclosed areas with two doors, where the first door must close and lock before the second door can open, physically preventing more than one person from entering simultaneously. Security awareness training must teach employees that it is not only acceptable but required to challenge people they do not recognize in secured areas, and that requiring badge verification is professional, not rude. Visitor escort policies requiring that all guests be accompanied by an authorized employee at all times prevent unauthorized access even when social engineering of an employee succeeds.

Caller ID spoofing allows attackers to make phone calls appear to originate from any number they choose, using VoIP services that allow arbitrary caller ID configuration. An attacker can make a call appear to come from a bank's official customer service number, a government agency, a known business partner, or even a number within the victim's own organization. The defense is to never treat caller ID as authentication — if someone calls claiming to be from your bank, hang up and call the number on your bank card or the bank's official website.

Identity fraud occurs when an attacker uses stolen personal information — Social Security number, date of birth, address, and other identifying details — to impersonate the victim in dealings with financial institutions, government agencies, or service providers. The attacker may open credit card accounts in the victim's name using their own address, take out loans, access existing accounts, or file fraudulent tax returns to capture the victim's refund. All of these activities damage the victim's credit and financial standing while providing the attacker with financial gain. The attacker benefits from the victim's good credit history and established identity while bearing none of the financial obligations.

The most commonly exploited impersonation scenario in corporate environments is the helpdesk call. The script is straightforward: "Hi, I'm from IT. We've detected a problem with your account and need your credentials to fix it immediately." This is effective because IT does legitimately contact users, and because the framing — a problem already exists, action is needed now — triggers both authority compliance and urgency. The critical defense is a firm organizational policy that IT staff will never ask for a user's password over the phone, by email, or through any other channel, combined with training so employees know to refuse such requests without guilt and report them through a designated suspicious contact reporting channel. Verification procedures — such as requiring the caller to provide a valid ticket number or employee ID, and calling back on a known internal number — should be standard for any request involving access to sensitive systems or information.

Doppelganger domains — also called lookalike or homograph domains — involve registering a domain name visually or phonetically similar to an organization's legitimate domain for use in email impersonation, fake websites, or credential harvesting. Combined with caller ID spoofing, pretexting, and carefully researched targeting, these technical impersonation aids make social engineering attacks highly convincing even to alert targets. The defense ecosystem must therefore combine technical controls with a culture that validates identity through known channels rather than trusting presented credentials at face value.
