The CIA Triad is one of the most fundamental models in information security. It provides a structured framework for thinking about what security is designed to protect and why. The three letters stand for Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability, and together they represent the three core security objectives that every security control, policy, and architecture is ultimately designed to support. You may occasionally see this model called the AIC Triad to avoid confusion with the United States Central Intelligence Agency, but both names refer to the same concept. The CIA Triad is typically depicted as a triangle in which each side represents one of the three objectives, reflecting the fact that all three must be balanced and that weakening any one of them weakens the overall security posture.

Confidentiality is the property of ensuring that information is accessible only to those who are authorized to access it. The goal of confidentiality controls is to prevent unauthorized disclosure of sensitive information. This matters across all types of data: personal health information, financial records, trade secrets, government classified data, and anything else whose exposure would cause harm.

Encryption is the primary technical mechanism for achieving confidentiality. When data is encrypted, it is transformed from readable plaintext into ciphertext that cannot be understood without the correct decryption key. This protects data both in transit across a network and at rest on a storage device. Even if an attacker intercepts the ciphertext, they cannot read its contents without the key. Symmetric encryption algorithms such as AES (Advanced Encryption Standard) and asymmetric algorithms such as RSA are both used to provide confidentiality depending on the use case.

Access controls are another foundational confidentiality mechanism. Access control lists, role-based permissions, and group membership restrict which users and systems can read or modify specific data. The need-to-know principle states that a person should only be granted access to information that is necessary for them to perform their specific job function. Someone in the marketing department should be able to access marketing presentations but not the organization's payroll or financial records.

Data classification is a structured confidentiality tool. Organizations label their data according to its sensitivity. Common classification levels include public data (freely shareable), private or internal data (for employees only), sensitive or confidential data (restricted to those with a business need), and regulated data (governed by laws such as HIPAA for healthcare information or PCI DSS for payment card data). Classification ensures that the level of protection applied to data matches its actual risk if disclosed.

Multi-factor authentication, commonly abbreviated as MFA, supports confidentiality by ensuring that even if an attacker obtains a user's password, they still cannot access the system without the additional authentication factor. Requiring something the user knows plus something the user has, or something the user is, dramatically reduces the likelihood of unauthorized access. Steganography, the practice of hiding data within other data such as embedding a message inside an image file, is another technique that can support confidentiality by concealing the very existence of sensitive information.

Integrity is the property of ensuring that data has not been altered, either accidentally or maliciously, during storage or transmission. Integrity controls allow a recipient to verify that what they received is exactly what was sent, and that nothing has been changed in between.

Hashing is the primary technical mechanism for verifying integrity. A hash function takes an input of any size and produces a fixed-length output called a hash value or message digest. The same input always produces the same hash, but any change to the input, even a single bit, produces a completely different hash. Common hashing algorithms include SHA-256 (Secure Hash Algorithm 256-bit), SHA-3, and MD5 (though MD5 is now considered cryptographically weak and should not be used for security purposes). By comparing the hash of received data against a known good hash, a recipient can confirm whether the data was modified in transit.

Digital signatures combine hashing with asymmetric cryptography to provide both integrity and authentication. The sender hashes their message and then encrypts that hash with their private key, creating the digital signature. The recipient decrypts the signature using the sender's public key and compares the resulting hash to their own hash of the received data. If the hashes match, the data has not been altered and it could only have been signed by the holder of the private key. Digital signatures are used to sign software packages, documents, code, and email.

File integrity monitoring, commonly called FIM, is a technical control that continuously monitors the hash values of critical system files and alerts administrators when an unexpected change is detected. This is particularly important for detecting malware that modifies operating system files or configuration files. Change control processes, which require documentation and approval before any system modification is made, are a managerial integrity control that prevents unauthorized or accidental changes to critical systems.

Non-repudiation is closely related to integrity. It refers to the property of being able to prove beyond reasonable doubt that a specific entity performed a specific action, such that they cannot later deny it. Digital signatures provide non-repudiation because the private key used to sign the message is unique to the sender, and only the holder of that private key could have created that signature. Non-repudiation is critical in legal and financial contexts where disputes about whether a transaction occurred must be resolved definitively.

Availability is the property of ensuring that information systems and the data they contain are accessible and usable by authorized users whenever they need them. Availability is often the security objective that users feel most directly because it affects their ability to do their jobs.

Redundancy is a core availability mechanism. Redundant systems ensure that if one component fails, another can take over without interrupting service. This applies at every layer: redundant power supplies within servers, redundant servers within a cluster, redundant network links between facilities, and redundant data centers in different geographic locations. RAID (Redundant Array of Independent Disks) provides disk-level redundancy so that a single drive failure does not cause data loss. RAID 1 mirrors data across two drives, RAID 5 uses striping with parity across three or more drives, and RAID 6 can tolerate two simultaneous drive failures.

Fault tolerance is the ability of a system to continue operating correctly even when some of its components fail. High availability clusters are groups of servers configured so that if one node fails, another node automatically takes over its workload within seconds, often transparently to end users. Load balancers distribute incoming requests across multiple servers, which both improves performance and ensures availability if any single server becomes unavailable.

Backups are an essential availability and recovery mechanism. Regular, tested backups ensure that even if data is corrupted, deleted, or encrypted by ransomware, the organization can restore operations from a clean copy. RTO (Recovery Time Objective) defines the maximum acceptable time to restore a system after a failure. RPO (Recovery Point Objective) defines the maximum acceptable amount of data loss measured in time, meaning how far back in time the most recent backup can be. Together, RTO and RPO define the organization's availability requirements and drive backup frequency and recovery architecture decisions.

DDoS protection, where DDoS stands for Distributed Denial of Service, addresses a specific availability threat in which an attacker floods a system or network with so much traffic that legitimate users cannot access it. DDoS mitigation services, traffic scrubbing, and rate limiting are technical controls that protect availability from this class of attack. UPS devices (Uninterruptible Power Supplies) protect against power outages that would otherwise cause systems to go offline, and they are a physical availability control.

Service Level Agreements, or SLAs, are contractual commitments from service providers about the minimum level of availability they guarantee, typically expressed as a percentage of uptime such as 99.9% (which allows approximately 8.7 hours of downtime per year) or 99.99% (which allows approximately 52 minutes per year). SLAs drive the technical requirements for redundancy and fault tolerance.

The adversarial counterpart to the CIA Triad is sometimes described as the DAD Triad: Disclosure, Alteration, and Destruction. Disclosure is the adversarial outcome that violates Confidentiality, achieved through data breaches, eavesdropping, and credential theft. Alteration is the adversarial outcome that violates Integrity, achieved through man-in-the-middle attacks, SQL injection that modifies records, and malware that corrupts files. Destruction is the adversarial outcome that violates Availability, achieved through ransomware, destructive malware, physical destruction of hardware, and denial of service attacks. Every security threat maps to at least one element of the DAD Triad, and every security control is designed to prevent at least one DAD outcome.

There is an inherent tension between the three pillars of the CIA Triad. Maximizing security controls to enforce confidentiality can make systems harder to use, reducing effective availability. Requiring very strong authentication before granting access improves confidentiality but introduces friction. Adding redundant systems improves availability but also increases the attack surface that must be secured for confidentiality and integrity. Security professionals must understand these trade-offs and make deliberate decisions about how to balance all three objectives in a way that supports the organization's mission. Risk, defined as the probability that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and cause harm, is the measure of how likely it is that any of the three CIA pillars will be violated, and risk management is the ongoing process of maintaining that balance.
