An Indicator of Compromise, abbreviated IoC, is an observable artifact, piece of evidence, or behavioral pattern that provides high confidence that a system, network, or account has been breached or is currently under attack. Security operations teams use IoCs to detect ongoing intrusions, investigate past incidents, and determine the scope of a breach. IoCs can be as specific as a file hash matching a known piece of malware or as behavioral as an account logging in at an unusual time from an unusual location. The ability to recognize, collect, and act on IoCs is fundamental to effective incident response and threat detection.

Account activity provides some of the earliest and most reliable indicators of compromise. Unusual login times are a common first signal: an account that consistently authenticates during business hours between 8 AM and 6 PM from an office IP address becoming active at 3 AM from an external IP address is immediately suspicious. Logins from geographic locations that are new for the account, particularly from countries with no business relationship to the organization, warrant investigation. Impossible travel is a specific and highly reliable indicator: if the same account authenticates from New York at 9 AM and from London at 11 AM, it is physically impossible for the same person to have made both logins, strongly suggesting account compromise. Multiple failed login attempts followed by a successful login, especially against accounts with complex passwords, may indicate that an attacker successfully cracked or obtained the password after repeated guessing. Privilege escalation events, where a standard user account is suddenly assigned administrative rights outside normal provisioning processes, are significant indicators. Dormant accounts that have not been used for months or years suddenly becoming active may indicate that an attacker has gained access to old credentials. New accounts created outside the normal provisioning workflow, particularly with elevated privileges, indicate possible attacker persistence establishment.

Network activity indicators reflect the communication patterns of compromised hosts. Unusually large outbound data transfers, particularly to previously unseen external IP addresses during off-hours, are a primary indicator of data exfiltration. Beaconing is a characteristic network pattern where a compromised host makes periodic connections to the same external IP address at regular intervals, such as every 60 seconds or every hour, indicating communication with a command and control server. These connections are often small and may appear to be routine, but the regularity distinguishes them from organic user activity. DNS queries to suspicious domains, including newly registered domains, algorithmically generated domains, or domains with no legitimate reputation, indicate possible malware communication. Traffic on unusual port numbers, particularly those commonly used by exploit frameworks such as port 4444 used by Metasploit or port 6666, suggests unauthorized tool use. Protocol anomalies such as HTTP traffic on non-standard ports or DNS queries with unusually long domain names or high TXT record query rates may indicate tunneling attempts. Connections to Tor exit nodes or known malicious IP addresses from the threat intelligence feeds are direct indicators of malicious network activity. Lateral movement patterns, specifically SMB, WMI, or PsExec traffic from workstations to other internal systems outside normal administrative patterns, indicate an attacker moving through the environment after initial compromise.

Host-based indicators of compromise provide evidence of malicious activity on individual systems. Unexpected processes running on a system, particularly processes that are children of applications that should not spawn them such as a Microsoft Word process that has launched a PowerShell child process, are a high-confidence indicator of exploitation. New services or scheduled tasks created outside normal administrative activity indicate attacker persistence mechanisms. Registry modifications, especially additions to the Run and RunOnce keys under HKEY_CURRENT_USER and HKEY_LOCAL_MACHINE, indicate malware establishing persistence that survives reboots. Files created or modified in system directories at unexpected times, particularly unsigned executable files, suggest malware installation. Newly installed browser extensions that were not deployed by IT can indicate credential-stealing malware. Unusual CPU or memory consumption by unexpected processes may indicate cryptomining malware or active exploitation. Security tools being disabled or tampered with, including antivirus signatures stopping updates or host-based firewall rules being modified, is a strong indicator that malware is actively evading detection on the host. Unexpected reboots or crashes, particularly on servers that should have high uptime, may indicate exploitation attempts that destabilized the system.

File-based indicators are among the most concrete and shareable IoC types. Malware files have known hash values for specific samples, and matching a file's MD5, SHA-1, or SHA-256 hash against a threat intelligence database can immediately identify known malware. File names that mimic legitimate system processes but with subtle differences, such as svchost32.exe or csrss_.exe with an underscore, indicate malware attempting to blend in by exploiting users' tendency not to look closely at process names. Files in unusual locations such as the AppData directory, the Temp directory, or the Windows Temp directory are suspicious for executable files, as legitimate system executables reside in System32 or established program directories. Unsigned executables in system directories, particularly in directories that normally contain only Microsoft-signed binaries, indicate possible malware or attacker tool placement. Recently created executable files, especially those with creation timestamps that align with suspicious access events, are high-priority investigation targets. Script files with PowerShell, VBScript, or batch extensions appearing in user profile directories or temporary directories outside normal script repositories warrant investigation.

Email-based indicators are crucial for detecting phishing, business email compromise, and social engineering campaigns. Phishing emails often originate from domains that closely mimic legitimate domains, with subtle character substitutions or additional words. Unusual attachment types, particularly macro-enabled Office documents arriving from external parties with no prior communication, are a common delivery mechanism for initial access malware. Emails containing Base64-encoded content or unusual character encodings in the body may be attempting to evade content-based filters. Forwarding rules created on an inbox to send copies of all received email to an external address are a significant indicator of business email compromise: attackers establish these rules early to maintain intelligence access even if the account password is later changed.

Threat intelligence sharing infrastructure enables organizations to exchange IoC data efficiently. STIX, Structured Threat Information eXpression, is an open standard format for representing threat intelligence including IoCs, TTPs, and threat actor information in a machine-readable way. TAXII, Trusted Automated eXchange of Intelligence Information, is the associated protocol for distributing STIX data between organizations and platforms. Information Sharing and Analysis Centers, called ISACs, are sector-specific organizations that facilitate threat intelligence sharing among peer organizations in industries such as financial services, healthcare, energy, and transportation. VirusTotal aggregates hash reputation data and antivirus scan results for file samples, allowing security teams to quickly determine whether a file hash is associated with known malware. Commercial threat intelligence platforms from providers including CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Unit 42, and Mandiant provide curated, contextualized IoC feeds with attribution information and tactical guidance.

The MITRE ATT&CK framework provides a structured taxonomy of attacker Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures organized by the stages of an intrusion. IoCs can be mapped to specific ATT&CK techniques, providing context about what the attacker may be trying to accomplish. For example, observing a process injecting code into another process maps to technique T1055 Process Injection under the Defense Evasion tactic, suggesting the attacker is attempting to execute code in the context of a trusted process.

Indicators of Attack, or IoAs, are distinct from IoCs in that they represent behavioral patterns associated with an active attack in progress, before a compromise has been fully confirmed. IoAs are based on attacker behavior rather than specific artifacts and can enable detection and response before the attacker achieves their objective, whereas IoCs confirm that a compromise has already occurred.

In a SIEM environment, IoCs are operationalized through correlation rules that trigger alerts when log data matches known-malicious patterns, automated blocking based on known-malicious IP addresses and file hashes, and threat hunting workflows that use IoCs to search historical log data for evidence of past compromise that was not detected in real time. IoC management requires attention to currency: IP addresses and domains change frequently, malware evolves and receives new hashes, and IoCs without expiration dates accumulate into threat intelligence feeds that generate excessive false positives. Maintaining a useful IoC database requires regularly reviewing, aging out, and refreshing data from current sources, and calibrating detection thresholds to balance detection sensitivity against analyst alert fatigue.
