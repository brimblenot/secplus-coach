Physical security is the set of controls designed to prevent unauthorized physical access to facilities, equipment, and resources, and to protect those assets from physical damage, theft, and environmental hazards. Security+ professionals must understand physical security because even the strongest digital security controls can be bypassed if an attacker gains physical access to systems. A threat actor with physical access to a server can bypass operating system authentication, extract hard drives, install hardware keyloggers, and cause damage that no firewall can prevent. Physical security follows the same defense-in-depth principle as logical security, implementing multiple layers of protection from the outermost perimeter inward to individual devices.

The layered approach to physical security begins at the perimeter, the outer boundary of the physical area being protected, and works inward through progressively more sensitive zones. Outer perimeter controls create the first barrier; building-level controls restrict access to the structure itself; room-level controls protect sensitive areas such as server rooms, executive offices, and research labs; and device-level controls protect individual workstations, servers, and portable equipment.

Perimeter security controls form the outer defense boundary. Fencing is one of the most visible perimeter controls. Chain-link fencing is common and cost-effective but can be cut or climbed. Steel palisade fencing, composed of steel posts welded together, is far more resistant to cutting and climbing. Security standards generally recommend fences be at least eight feet tall to deter casual climbing attempts. Razor wire or barbed wire along the top of a fence significantly increases the difficulty of climbing over it. Opaque fencing prevents potential attackers from visually surveying the interior before attempting entry. Transparent fencing allows security cameras to monitor the exterior from inside and can make anyone attempting to climb the fence visible to guards.

Bollards are reinforced concrete or steel posts, typically cylindrical in shape, installed at vehicle access points and around building perimeters to prevent vehicle ramming attacks. Bollards protect against threats that range from accidental vehicle incursions to deliberate vehicle-borne attacks intended to breach a perimeter or destroy equipment. Fixed bollards permanently block vehicle access. Removable or retractable bollards can be lowered to allow authorized vehicle access. Jersey barriers and K-rails are larger pre-cast concrete barriers used for temporary or permanent vehicle access control in high-security environments. Vehicle barriers are particularly important for data centers and government facilities where the consequence of a vehicle breach is severe.

Security lighting is an environmental perimeter control. Well-lit exteriors deter unauthorized access attempts because most intruders prefer to operate in darkness where they are less likely to be observed. Security lighting should illuminate all entry points, parking areas, loading docks, stairwells, and the spaces along fence lines. Motion-activated lighting conserves energy while ensuring that movement in previously dark areas is immediately illuminated and draws attention. Lighting placement must consider camera coverage angles; if cameras are used for facial recognition at entry points, lighting must be positioned to eliminate shadows and ensure adequate illumination for image capture.

Security signage is a directive and deterrent physical control. Signs notifying visitors that an area is under CCTV surveillance, that trespassers will be prosecuted, or that an area requires authorization for entry set behavioral expectations and may deter casual intruders. Signs are not a strong physical control on their own but reinforce other physical and procedural controls.

Access control vestibules, sometimes called mantraps, are a critical building entry control. A vestibule is a small room or enclosure with two sets of interlocking doors between the public exterior and the secured interior. Only one door can be open at a time, either through mechanical interlock or electronic control. This design prevents tailgating and piggybacking. Tailgating occurs when an unauthorized person follows an authorized person through a secured door without the authorized person's knowledge. Piggybacking is similar but occurs with the authorized person's consent, which is a social engineering technique. Because the vestibule can only admit one person (or one controlled group) at a time, and requires each person to authenticate individually, neither tailgating nor piggybacking can succeed. Access control vestibules are commonly deployed at data center entrances, secure laboratory entries, and high-security government facilities.

Badge readers and access card systems are the primary electronic access control mechanism for buildings and rooms. Proximity cards, also called RFID (Radio Frequency Identification) cards, use radio frequency signals to communicate with a reader when brought within range without direct contact. Smart cards contain an embedded integrated circuit chip that can perform cryptographic operations, providing stronger authentication than simple RFID proximity cards. Many access control systems combine card reading with PIN (Personal Identification Number) entry to implement two-factor authentication for physical access. All badge reader events should be logged to a central access control database, creating an audit trail of who entered which area at what time.

Security guards provide human intelligence, judgment, and physical response capability that automated systems cannot replicate. Guards at reception desks verify visitor identities, issue visitor badges, contact the employee being visited to confirm the appointment, and ensure visitors are escorted to their destination and returned to the lobby upon departure. Guards at perimeter checkpoints, such as guard shacks at vehicle entry gates, control vehicle and pedestrian access and can inspect vehicles, packages, and personnel. Security patrols involve guards walking prescribed routes through a facility and its perimeter at regular intervals, looking for signs of unauthorized access, security vulnerabilities such as propped-open doors, and physical threats.

The two-person integrity rule, also called two-person control, requires that no single individual is permitted to be alone in especially sensitive areas such as server rooms, vaults, or areas containing classified information. By requiring a second person to always be present, the rule prevents any one person from taking unauthorized actions without a witness. This is both a physical security control and a fraud prevention control.

Biometric access control systems use unique physiological or behavioral characteristics to verify identity. Fingerprint readers scan the unique ridge patterns of a fingertip. Retinal scanners analyze the unique vascular pattern at the back of the eye. Iris scanners analyze the colored ring around the pupil. Facial recognition systems compare facial geometry against enrolled photographs. Hand geometry readers measure the dimensions of the hand and fingers.

All biometric systems are evaluated using three key metrics. The False Acceptance Rate, or FAR, is the percentage of unauthorized users that the system incorrectly authenticates. A high FAR is a security failure. The False Rejection Rate, or FRR, is the percentage of authorized users that the system incorrectly rejects. A high FRR causes operational disruption and user frustration. The Crossover Error Rate, sometimes called the Equal Error Rate or CER/EER, is the point at which the FAR and FRR are equal. The CER provides a single number for comparing the accuracy of different biometric systems: a lower CER indicates a more accurate system with a better balance between security and usability.

CCTV (Closed-Circuit Television) surveillance systems provide continuous visual monitoring and recording of physical areas. Camera placement strategy must ensure coverage of all entry and exit points, server room doors, loading docks, parking areas, and any other areas where security-relevant activity might occur. PTZ cameras, which stands for Pan-Tilt-Zoom, can be remotely controlled to adjust their direction and zoom level, enabling a single camera to cover a large area or to follow a subject. Modern IP cameras integrate analytics capabilities including motion detection, object classification, license plate recognition, and facial recognition directly in the camera or in connected software systems. All camera footage should be recorded and retained according to the organization's video retention policy. Recording durations of thirty to ninety days are common, though regulated industries may have specific retention requirements. Monitoring stations where security staff can view live camera feeds are most effective when staffed continuously.

Environmental controls in data centers and server rooms protect equipment from temperature, humidity, and fire. ASHRAE (the American Society of Heating, Refrigerating and Air-Conditioning Engineers) guidelines recommend that server inlet air temperature be maintained between 64 and 80 degrees Fahrenheit (18 to 27 degrees Celsius) and that relative humidity be maintained between 40 and 60 percent. Temperatures above this range cause components to overheat and fail. Humidity above this range causes condensation that can damage electronics. Humidity below this range increases static electricity buildup, which can damage sensitive components. Redundant HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning) systems ensure that cooling continues if one unit fails.

Hot aisle and cold aisle server room layout is a design pattern that maximizes cooling efficiency and prevents hot air recirculation. Server racks are arranged so that all server front faces (where cool air is drawn in) face the same direction and all server rear faces (where hot air is exhausted) face the opposite direction. Cold aisles contain cool air supplied by raised-floor vents or overhead diffusers, and servers draw in this cool air from the front. Hot aisles capture the hot exhaust air from the rear of servers and return it to the HVAC system for cooling.

Fire suppression systems protect data centers from fire. Wet-pipe sprinkler systems, the most common type in general buildings, keep water in the pipes at all times and activate immediately when a sprinkler head detects heat. Wet-pipe systems can cause severe water damage to equipment and are not preferred for areas with expensive electronics. Dry-pipe systems keep the pipes empty until a fire causes a sprinkler to open, allowing water to flow; this provides a brief delay that can reduce accidental discharges. Pre-action systems require both a fire detector and a sprinkler activation to release water, providing additional protection against accidental discharge. Clean agent fire suppression systems, which include FM-200 (HFC-227ea) and Novec 1230, extinguish fires by interrupting combustion chemistry or by removing heat, without leaving any residue and without harming electronic equipment. These have replaced Halon 1301, which was formerly the standard clean agent but is now banned under the Montreal Protocol because of its ozone-depleting properties. Carbon dioxide (CO2) systems flood an area with CO2 to displace oxygen and extinguish fires, but they are lethal to humans and should only be used in unmanned areas.

Physical device security protects individual pieces of hardware from theft or tampering. Cable locks anchor laptops and workstations to fixed furniture using a steel cable and a keyed or combination lock, typically through a Kensington security slot. Server cages within data centers provide a lockable enclosure that restricts access to specific racks even within a shared data center facility. USB port blockers physically fill unused USB ports to prevent unauthorized USB device insertion, which could be used to install malware or exfiltrate data. Asset tracking tags, whether RFID-based or GPS-enabled, allow organizations to track the physical location of portable equipment.

Faraday cages are enclosures made of conductive material that block electromagnetic fields from passing through. A Faraday cage around a sensitive room prevents eavesdropping through electromagnetic emissions (a technique called TEMPEST or van Eck phreaking) and protects against electromagnetic pulse (EMP) attacks that would otherwise destroy electronic equipment. Secure government and military facilities use Faraday-shielded rooms to prevent electronic intelligence collection.

Air-gapped networks are computer networks that are physically isolated from all unsecured networks, including the internet. An air-gapped network has no wired or wireless connections to the outside world. Data can only enter or leave through physical media such as USB drives or optical discs, which themselves must be strictly controlled. Air gaps are used to protect the most sensitive systems, including industrial control systems for nuclear facilities, classified government networks, and critical infrastructure controls.

The clean desk policy requires employees to clear all sensitive documents and media from their work surfaces when they leave for the day or step away from their desks. Printed documents containing confidential information should be locked in drawers or file cabinets. Computer screens should be locked before leaving a workstation, and screen privacy filters can be used to prevent visual eavesdropping by people passing by. Clean desk policies prevent physical security threats such as shoulder surfing and opportunistic document theft.

Industrial camouflage is the practice of making a facility appear to be something other than what it is. Data centers housing critical infrastructure may be constructed to look like unremarkable commercial buildings or warehouses from the outside, with no external signage indicating their true purpose. This reduces the likelihood of a targeted physical attack by making the facility difficult to identify as a high-value target.
