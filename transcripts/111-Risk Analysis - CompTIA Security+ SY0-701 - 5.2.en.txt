Risk analysis is the process of determining the likelihood and potential impact of identified risks in order to prioritize them and inform decisions about which risks require treatment and what level of investment in controls is appropriate. Two primary methodologies are used in risk analysis: qualitative and quantitative. Each has distinct characteristics, advantages, and appropriate use cases, and security professionals must understand both approaches and the specific formulas associated with quantitative analysis.

Qualitative risk analysis uses descriptive, subjective scales to rate risk rather than precise monetary values. Analysts assess each risk according to two dimensions: likelihood, which describes how probable it is that a threat will exploit a vulnerability and cause harm, and impact, which describes how severe the consequences would be if the risk were realized. Both dimensions are rated using categorical scales. A typical likelihood scale might use categories such as Rare, Unlikely, Possible, Likely, and Almost Certain, or alternatively a numeric scale from one to five where one represents the lowest likelihood and five represents the near-certain occurrence. A typical impact scale might use categories such as Negligible, Minor, Moderate, Major, and Catastrophic, again potentially mapped to a one-to-five numeric scale. The overall risk level for each risk is derived by combining the likelihood and impact ratings, often by multiplying the numeric scores, producing a risk score that can be used to rank risks relative to each other.

Heat maps are the primary visualization tool for qualitative risk analysis. A heat map is a two-dimensional matrix with likelihood on one axis and impact on the other, divided into cells that are color-coded according to the combined risk level. Cells in the high-likelihood, high-impact corner are typically colored red to indicate high risk requiring immediate attention. Cells in the low-likelihood, low-impact corner are colored green to indicate low risk that may be accepted. Intermediate cells are yellow or orange to indicate medium risk that warrants monitoring and planned treatment. Heat maps allow executive audiences to immediately comprehend the overall risk profile without needing to interpret tables of numbers.

The advantages of qualitative risk analysis include that it is faster and simpler to conduct than quantitative analysis, works effectively even when precise data about threat frequency or loss magnitude is not available, requires less specialized expertise, and produces results that communicate well to non-technical audiences. The disadvantages include that ratings are inherently subjective and different analysts may assess the same risk differently, results are inconsistent across different analysts and organizations, and the approach cannot produce a return on investment calculation for security controls because it does not generate monetary values.

Quantitative risk analysis assigns monetary values to both the likelihood of risk events and their financial impact, enabling the calculation of expected financial loss and the return on investment of security controls. The Security+ exam requires knowledge of specific quantitative risk formulas and the terms associated with them.

Asset Value, abbreviated AV, is the monetary value of the asset to the organization. This is not simply the replacement cost of a hardware item but rather the full value the asset represents to the organization, including the revenue it generates, the data it stores, the regulatory fines that could result from its compromise, and any other costs associated with losing or compromising the asset.

Exposure Factor, abbreviated EF, is the percentage of the asset's value that would be lost if a specific threat were to occur. The exposure factor is expressed as a decimal between zero and one. If a fire completely destroys a server with no possibility of recovering any data, the exposure factor is 1.0 because one hundred percent of the asset value is lost. If a ransomware attack encrypts forty percent of an organization's data and the organization has backups that allow recovery of the remaining sixty percent, the exposure factor for that threat scenario would be 0.4 because forty percent of the data asset value is lost.

Single Loss Expectancy, abbreviated SLE, represents the expected monetary loss from a single occurrence of a specific threat against a specific asset. SLE is calculated by multiplying the Asset Value by the Exposure Factor: SLE equals AV multiplied by EF. For example, if a server has an asset value of fifty thousand dollars and the exposure factor for a complete hardware failure is 1.0 because the server would be completely destroyed, the SLE for that scenario is fifty thousand dollars. If a laptop has an asset value of one thousand dollars and the exposure factor for theft is 1.0, the SLE for a single laptop theft event is one thousand dollars.

Annualized Rate of Occurrence, abbreviated ARO, represents the estimated frequency with which a specific threat is expected to occur in a given year. An ARO of 0.1 means the threat is expected to occur once every ten years on average. An ARO of 1.0 means the threat is expected to occur approximately once per year. An ARO of 2.0 means the threat is expected to occur twice per year on average. Geographic, industry, and organizational factors influence ARO values. The annualized rate of occurrence for a hurricane is much higher in Florida than in Montana.

Annualized Loss Expectancy, abbreviated ALE, represents the expected total monetary loss from a specific risk over the course of a year. ALE is calculated by multiplying the Single Loss Expectancy by the Annualized Rate of Occurrence: ALE equals SLE multiplied by ARO. Using the laptop theft example: if seven laptops are expected to be stolen per year, the ARO is seven and the ALE is seven laptops times one thousand dollars per laptop equals seven thousand dollars per year. The ALE is the primary metric used to justify and compare the cost of security controls.

The return on investment calculation for a security control compares the cost of the control to the reduction in ALE it produces. If a laptop tracking and encryption solution costs two thousand dollars per year to operate and reduces the ALE from seven thousand dollars to two thousand dollars, the reduction in ALE is five thousand dollars. The net benefit of the control is five thousand dollars minus two thousand dollars equals three thousand dollars per year, justifying the investment. If a proposed control costs more than the reduction in ALE it produces, the investment cannot be justified on purely financial grounds and the risk may be better managed through acceptance or another strategy.

Beyond the specific formulas, risk analysis must account for different types of risk that interact with each other. Inherent risk is the risk that exists in the absence of any controls, representing the organization's natural exposure to a threat before any mitigation measures are applied. Residual risk is the risk that remains after all implemented controls are applied. The goal of security controls is to reduce inherent risk to a residual risk level that falls within the organization's risk tolerance. Control risk is the risk that security controls themselves will fail to operate as intended, whether due to misconfiguration, failure, or circumvention. Detection risk is the risk that problems will go undetected, allowing threats to persist or escalate without the organization's awareness.

Risk levels change over time as new threats emerge, new vulnerabilities are discovered, new assets are acquired, and the business environment evolves. Risk assessments should be reviewed periodically, typically quarterly, and re-analyzed whenever significant changes occur to the organizational environment, threat landscape, or asset profile.

Data sources for quantitative risk analysis include actuarial data published by insurance companies such as Marsh and AON, which track the actual financial losses organizations experience from different categories of incidents. The Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report and its associated VERIS database provide empirical data on breach frequency and characteristics across many industries. The Ponemon Institute publishes annual research on the cost of data breaches including average per-record costs, notification costs, and business interruption losses. The Factor Analysis of Information Risk framework, known as FAIR, is a specialized quantitative risk framework that provides a structured approach to defining and measuring information risk in financial terms, making it particularly useful for communicating risk to financial and executive audiences.
