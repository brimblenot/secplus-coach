Social engineering is the practice of manipulating people rather than systems to achieve an attacker's objective. While specific techniques like phishing and impersonation receive significant attention, the category of social engineering is broad and includes many additional methods that exploit human psychology, information overload, and organizational processes. Understanding the full range of social engineering techniques and the psychological principles that make them effective is essential for building security awareness programs and designing robust organizational defenses.

Typosquatting, also called URL hijacking, is the practice of registering domain names that are common misspellings or near-identical variations of legitimate, popular domains. When a user mistyping a URL — entering "gooogle.com" instead of "google.com," or "paypa1.com" with a numeral one instead of a lowercase letter L — lands on a typosquatted domain, they may encounter a credential-harvesting page designed to look like the legitimate site, a page serving malware through drive-by download, or simply advertising that generates revenue for the registrant. Attackers register hundreds of variants of high-traffic domain names because even a small fraction of mistyped visits generates sufficient traffic to be profitable. Bitsquatting is a variant that targets single-bit memory errors: because memory corruption can occasionally flip a single bit in a stored URL, domains that differ from a legitimate domain by exactly one bit in one character can capture traffic from systems experiencing random memory errors, which is particularly relevant for high-traffic servers making millions of DNS queries.

Influence campaigns are coordinated efforts to manipulate public opinion, spread disinformation, or sow division using social media and other online platforms. Nation-states are documented sponsors of such campaigns, which are used to undermine trust in democratic institutions, exacerbate social divisions, support cyberattack objectives by discrediting organizations before or after an operation, or create confusion during a crisis that prevents effective responses. The mechanics of an influence campaign typically begin with the attacker creating networks of fake accounts — sometimes called sockpuppets — that appear to be independent users. These accounts create and amplify content: they post fabricated or misleading articles, like and share posts from other fake accounts to create the appearance of organic popularity, and follow each other to seem like genuine community members. Social media algorithms detect engagement signals and amplify popular content to additional users, allowing a relatively small set of fake accounts to expose their content to vast real audiences. Once real users engage with and share the content, traditional media may amplify it further by reporting on the trend, completing the cycle from manufactured content to mainstream narrative. Brand impersonation in search results is a related technique: attackers create hundreds of websites using well-known brand names such as those of major corporations, which are then indexed by search engines. When a user searches for the brand, they may be directed to one of these impersonated sites, which presents fake offers, download links, or alert popups claiming the user must install software — software that is, of course, malware.

Invoice scams target accounts payable departments by sending fraudulent invoices for services or goods that were never provided. These scams rely on the volume of legitimate invoices that busy departments process: in a large organization receiving hundreds of invoices weekly, a plausible-looking invoice for a modest amount may be approved and paid without close examination. More sophisticated invoice scams are combined with Business Email Compromise: the attacker impersonates a known vendor and sends a message changing the payment details — bank account number or wire transfer routing — for future invoices, directing subsequent legitimate payments to attacker-controlled accounts.

The psychological principles that social engineers exploit are well-documented and reflect fundamental aspects of human decision-making. Authority is the principle that people are inclined to comply with requests from perceived authority figures without questioning them. An attacker impersonating an executive, a government agent, a law enforcement officer, or a senior IT administrator leverages authority to push targets past their instinct to verify. Urgency and scarcity create artificial time pressure that degrades the quality of decision-making: "Your account will be locked in one hour unless you verify immediately" is designed to prevent the target from pausing, researching, or asking colleagues for advice. Social proof, or consensus, exploits the human tendency to look at what others are doing as a guide to appropriate behavior: "Other departments have already submitted this information" or fake reviews and testimonials on phishing sites make the request seem normal and safe. Intimidation uses threats of negative consequences — job termination, legal action, service disruption — to override resistance. Familiarity and liking reflect the fact that people are significantly more likely to comply with requests from people they like or feel a connection to; attackers build rapport through friendly conversation, expressions of shared interest, or flattery before making their request. Reciprocity exploits the human tendency to return favors: an attacker who provides small helpful actions, useful information, or apparent assistance creates a sense of social obligation that the target then feels compelled to repay with cooperation. Fear is closely related to urgency and intimidation but operates more broadly, including fear of missing out, fear of embarrassment, and fear of being responsible for a negative outcome.

Hoaxes are false information or warnings spread to cause targets to take harmful actions. A classic hoax is a fake virus warning claiming that a specific system file is dangerous malware and instructing users to delete it, when in reality the file is a legitimate operating system component. Users who follow the hoax instructions damage their own systems without any malware ever being deployed. Modern hoaxes spread through social media and messaging applications and can cause widespread panic, wasted organizational resources, or actual system damage.

Diversion theft involves misdirecting a legitimate physical delivery — such as a package or document courier — to a location controlled by the attacker, rather than its intended destination. This may involve social engineering the delivery company, the sender, or the recipient, and can be used to intercept sensitive physical mail, payment instruments, or equipment.

Deepfake attacks represent an emerging and rapidly maturing social engineering threat. Audio deepfakes use AI-generated voice synthesis to create recordings that sound like specific individuals — executives, colleagues, or family members — and are used in voice-based fraud. In documented cases, attackers have used audio deepfakes impersonating a CEO's voice to instruct a finance employee to authorize an urgent wire transfer. Video deepfakes can create convincing video conference appearances, making the impersonation even more difficult to detect. As the quality of AI-generated audio and video continues to improve, deepfake attacks will become an increasingly significant component of the social engineering threat landscape.

The defense strategy against social engineering must be multi-layered because no single technical control can reliably stop all forms of human manipulation. Security awareness training is the foundational control: employees at all levels must understand the psychological triggers that attackers exploit and be conditioned to recognize when those triggers are being activated. Training should cover phishing recognition, verification procedures for sensitive requests, the right to question authority when security is at stake, and a clear, easy process for reporting suspicious contacts without fear of consequences. Verification procedures must be established for all requests involving financial transactions, access changes, or sensitive information sharing: verification means calling back on a known number or confirming through a separate channel, not simply calling back the number provided by the caller. MFA serves as a technical backstop when credentials are compromised through social engineering — even if a user provides their password, the attacker cannot complete authentication without the second factor. Financial approval workflows that require multiple authorizations for wire transfers and payment detail changes reduce the exposure to BEC and invoice scams. Reporting culture — an organizational environment where suspicious contacts are quickly reported and investigated — enables early detection of ongoing social engineering campaigns before they succeed.
