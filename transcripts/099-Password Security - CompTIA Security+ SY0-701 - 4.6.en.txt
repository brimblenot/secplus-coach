Passwords remain the most prevalent authentication mechanism across computing systems despite decades of discussion about their weaknesses. The persistence of passwords reflects the difficulty of deploying alternatives at scale rather than any inherent virtue of passwords as a security control. Understanding password security requires understanding both how to construct and enforce effective password policies and how password storage works at a technical level, since the security of stored passwords determines whether a database breach results in immediate account compromise or merely a temporary inconvenience while passwords are reset.

Password policies define the requirements that user-chosen passwords must meet. The guidance on password policy has evolved significantly as research has revealed that traditional requirements often produce counterproductive results. Length is the most important single characteristic of a password's security: longer passwords provide exponentially more entropy, making brute-force search computationally infeasible even with modern hardware. NIST Special Publication 800-63B recommends a minimum of eight characters for user-chosen passwords used with MFA, and a minimum of approximately eight characters for other user-chosen passwords, while noting that organizations should allow passwords up to at least 64 characters. More practically, organizations protecting sensitive systems should recommend 15 or more characters. Passphrases, which are sequences of multiple random words rather than a single complex word, achieve both length and memorability. A passphrase like "correct-horse-battery-staple" is significantly longer and more entropy-dense than a complex short password like "P@ssw0rd1!" while being far easier to remember.

Complexity requirements, which mandate mixtures of uppercase letters, lowercase letters, numbers, and special characters, are now recognized by NIST and most modern security guidance as producing negative security outcomes when applied rigidly. When users are forced to meet complexity requirements, they respond with predictable patterns: capitalizing the first letter, appending a number, and adding a special character at the end, producing passwords like Password1! that are trivially predictable once an attacker understands this pattern. NIST 800-63B specifically recommends against mandatory complexity requirements that force specific character types, preferring instead to require length and prohibit commonly used passwords. Prohibiting common and compromised passwords is one of the most effective policy controls available. Checking proposed passwords against known compromised password lists, such as the HaveIBeenPwned, or HIBP, database maintained by Troy Hunt, which contains billions of credentials exposed in historical data breaches, prevents users from choosing passwords that are already in attackers' wordlists regardless of how complex those passwords appear. NIST specifically recommends checking user-chosen passwords against breached password databases.

Password expiration policy is another area where traditional guidance has been substantially revised. The long-standing practice of forcing periodic password changes on a fixed schedule, such as every 90 days, is now recognized as producing more harm than good. When forced to change passwords regularly, users respond by making minimal changes such as incrementing a number at the end of their password, choosing a new password from the same small set they cycle through, or writing passwords down to remember them. NIST 800-63B recommends against mandatory periodic password expiration in the absence of evidence of compromise, while recommending immediate password changes when a password is known or suspected to have been compromised.

Account lockout policy is the mechanism that prevents online brute-force and password spraying attacks by limiting the number of consecutive failed authentication attempts. The lockout threshold defines how many consecutive failures are permitted before the account is locked, typically set between three and ten attempts. The observation window defines the time period within which failures are counted; a threshold of five failures within ten minutes is less aggressive than five failures ever. The lockout duration defines how long the account remains locked: automatic unlocking after a time period, commonly 15 to 30 minutes, allows legitimate users to regain access without administrator intervention, while requiring administrator unlocking provides stronger protection but creates operational overhead and potential denial-of-service risk if an attacker deliberately triggers lockouts. Password history controls prevent users from reusing recent passwords, typically storing the hashes of the last 10 to 24 passwords and rejecting new passwords that match any stored hash. A minimum password age requirement prevents users from immediately cycling through the history list by changing their password multiple times in rapid succession to reach a previously used password.

Password managers are the single most effective tool for improving the practical security of passwords in users' daily lives. The fundamental problem with password security is the cognitive limitation of human memory: people cannot remember long, complex, unique passwords for dozens or hundreds of accounts, so they resort to reusing the same few passwords across multiple sites. When one site is breached and passwords are exposed, those credentials are immediately tested against all other major sites in credential stuffing attacks. Password managers solve this problem by generating long, cryptographically random, unique passwords for every account and storing them in an encrypted vault that the user accesses with a single master password or biometric. The vault is protected by strong encryption, typically AES-256, so that even if the vault file is stolen, it cannot be read without the master password. Password managers also provide an important anti-phishing benefit: the autofill function detects the actual domain of the page where credentials are being entered and will not autofill credentials on a phishing site with a different domain, even if the page looks identical to the real site. Enterprise password managers including 1Password Teams, Bitwarden Business, Keeper Business, and LastPass Business provide centralized management, audit logging, and policy enforcement. KeePass is a popular open-source offline password manager for users who prefer not to trust a cloud service with their vault.

Password hashing and storage is the technical foundation of how passwords should be protected at the system level. The cardinal rule is that plaintext passwords must never be stored. A database of plaintext passwords is immediately and completely compromised if an attacker gains read access to the database. Instead, passwords should be processed through a cryptographic one-way function before storage. Simple cryptographic hash functions such as MD5 and SHA-1 are not appropriate for password storage because they are designed to be fast, which allows attackers to test billions of password guesses per second with GPU-based cracking tools. Rainbow table attacks precompute hash values for millions of common passwords and look up a hash in the table to instantly find the corresponding plaintext without any computation at login time. Salting defeats rainbow table attacks by adding a unique random value, the salt, to each user's password before hashing. The salt is stored alongside the hash, but because each user's salt is different, the same password produces a different hash for each user, and precomputed tables cannot be used. Even with salting, simple hash functions remain vulnerable to GPU-accelerated brute-force attacks because they are too fast.

Purpose-built password hashing algorithms are designed to be deliberately slow, making brute-force attacks computationally expensive even with powerful hardware. Bcrypt is a well-established password hashing function based on the Blowfish cipher that includes automatic salting and a configurable cost parameter, or work factor, that controls how many iterations of the algorithm are performed. Increasing the cost factor doubles the computation time per attempt, making brute force exponentially more expensive while adding only acceptable delay for legitimate authentication. Scrypt is a memory-hard password hashing function that requires large amounts of memory in addition to computational work, making GPU-based cracking less effective because GPUs have limited per-core memory. Argon2, the winner of the Password Hashing Competition in 2015, is the current recommended standard for new systems and comes in three variants: Argon2i, optimized for resistance to side-channel attacks; Argon2d, optimized for GPU resistance; and Argon2id, which combines the properties of both and is the recommended variant for general use. Argon2id supports independent configuration of iteration count, memory usage, and parallelism, allowing security teams to tune the function's performance to match their security requirements and available hardware.

Passwordless authentication represents the emerging direction for identity verification in high-assurance environments. FIDO2 passkeys, which store a cryptographic key pair on the user's device and authenticate using device biometrics or PIN, provide phishing-resistant authentication that completely eliminates the password from the authentication flow. Magic links, delivered via email, provide single-use authentication tokens that expire after a short time and allow login without a password, though they depend on the security of the user's email account. Biometric authentication on mobile devices and dedicated authenticators can replace passwords for device access, though they typically still rely on a PIN as a fallback.

Privileged account password management in enterprise environments uses PAM solutions that vault passwords for shared administrative accounts, rotate those passwords automatically after each use, and provide checked-out sessions where the administrator uses the privileged account through the PAM platform's session proxy without ever seeing the actual password. This ensures that no individual knows the current password to a privileged account and that password rotation after each use prevents reuse of harvested credentials.
