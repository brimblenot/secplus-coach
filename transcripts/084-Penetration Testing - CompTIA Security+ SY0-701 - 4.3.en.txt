Penetration testing is the authorized, simulated cyberattack conducted against an organization's systems, networks, and applications to identify security weaknesses before real attackers can exploit them. The fundamental distinction between penetration testing and vulnerability scanning is that vulnerability scanning identifies potential weaknesses without exploiting them, while penetration testing goes further by actually exploiting vulnerabilities to demonstrate their real-world impact — showing not just that a vulnerability exists but exactly what an attacker could accomplish by exploiting it. Penetration testing provides evidence-based risk assessment that helps organizations prioritize remediation efforts and justify security investments to management.

The written authorization is the most important preliminary requirement for any penetration test. Performing security testing against systems without explicit written authorization from the appropriate organizational authority is illegal under computer fraud and abuse laws regardless of intent. The authorization document should clearly identify who has authorized the testing, what systems are in scope, and the time window during which testing is permitted. Without this document, both the testing organization and the testers themselves face significant legal exposure.

Penetration testing is classified by the amount of prior knowledge testers are given about the target environment. Black box testing provides testers with no prior knowledge of the target systems, infrastructure, or applications — they are given only the name of the organization or a set of IP addresses and must discover everything else through their own reconnaissance. This approach most closely simulates an external attacker with no insider knowledge, making it the most realistic test of whether external threats can be detected and repelled. However, black box testing is the slowest and most expensive approach because testers must spend substantial time on reconnaissance before they can attempt exploitation. Gray box testing provides testers with partial knowledge — network diagrams, a non-privileged user account, or information about the technology stack — simulating a threat scenario where an attacker has already conducted some reconnaissance, a supplier or contractor has insider knowledge, or an employee account has been compromised. Gray box testing balances realism with efficiency by allowing testers to skip the most time-consuming discovery phases and focus on meaningful exploitation attempts within a defined engagement window. White box testing, also called crystal box testing, provides testers with complete information about the target environment including network diagrams, source code, architecture documentation, and administrative credentials. White box testing is the most thorough approach because testers can comprehensively evaluate all components of the environment including code that would never be reached through external testing. White box testing simulates a scenario where an attacker has had extended access to the environment or where an insider with full knowledge is the threat model.

The penetration test engagement follows a defined methodology with several distinct phases. The planning and reconnaissance phase establishes the scope, rules of engagement, and legal authorization before any technical activity begins. During this phase, passive reconnaissance gathers information about the target without interacting with target systems: WHOIS lookups reveal domain registration details and contact information, DNS queries enumerate subdomains and mail servers, searching certificate transparency logs reveals internal hostnames from TLS certificates, LinkedIn profiles identify employees and their technical roles, Shodan searches find internet-connected devices associated with the target organization, and job postings reveal technology choices and potential vulnerabilities. Active reconnaissance directly interacts with target systems to enumerate services: port scanning identifies open TCP and UDP ports, service enumeration identifies the software and version behind each open port, web application spidering discovers application pages and functionality, and vulnerability scanning identifies potential attack paths. The exploitation phase attempts to leverage identified vulnerabilities to gain unauthorized access. Successful exploitation demonstrates to the organization that a vulnerability is practically exploitable, not merely theoretical. The post-exploitation phase demonstrates the full impact of successful compromise: escalating privileges from limited initial access to administrative control, moving laterally from the initial compromise point to other systems on the network, accessing sensitive data that would be valuable to an attacker, and establishing persistence mechanisms that would survive remediation of the initial entry point. The reporting phase documents all findings with sufficient detail for both technical teams and organizational leadership.

Rules of Engagement is the critical pre-engagement document that formally defines the terms, scope, and boundaries of the penetration test. The Rules of Engagement specifies which systems and IP ranges are in scope for testing and which are explicitly excluded — systems that are fragile, critical to operations, or managed by third parties are often excluded to prevent unintended outages. Timing restrictions define when testing activity is permitted — some organizations restrict testing to off-hours to minimize the risk of disrupting business operations, while others prefer business-hours testing so that their security operations team can observe attacker behavior in real time. The notification section identifies key contacts who must be informed when testing begins, including the security operations center, system owners, and management, along with emergency contacts who can be reached if testing inadvertently causes a service disruption. Authorization is provided in writing by an appropriate organizational authority such as the Chief Information Security Officer, the system owner, or the board of directors. Limitations specify what types of testing are prohibited — some engagements exclude physical social engineering, denial of service testing, or exploitation of vulnerabilities in systems with production data. Evidence handling procedures specify how sensitive information discovered during testing, such as customer data or credentials, must be protected, stored, and destroyed after the engagement.

Social engineering is a penetration testing technique that tests the human element of security rather than technical systems. Phishing simulation campaigns send crafted email messages to employees to measure click rates on malicious links, attachment open rates, and credential submission rates on fake login pages. The results identify training needs and reveal which employee groups are most susceptible to phishing. Physical social engineering tests whether unauthorized individuals can gain physical access to restricted areas through tailgating, impersonation of employees, contractors, or delivery personnel, or manipulation of reception staff and employees into granting access. Vishing — voice phishing via telephone — tests whether employees can be manipulated into disclosing sensitive information or performing actions such as resetting credentials for an impersonator.

Passive reconnaissance uses publicly available information without sending any packets to target systems, making it completely undetectable. Active reconnaissance interacts with target systems, which can be detected by monitoring tools — scanners, firewalls, and IDS systems may log and alert on reconnaissance activity. In an engagement that tests the organization's detection capabilities, active reconnaissance provides valuable feedback about whether monitoring systems detect attacker reconnaissance activity.

The most commonly used penetration testing tools include several categories. For network scanning and reconnaissance, Nmap is the standard tool providing port scanning, service version detection, operating system fingerprinting, and scripted vulnerability checks through the Nmap Scripting Engine. For exploitation, the Metasploit Framework is the most comprehensive open-source exploit platform providing exploit modules, payload generation, and extensive post-exploitation capabilities through its Meterpreter agent. Burp Suite is the standard tool for web application penetration testing, providing an intercepting proxy, automated scanner, intruder for automated attack payloads, and repeater for manual request manipulation. For password attacks, Hashcat provides GPU-accelerated offline password cracking using dictionary, rules-based, and brute force modes. Hydra provides online password spraying against network services including SSH, FTP, HTTP, and SMB. Mimikatz is a Windows post-exploitation tool that extracts NTLM password hashes and plaintext credentials from the LSASS process memory and enables pass-the-hash and pass-the-ticket attacks. BloodHound maps Active Directory relationships to identify attack paths from a compromised user to domain administrator privileges. Cobalt Strike is a commercial command and control framework used extensively in red team engagements, providing advanced capabilities for post-exploitation, lateral movement, and long-term persistent access simulation.

The penetration test report is the deliverable that transforms technical findings into organizational value. The executive summary is written for organizational leadership without technical jargon: it describes the overall risk level, the most significant findings and their business impact, and recommended prioritization of remediation efforts. The technical findings section documents each vulnerability in detail: a description of the vulnerability, the CVE identifier where applicable, the systems affected, step-by-step reproduction instructions, evidence such as screenshots and command output demonstrating successful exploitation, and specific remediation guidance. Risk ratings using a Critical/High/Medium/Low scale based on likelihood of exploitation and potential business impact allow organizations to prioritize their remediation efforts. The report should be protected as a sensitive document because it effectively describes exactly how to attack the organization's systems.

Red team, blue team, and purple team exercises structure security testing around defined roles. The red team plays the role of attacker, conducting an extended adversarial simulation that may last weeks or months and is designed to simulate how a sophisticated persistent threat actor would operate against the organization. The blue team is the organization's defensive security operations team whose responsibility is to detect and respond to the red team's activities — the blue team may or may not know that a red team exercise is underway, depending on whether the engagement is testing detection capabilities or training response procedures. A purple team exercise brings red and blue teams together in a collaborative mode where the red team shares its techniques with the blue team in real time, allowing the blue team to develop and test detection rules and response playbooks against actual attack techniques rather than in isolation. The white team manages and administers the exercise, sets the rules of engagement, arbitrates disputes, and collects metrics on both offensive and defensive performance.

Bug bounty programs complement periodic penetration testing by providing continuous testing through external security researchers. Organizations running bug bounty programs define a scope of systems, applications, and vulnerability types that are eligible for bounty payment, and invite security researchers to test within that scope and report vulnerabilities through a responsible disclosure process. Researchers who discover qualifying vulnerabilities receive monetary rewards scaled to the severity of the finding. Platforms such as HackerOne, Bugcrowd, and Synack facilitate bug bounty programs by providing program management, researcher vetting, vulnerability tracking, and payment processing. Bug bounties are not a replacement for professional penetration testing but provide cost-effective coverage between formal engagements by leveraging the diverse skills and perspectives of many independent researchers.
