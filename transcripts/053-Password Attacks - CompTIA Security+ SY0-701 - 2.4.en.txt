Passwords remain the most widespread authentication mechanism in computing, despite decades of documented failures. Their dominance stems from simplicity: they require no hardware token, no biometric reader, and no special enrollment process. Their weakness stems from the same property: human beings choose passwords, and human password choices are predictable, reused, and frequently far shorter and less complex than policy requires. Password attacks exploit these tendencies systematically, and the category encompasses techniques ranging from automated online guessing to sophisticated offline cracking operations that process billions of candidate passwords per second.

The fundamental distinction in password attack methodology is between online attacks and offline attacks. An online attack submits guesses directly to a live authentication service, such as a web login form, SSH server, or Active Directory authentication endpoint. Online attacks are constrained by network latency, by account lockout policies that suspend accounts after a certain number of consecutive failures, and by CAPTCHA or rate limiting defenses. Attackers performing online attacks must make guesses slowly and carefully to avoid triggering these controls. An offline attack operates against a file or database of stolen hashed passwords. The attacker has already obtained the hash values and can now submit unlimited candidate passwords to a local hashing function, comparing results against the captured hashes, without any interaction with the target system. GPU-accelerated offline cracking tools such as Hashcat can process billions of hash values per second on consumer graphics hardware, making offline attacks orders of magnitude faster than online approaches and capable of cracking most poorly protected passwords within hours or days.

Brute force attacks systematically try every possible character combination for a given character set and length. The time required grows exponentially with password length and character set size. A six-character password using only lowercase letters has approximately 300 million possible values, cracked in under a second offline. An eight-character password using all printable ASCII characters has roughly 700 trillion possibilities, cracked in hours to days. A twelve-character random password using the full character set would take centuries with current hardware, illustrating why length is the most important password security property. Brute force is most practically applied when the attacker knows or suspects a maximum password length, when the character set is restricted by the application, or as a fallback after targeted dictionary and hybrid attacks have been exhausted.

Dictionary attacks leverage the observation that most passwords people actually choose are not random. They are words, names, dates, or phrases from natural language, often with predictable modifications applied. A dictionary attack uses a wordlist of common words, known passwords from past breaches, and application-specific terms, hashing each candidate and comparing it against the target hashes. The rockyou.txt wordlist, compiled from a 2009 breach that exposed over 14 million accounts in plaintext, contains the actual passwords that real users chose and is the baseline for most password cracking engagements. Additional wordlists are assembled from other breaches, crawls of publicly available text, and target-specific information such as company names, product names, and employee names gathered through open source intelligence.

Hybrid attacks combine dictionary words with rule-based transformations that reflect the modifications users make to simple words in an attempt to meet complexity requirements. Common rules include appending numbers to the end of a word, prepending exclamation points or other symbols, capitalizing the first letter, capitalizing the first and last letters, substituting common letter-to-symbol equivalences such as replacing the letter a with an at sign or the letter i with an exclamation mark, doubling the word, and combining two words. Hashcat's rule engine supports extremely complex transformations and the application of multiple rules in sequence, allowing a single base wordlist to generate hundreds of variations per word. Rule-based attacks are highly effective against the most common real-world password patterns because they model the same cognitive shortcuts that users apply when trying to satisfy complexity requirements with a memorable word.

Password spraying is specifically designed to evade account lockout policies, which are triggered by a high number of failed attempts against a single account. Rather than guessing many passwords against one account, password spraying guesses one or a few commonly used passwords against every account in the organization, then waits before the next round. Most lockout policies are based on a threshold of five to ten failures within a given time window, and spraying stays well below this threshold on every account. Common spraying candidates include seasonal patterns such as the current season and year with an exclamation mark, the organization's name with a number appended, generic corporate passwords such as Welcome1, Password1, and ChangeMe1, and known default passwords for the target's systems. Spraying attacks have been highly effective against large organizations using Microsoft Active Directory, where a significant percentage of users may share predictable password patterns. Detection requires monitoring for the pattern of low-frequency failures spread across many accounts rather than concentrated on few accounts, which is the inverse of the pattern that traditional brute-force detections look for.

Credential stuffing differs from traditional password guessing in that it does not guess at all. It replays username and password pairs verified to be correct at some other service, obtained from publicly available breach data or purchased on underground markets. If a user has the same password on a breached shopping site and their corporate email, credential stuffing will succeed at the corporate email without any need to guess. The scale of available breach data makes this attack broadly effective: billions of credential pairs from thousands of breaches are available to attackers. Have I Been Pwned and similar services allow users to check whether their email addresses appear in known breach datasets. Automated credential stuffing tools such as Sentry MBA and OpenBullet simulate browser behavior to defeat simple bot detection mechanisms. Multi-factor authentication is the most effective defense because the replayed credential pair alone is insufficient for access.

Rainbow table attacks precompute a lookup table mapping hash values to the passwords that generated them, enabling near-instantaneous lookup of any password in the table. The table is built offline at enormous computational cost, but subsequent lookups require only a table search. Rainbow tables are effective only against unsalted hashes because a salt value unique to each password account ensures that even identical passwords produce different hashes, making a single table inapplicable. The defense is ensuring that every stored password hash includes a unique random salt. Modern password storage standards using bcrypt, scrypt, or Argon2 automatically incorporate per-user salts and configurable work factors that make even offline cracking expensive by requiring substantial CPU and memory time to compute each candidate hash.

Hash dumping is the process of extracting stored password hashes from a target system. The Windows Security Accounts Manager database stores the NTLM hashes of local user accounts and can be extracted offline by booting to another operating system and accessing the raw registry hive files. The NTDS.dit file is the Active Directory database on domain controllers and contains the NTLM hashes and Kerberos keys for every user account in the domain. Extracting NTDS.dit requires domain administrator access but provides a complete set of hashes for all domain accounts. The LSASS process, the Local Security Authority Subsystem Service, stores credentials of recently authenticated users in memory to support single sign-on features. This cache can include NTLM hashes, Kerberos tickets, and in older Windows versions or certain configurations, plaintext credentials. The Mimikatz tool and its sekurlsa module read this cache directly from LSASS memory, a technique that has made hash extraction accessible to attackers who have achieved code execution on a system.

Defending against password attacks requires multiple complementary controls. Minimum password length requirements should mandate at least twelve characters. Password managers generate and store unique random passwords for every service, eliminating reuse and enabling truly complex passwords that users do not need to memorize. Modern password storage must use adaptive hashing algorithms such as bcrypt, scrypt, or Argon2 with a work factor configured to require at least 100 milliseconds to compute, making offline cracking extremely expensive. Multi-factor authentication is the most impactful single control because it ensures that compromised passwords alone are insufficient for access. Account lockout policies prevent unlimited online guessing, but their thresholds must be calibrated to balance security against denial-of-service risk, since an attacker can intentionally trigger lockouts. CAPTCHA and rate limiting control automated submissions against web login forms. SIEM detection rules that identify the pattern of low-frequency failures across many accounts catch password spraying campaigns. Windows Credential Guard prevents Mimikatz from reading NTLM hashes from LSASS memory by storing credentials in an isolated Hyper-V container. The Protected Users security group in Active Directory enforces restrictions including disabling NTLM authentication for members, limiting the window of exposure to pass-the-hash attacks. Breach credential monitoring services that alert when corporate email addresses appear in new breach datasets provide early warning of credential compromise outside the organization's own systems. Privileged access workstations for administrative accounts, regular access reviews, and not caching credentials on systems that face elevated risk are additional layers of a complete password security posture.
