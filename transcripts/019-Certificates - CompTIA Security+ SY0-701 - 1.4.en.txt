Digital certificates are the mechanism by which public key cryptography is applied to real-world identity and trust at internet scale. A digital certificate is a structured data file that contains a public key together with identity information about who that key belongs to, all authenticated by a Certificate Authority's digital signature. Without certificates, asymmetric cryptography has no reliable way to bind a public key to a specific person, organization, or server, leaving it vulnerable to man-in-the-middle attacks. Understanding certificate formats, types, validation levels, lifecycle management, and revocation mechanisms is essential for the Security Plus exam.

The international standard for digital certificate format is X.509, defined in ITU-T standard X.509 and widely used on the internet. When security professionals refer to an SSL certificate or a TLS certificate, they are referring to an X.509 certificate. The X.509 format defines a precise set of fields that must or may be present in every certificate. The Subject field identifies the entity the certificate belongs to, typically including a Common Name, which for server certificates is the domain name, as well as organizational fields like organization name, organizational unit, locality, state, and country. The Issuer field identifies the Certificate Authority that signed the certificate. The Serial Number is a unique identifier assigned by the issuing CA, used in Certificate Revocation Lists to identify specific revoked certificates. The Validity Period consists of a Not Before date and a Not After date, defining the window during which the certificate is considered valid. The Public Key field contains the actual certified public key along with the algorithm identifier. The Signature Algorithm field specifies which algorithm the CA used to create its signature. The Extensions section contains optional fields such as Subject Alternative Names, Key Usage, Extended Key Usage, and CRL Distribution Points. Finally, the CA's Digital Signature covers all of the above fields and allows anyone with the CA's public key to verify the certificate's authenticity.

Certificate validation levels define how thoroughly the CA verified the identity of the certificate applicant before issuing the certificate. Domain Validated certificates, DV, represent the lowest level of validation. The CA verifies only that the applicant controls the domain name listed in the certificate. This verification is automated and typically accomplished through one of three methods: sending an email to a standard administrative address for the domain, serving a specific token at a specific URL on the domain's web server, or placing a specific DNS record in the domain's DNS zone. DV certificates can be issued in minutes at low or no cost, which has enabled the widespread deployment of HTTPS across the web. Browsers display a padlock icon for DV-secured sites but do not display any organizational information in the certificate details. DV certificates are appropriate for any site that needs to encrypt traffic but where detailed organizational identity assurance is not required.

Organization Validated certificates, OV, require the CA to perform manual verification of the applicant's organizational identity, including confirming the organization's legal name, physical address, and the applicant's authorization to request a certificate on behalf of the organization. This process typically takes several days and involves reviewing business registration documents. OV certificates display the organization's verified name in the certificate details, providing a higher level of assurance to users who examine the certificate. OV certificates are appropriate for business websites, B2B portals, and any site where organizational identity is relevant.

Extended Validation certificates, EV, represent the highest level of identity assurance. EV certificates require the most rigorous vetting process, governed by the CA/Browser Forum's EV Guidelines, which specify detailed procedures for verifying the organization's legal existence, physical address, operational existence, and the identity and authority of the individual requesting the certificate. EV certificates traditionally triggered a distinctive visual indicator in browsers, including the organization's name displayed in the address bar. This green bar indicator has been removed from most modern browsers, but EV certificates still carry the full identity verification and are used by financial institutions, government agencies, and other high-assurance applications.

Wildcard certificates use an asterisk as a wildcard character in the left-most position of the domain name, such as the pattern star-dot-example-dot-com. A wildcard certificate for this pattern is valid for any immediate subdomain of example.com, including www.example.com, mail.example.com, api.example.com, ftp.example.com, and any other single-level subdomain. However, a wildcard certificate does NOT cover the apex domain itself, which in this case would be example.com without any subdomain prefix, unless the certificate explicitly lists the apex domain as a SAN. A wildcard certificate also does not cover deeper subdomain levels; star-dot-example-dot-com would not cover sub.subdomain.example.com. Wildcard certificates allow a single certificate to secure all services running under a domain without requiring separate certificates for each subdomain, which simplifies management and reduces cost. However, wildcard certificates also increase risk: if the private key for a wildcard certificate is compromised, all services under that domain are affected.

Subject Alternative Name certificates, also called SAN certificates or multi-domain certificates, allow a single certificate to list multiple fully qualified domain names, IP addresses, email addresses, and other identifiers in the Subject Alternative Name extension. Modern TLS implementations use the SAN extension for hostname verification rather than the Common Name field, which is now deprecated for this purpose. A SAN certificate might list dozens or hundreds of domain names, and it can include both wildcard entries and specific hostnames. Organizations like Google and Microsoft use SAN certificates to secure large numbers of different domain names with a single certificate. This approach is also used with automated certificate management to issue certificates covering all hostnames for a given application cluster.

Code signing certificates are issued to software developers and organizations to enable digital signing of executable files, scripts, and software packages. When a developer signs their software, the certificate binds the developer's identity to the signed code. When a user runs the signed software, the operating system verifies the signature using the developer's certificate. Windows displays the organization's name from the code signing certificate during installation rather than the generic Unknown Publisher warning. Code signing certificates must be issued to verified organizations and individuals, requiring identity verification by the CA. Kernel mode driver signing is mandatory on 64-bit Windows 10 and later, and drivers must be signed with EV code signing certificates submitted through the Microsoft Hardware Dev Center.

S/MIME certificates, where S/MIME stands for Secure Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions, are used to secure email communications. An S/MIME certificate binds a user's email address to their public key and is issued by a CA after verifying control of the email address and, for higher assurance levels, the user's identity. With an S/MIME certificate, email clients can digitally sign outgoing messages, providing the recipient with authentication and non-repudiation, and can encrypt outgoing messages using the recipient's public key, providing confidentiality. S/MIME is the standard for enterprise secure email and is supported by Microsoft Outlook, Apple Mail, and other major email clients.

Client certificates are issued to individual users or devices to authenticate them to servers, reversing the more familiar arrangement where only the server presents a certificate. Client certificate authentication is used in enterprise VPN deployments, mutual TLS configurations where both sides authenticate with certificates, and smart card or PIV card implementations where the private key is stored on hardware. Client certificates provide significantly stronger authentication than passwords because the private key never leaves the device and cannot be phished. When a browser presents a client certificate, the server can verify the user's identity without requiring any credential to be transmitted.

Server certificates, the most familiar type, authenticate servers to clients. When a browser connects to an HTTPS website, the server presents its certificate during the TLS handshake. The browser verifies that the certificate was signed by a trusted CA, that the domain name in the certificate matches the domain being connected to, and that the certificate is within its validity period. If all checks pass, the browser accepts the server's identity and proceeds with the encrypted session.

Root certificates are self-signed certificates issued by root Certificate Authorities. A self-signed certificate is one where the issuer and subject are the same entity and the certificate is signed by its own private key rather than by any higher authority. Root certificates cannot be verified by checking a higher CA's signature; they are trusted unconditionally because they are pre-installed in the trust store of operating systems and browsers by the OS vendor or browser developer. Root CAs are thus the ultimate anchors of trust in the web PKI. Because the compromise of a root CA's private key would allow issuance of fraudulent certificates for any domain, root CAs are kept offline and are used only to sign intermediate CA certificates.

Intermediate certificates occupy the middle of the certificate hierarchy between root CAs and end-entity certificates. Intermediate CAs sign the server certificates, code signing certificates, and other end-entity certificates used in practice. Using intermediate CAs instead of having the root CA sign end-entity certificates directly limits the exposure of the root CA's private key, because the root CA can remain offline. If an intermediate CA is compromised, its certificate can be revoked and a new intermediate CA can be issued without affecting the root CA or any other intermediate CAs.

The Certificate Signing Request, CSR, is the mechanism by which an applicant requests a certificate from a CA. To create a CSR, the applicant first generates a public and private key pair. The applicant then creates a structured data file containing their public key, the domain name or identity information to be certified, and other details such as organization name and country. The CSR is signed with the applicant's private key to prove that the requester possesses the private key corresponding to the public key in the CSR. The CSR is submitted to the CA, which validates the identity information according to its policies and then signs the CSR to create the finished certificate. Critically, the private key itself is never submitted to the CA; the CA's job is to certify the public key, not to generate or store the private key.

Certificate lifecycle management encompasses the full set of processes required to keep certificates operational and secure. The lifecycle begins with key pair generation using a cryptographically secure random number generator. A CSR is created and submitted to the CA. The CA performs identity verification and issues the certificate. The certificate and private key must be securely installed on the server or device. The organization must monitor certificate expiration dates and initiate renewal before certificates expire, because expired certificates cause connection errors that disrupt users and services. When certificates are no longer needed, such as when a server is decommissioned or a private key is compromised, the certificate must be revoked. Finally, private key material must be securely destroyed to prevent future misuse.

Certificate revocation allows a CA to declare that a certificate is no longer trustworthy before its natural expiration date. Reasons for revocation include compromise of the private key, change of organizational information, issuance error, or cessation of operation. The Certificate Revocation List, CRL, is a file published by the CA that lists the serial numbers of all certificates it has revoked, along with the reason for revocation and the date. Clients periodically download the CRL from a distribution point listed in the certificate's CRL Distribution Points extension and check whether the certificate they are validating appears in the list. The primary weakness of CRLs is timeliness: there is an inherent delay between when a certificate is revoked and when clients download the next CRL update. During this window, revoked certificates may still be accepted.

The Online Certificate Status Protocol, OCSP, provides a real-time alternative to CRL checking. Rather than downloading a complete list of revoked certificates, a client sends a query to the CA's OCSP responder containing the serial number of the specific certificate it wants to check. The OCSP responder returns a signed response indicating whether the certificate is good, revoked, or has an unknown status. Because the query is in real time, OCSP eliminates the latency problem of CRLs. However, standard OCSP has a privacy drawback: the CA's responder learns which websites its clients are visiting every time a browser checks a certificate's status.

OCSP stapling resolves this privacy concern and also improves performance. With OCSP stapling, the web server itself contacts the CA's OCSP responder periodically, typically every few hours, and obtains a signed OCSP response for its own certificate. The server caches this response and presents it to clients during the TLS handshake as an additional data item stapled to the handshake. The client receives the revocation status information directly from the server, so no connection to the CA's OCSP responder is required. Because the OCSP response is signed by the CA, clients can verify its authenticity independently of the server. OCSP stapling reduces CA infrastructure load, speeds up TLS handshakes by eliminating an extra network round trip, and eliminates the privacy concern of clients directly querying the CA.

Certificate pinning is a security technique in which an application hardcodes the expected certificate or public key for a specific server. When the application connects to the server, it compares the certificate or public key presented by the server against the hardcoded expectation. If they do not match, the connection is immediately rejected, regardless of whether the presented certificate is signed by a CA in the system trust store. Certificate pinning provides protection against man-in-the-middle attacks that use certificates issued by a compromised or coerced trusted CA. Mobile applications frequently implement certificate pinning to prevent interception by security analysis tools or by network attackers. The downside of pinning is operational: if a pinned certificate or key needs to be changed, the application must be updated with the new pin before the old certificate expires, or users will experience connection failures.

Certificate transparency, CT, is a system requiring all publicly trusted CAs to log every certificate they issue to publicly auditable, append-only log servers. Browsers increasingly require that certificates be logged in CT logs before they will be trusted. Domain owners can subscribe to CT log monitoring services to receive alerts whenever any CA issues a certificate for their domain. This allows rapid detection of unauthorized certificate issuance, such as a CA that has been compromised or coerced into issuing a certificate for a domain it has no legitimate relationship with. Certstream is a freely available service that provides a real-time stream of all newly logged CT entries. Security researchers monitor CT logs to track the issuance of certificates for domains associated with phishing campaigns, because attackers frequently obtain legitimate TLS certificates for convincingly named phishing domains.

Self-signed certificates are signed by the entity's own private key rather than by a recognized CA. Because no trusted third party has vouched for the certificate's subject, browsers and operating systems do not trust self-signed certificates by default and present prominent security warnings. Self-signed certificates are appropriate for internal testing, development environments, and lab configurations where trust can be established by manually installing the certificate in the trust store. They are not appropriate for any externally facing service because users will receive alarming warning messages that undermine security and user trust.

Organizations may choose between using a commercial public CA and running their own internal CA. A commercial public CA has its root certificate pre-installed in major OS and browser trust stores, so certificates it issues are automatically trusted by clients worldwide without any configuration. This is required for any externally accessible service. An internal CA is appropriate for internal services accessed only by organization members. The organization deploys its root CA certificate to all managed devices through Group Policy or mobile device management, after which devices trust certificates issued by the internal CA just as they trust commercial CAs. Internal CAs eliminate ongoing per-certificate fees and allow the organization to issue certificates with custom policies.

Certificate errors provide diagnostic information about what went wrong during certificate validation. A certificate expired error means the current date is past the certificate's Not After date, requiring certificate renewal. A certificate not trusted error means the certificate's issuer is not present in the client's trust store, which occurs with self-signed certificates, internal CA certificates on unmanaged devices, and certificates from CAs that have been removed from trust stores. A certificate name mismatch error means the domain name the client is connecting to does not match any name in the certificate's Subject or Subject Alternative Name fields, which can occur due to configuration errors or when connecting to a server by IP address instead of hostname. A certificate revoked error means the CA has declared the certificate invalid through a CRL or OCSP response, requiring immediate certificate replacement.
