A gap analysis is a structured evaluation process that compares an organization's current security posture to a defined target state and identifies the differences, called gaps, between the two. The purpose is to give organizations a clear, documented picture of where their security program falls short of a required or desired standard, and to provide a foundation for building a remediation roadmap. The concept is simple to state but complex and time-consuming to execute properly, typically requiring weeks or months and involving personnel across IT, operations, legal, compliance, and business leadership.

Organizations perform gap analyses for a variety of reasons. Regulatory compliance requirements such as HIPAA, PCI DSS, and CMMC mandate that organizations demonstrate they have implemented specific security controls, and a gap analysis is how they measure their current compliance posture. Organizations preparing for an external audit or certification examination use gap analysis to identify deficiencies before the auditors arrive, allowing time to remediate findings. When developing or maturing a security program, gap analysis provides a structured starting point by revealing which foundational controls are missing. After a security incident, gap analysis helps identify the control failures that allowed the breach to occur and guides post-incident improvement. Budget cycles also drive gap analysis, because security leaders must justify investment in security controls to executive management and boards of directors, and a gap analysis quantifies the current shortfall and the business risk it represents.

The gap analysis process follows a logical sequence of steps. The first step is to define the desired future state, which means selecting a target baseline or framework that represents what "good" security looks like for this organization. Frameworks and standards commonly used as gap analysis baselines include the NIST Cybersecurity Framework (CSF), NIST Special Publication 800-53 which provides a comprehensive catalog of security controls, NIST SP 800-171 which specifically addresses protection of Controlled Unclassified Information (CUI) in non-federal systems, ISO/IEC 27001 which is the international standard for information security management systems, PCI DSS for organizations that handle payment card data, and HIPAA Security Rule requirements for healthcare organizations. An organization might use an external standard, create its own custom baseline, or combine elements from multiple frameworks. The choice of target baseline should align with the organization's industry, regulatory environment, and risk tolerance.

The second step is to assess the current state. This involves gathering evidence about what security controls currently exist and how well they are operating. Assessment methods include structured interviews with IT staff, security personnel, system administrators, HR, and business unit leaders; documentation review of existing policies, procedures, system configurations, network diagrams, and audit logs; technical testing such as vulnerability scanning, penetration testing, and configuration reviews; and direct observation of operational procedures. The goal is to develop an accurate and objective picture of what is actually happening in the organization, not what policies say should be happening. The distinction between intended behavior and actual behavior is often where significant gaps are discovered.

When evaluating people as part of the gap analysis, the assessors look at the formal IT security experience and qualifications of the security team, what security training employees have completed, whether staff have demonstrated knowledge of security policies and procedures, and whether the organization has defined roles with clear security responsibilities. Even a technically strong security program can have significant gaps if the people responsible for implementing and operating it lack the knowledge or training to do so effectively.

When evaluating technology, the assessment identifies what technical controls are in place, whether they are properly configured and maintained, whether logging and monitoring is functioning, whether vulnerability management processes are operating, and whether the technical controls cover all systems including cloud environments, endpoints, network infrastructure, and operational technology.

When evaluating processes and policies, the assessment determines whether formal documented policies exist for all required security domains, whether those policies are current and approved by management, whether procedures describe how policies are implemented in practice, whether those procedures are consistently followed, and whether there are defined processes for incident response, change management, access provisioning and deprovisioning, and vendor risk management.

The third step is to identify the gaps, which means documenting each area where the current state falls short of the target baseline. Each gap should be described specifically enough that the organization understands exactly what is missing or insufficient. Vague findings like "security could be better" have no value. Specific findings like "the organization does not perform regular reviews of privileged user account access, which is required by NIST SP 800-53 control AC-2(7)" give the organization actionable information.

The fourth step is to prioritize the gaps by risk and business impact. Not all gaps are equally important. A gap in a control that prevents the most severe and likely threats should be remediated before a gap in a control addressing a low-probability, low-impact scenario. Risk prioritization considers the likelihood that a gap will be exploited, the potential business impact if it is exploited, the cost and complexity of remediation, and any regulatory deadlines that require specific controls to be in place by a certain date. Heat maps, color-coded by severity (red for critical gaps, yellow for moderate, green for minor), are a common visualization tool in gap analysis reports.

The fifth step is to develop a remediation roadmap that assigns ownership, timelines, resource requirements, and success metrics to each identified gap. The roadmap translates the gap analysis findings into an actionable plan that can be tracked and reported. This roadmap is the input to the organization's security program planning, budget requests, and executive reporting.

Technical gap analysis focuses on missing security controls such as absence of multi-factor authentication, unpatched software with known vulnerabilities, insecure configurations on systems and network devices, insufficient logging and monitoring, lack of encryption for sensitive data, and missing network segmentation. Policy and process gap analysis focuses on the absence of required policies, policies that exist but are not being followed, untrained staff, undefined incident response procedures, unclear security ownership, and inadequate vendor risk management. Both dimensions must be addressed because technical controls without supporting policies are likely to be misconfigured or bypassed, and policies without supporting technical controls cannot be reliably enforced.

Zero trust gap analysis is a specific application of gap analysis methodology to evaluate an organization's readiness to adopt a zero trust architecture. Zero trust requires eliminating implicit trust relationships within the network, implementing strong identity verification for every access request regardless of network location, enforcing microsegmentation, requiring device health verification, and applying continuous validation rather than trusting users and devices once they have authenticated. A zero trust gap analysis identifies where implicit trust currently exists (such as within a flat internal network where any device can reach any other device), which identity verification mechanisms are missing or insufficient, and what the path to implementing zero trust controls would look like.

Gap analysis is related to but distinct from risk assessment. A gap analysis asks "what controls do we have or lack compared to a defined standard?" A risk assessment asks "what is the likelihood and impact of specific threats exploiting specific vulnerabilities?" Gap analysis is typically performed against an external or internal standard, while risk assessment is typically performed against specific threat scenarios. In practice, gap analysis findings often feed directly into risk assessments, because identified gaps represent vulnerabilities that may be exploited by relevant threats.

Operational Technology environments, often called OT environments, present particular challenges for gap analysis. OT environments include industrial control systems (ICS), SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) systems, and other systems that control physical processes in manufacturing, utilities, and critical infrastructure. These systems often run on legacy operating systems that cannot be patched, use proprietary protocols that security tools do not support, and have availability requirements that make security scanning potentially disruptive. Frameworks like IEC 62443 are specifically designed for OT security gap analysis.

The final deliverable of a gap analysis is a report that presents the target baseline, the assessment findings, the identified gaps, the prioritized remediation roadmap, and the estimated resource requirements. This report is used to brief executive leadership and boards of directors, inform budget requests, satisfy auditor requirements, and serve as a reference document against which future progress can be measured. Repeating gap analysis on a regular cycle, such as annually, allows organizations to track improvement over time and identify new gaps introduced by changes in the threat landscape, regulatory requirements, or the organization's technology environment.
