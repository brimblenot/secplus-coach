An audit is a systematic, independent, and documented examination of controls, processes, systems, and evidence to determine whether they are effective and whether they meet applicable requirements. Audits provide the objective evidence that security programs require to demonstrate their effectiveness to management, customers, regulators, and other stakeholders. Many compliance frameworks mandate specific types of audits at defined frequencies, and the inability to produce clean audit results or to remediate findings promptly can have serious consequences for regulatory standing and business relationships. Security professionals need to understand the different types of audits and assessments, the evidence that supports audit findings, and the processes through which audits are conducted.

The distinction between internal and external audits is fundamental. An internal audit is conducted by the organization's own audit or security team. Internal audits are the most frequently conducted form of audit because they can be scheduled flexibly, scoped to specific areas of concern, and adjusted based on organizational needs. Internal audits identify control deficiencies and compliance gaps before they are discovered by external auditors, giving the organization the opportunity to remediate issues before they appear in formal audit reports shared with customers, regulators, or certification bodies. The main limitation of internal audits is the potential for lack of independence: when IT security personnel audit controls that they themselves implemented and operate, objectivity may be compromised. Organizations address this by having audit functions report to the audit committee of the board rather than to the CIO or CISO, creating organizational separation between the people implementing controls and the people assessing them.

An external audit is conducted by an independent third party who has no prior relationship with the organization that would compromise their objectivity. External audits are required for formal certifications such as ISO 27001, for compliance reports such as SOC 2, and for regulatory examinations such as PCI DSS qualified security assessor assessments and HIPAA audits. External audits carry greater credibility with stakeholders because the independence of the auditor provides assurance that the findings are not influenced by organizational politics or self-interest. External audits are more expensive than internal audits and require significant coordination with the audit firm to define scope, provide access to evidence, and schedule personnel for interviews and walkthroughs.

Different types of audits serve different purposes. A compliance audit verifies whether the organization adheres to the specific requirements of a regulation or standard. A PCI DSS qualified security assessor assessment determines whether the cardholder data environment meets all twelve PCI DSS requirements. A HIPAA audit determines whether administrative, physical, and technical safeguards for electronic protected health information are implemented appropriately. A SOX IT controls audit evaluates whether the information technology controls affecting financial reporting are designed appropriately and operating effectively. A security control audit evaluates the design and effectiveness of security controls against a recognized framework such as NIST 800-53 or ISO 27001. A financial audit conducted by a certified public accountant firm examines the accuracy of financial statements and includes evaluation of IT controls affecting financial data. An operational audit evaluates the efficiency and effectiveness of business processes including security processes.

Attestation is a formal statement by a qualified auditor or responsible management official affirming that something is true or that specific conditions exist. A SOC 2 Type 2 attestation report is issued by a CPA firm attesting that a service organization's security controls were suitably designed and operated effectively over a defined period. Management attestation is the formal declaration by senior executives of their responsibility for the effectiveness of internal controls, required by Sarbanes-Oxley for public company financial reporting. In compliance contexts, attestation creates legal accountability: an executive who attests to the accuracy of a compliance certification or financial report is personally accountable for the accuracy of that attestation.

Assessment types in the security context differ from formal audits in their scope and methodology. A vulnerability assessment uses automated scanning tools combined with manual analysis to identify and quantify vulnerabilities in systems, applications, and network configurations. Vulnerability assessments do not attempt to exploit vulnerabilities; they identify and categorize them based on technical characteristics and assign severity ratings based on scores such as CVSS. A risk assessment is a broader process that identifies threats and vulnerabilities, analyzes their likelihood and impact, and produces a prioritized picture of the organization's risk exposure. A security assessment evaluates the overall security posture of the organization through a combination of document review, interviews, observation, and technical testing. A threat assessment evaluates specific threats that the organization faces based on threat intelligence, industry data, and the organization's profile. A gap assessment compares the organization's current security controls and practices against a desired target state, typically defined by a compliance framework or security standard, to identify the specific areas where current capability falls short of requirements.

Regulatory examinations are a specific category of oversight that organizations in regulated industries must submit to. Banking organizations face periodic examination by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, the Federal Reserve, and state banking regulators. Securities firms face examination by the Securities and Exchange Commission. Insurance companies face examination by state insurance regulators. Healthcare organizations may face compliance reviews by the Office for Civil Rights within the Department of Health and Human Services. These examinations differ from voluntary third-party audits in that the regulator has legal authority to compel cooperation, access records, and impose sanctions for violations found.

The audit process follows a standard sequence. The planning phase defines the audit scope, objectives, methodology, timeline, and resource requirements. Scope definition is critical because it determines which systems, processes, locations, and controls will be included in the assessment and which will be excluded. The evidence collection phase gathers the information needed to evaluate whether controls meet requirements through multiple techniques. Documentation review examines written policies, procedures, standards, architecture diagrams, configuration baselines, and other documents that describe how controls should work. Inquiry or interviews with process owners, system administrators, security team members, and business users provide an understanding of how controls operate in practice. Observation involves watching processes being performed to verify that they match documented procedures. Inspection involves examining physical assets such as server rooms, badge readers, and workstation configurations, as well as reviewing system logs, configuration files, and access control lists. Re-performance involves the auditor independently performing a control to verify that it works as described. Analytical procedures compare metrics, look for anomalies, and analyze trends to identify areas requiring closer examination. The analysis phase evaluates all collected evidence against the defined audit criteria to determine whether controls are designed appropriately and operating effectively. The reporting phase documents findings with supporting evidence, assigns risk ratings to each finding, and provides specific recommendations for remediation. The follow-up phase verifies that remediation actions identified in the audit report have been completed effectively.

Continuous monitoring represents an evolution from periodic point-in-time audits to ongoing real-time assessment. Automated compliance scanning tools continuously evaluate system configurations against defined baselines and generate immediate alerts when noncompliant configurations are detected. Security information and event management platforms collect and correlate security events continuously, providing real-time visibility into security posture. Dashboards displaying key security performance indicators allow management to monitor security status between formal audits. This continuous monitoring approach identifies issues sooner, enables faster remediation, and produces a continuous body of evidence that can be provided to auditors rather than requiring intensive evidence collection in the days before a scheduled audit.

Bug bounty programs complement periodic formal assessments by providing continuous crowd-sourced security testing. Independent security researchers discover and responsibly disclose vulnerabilities to the organization in exchange for monetary rewards. Bug bounty programs provide an outside-in perspective that is difficult to replicate internally and create a continuous stream of security findings throughout the year. They are particularly effective at identifying vulnerabilities that automated scanning tools miss because researchers apply creative human analysis rather than pattern-matching against known vulnerability signatures.

The audit committee of the board of directors provides governance oversight of the audit function. In mature organizations, the audit committee reviews audit findings, requires management responses to findings, tracks remediation progress, and provides independent oversight of both the internal audit function and the engagement with external auditors. Board-level engagement with audit findings demonstrates governance maturity and provides assurance to investors, regulators, and customers that security is subject to independent oversight at the highest organizational level.
