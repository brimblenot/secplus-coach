Incident response is the organized approach to addressing and managing the aftermath of a security breach or cyberattack. It is not something that can be improvised in the moment: organizations that have not invested in planning, tooling, training, and practiced procedures before an incident occurs will consistently perform worse in their response than those that have prepared systematically. The fundamental premise of incident response is that security incidents will happen, that no organization is immune, and that the difference between a manageable event and a catastrophic breach often comes down to how quickly and effectively the response team can detect, contain, eradicate, and recover from the incident.

The terminology used in incident response must be understood precisely. An event is any observable occurrence in a system or network — a user logging in, a firewall denying a connection, a file being created. Events are routine and occur continuously at high volume. An alert is an event or group of events that a security tool has flagged as potentially significant, requiring human or automated review. Alerts may be true positives or false positives. An incident is a security event that has been confirmed as having a negative impact on the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of the organization's information or systems. The distinction matters because the full incident response process should be reserved for confirmed incidents, while investigation determines whether an alert represents a true incident.

The NIST Computer Security Incident Handling Guide, Special Publication 800-61, defines the standard lifecycle for incident response, commonly presented as the PICERL model covering Preparation, Identification, Containment, Eradication, Recovery, and Lessons Learned. Each phase has specific objectives and activities that collectively constitute a complete incident response process.

Preparation is the phase that occurs before any specific incident and is arguably the most important phase because its quality determines the effectiveness of all subsequent phases. During preparation, the organization develops its incident response plan and supporting procedures, trains the incident response team members in their roles and responsibilities, establishes the tools and technical capabilities required for investigation and response, configures logging and monitoring to ensure visibility into security-relevant events, creates playbooks for the most likely incident types, defines escalation paths and communication channels including out-of-band channels that do not rely on potentially compromised infrastructure, establishes relationships with external resources including forensic vendors and law enforcement contacts, and prepares an incident go-bag with the hardware and software tools the response team will need when an incident occurs, including forensic workstations, portable storage for evidence collection, write blockers, and licensed forensic software.

Identification and Detection is the phase in which the organization recognizes that an incident is occurring or has occurred. Detection may come from automated sources such as SIEM alerts, EDR detections, or intrusion prevention system notifications, from user reports of unusual system behavior, from threat intelligence matches to known indicators of compromise, from external notification by a partner organization or law enforcement, or from the attacker directly in the case of ransomware or extortion. Triage during this phase determines whether an alert represents a true positive incident or a false positive that requires no further action, assesses the initial scope and severity of the incident, and initiates the appropriate response procedures. Scope determination answers questions about which systems are affected, what data may have been accessed or exfiltrated, how long the attacker may have had access, and what the potential business impact is.

Containment is the phase in which the spread of the incident is limited and damage is minimized while evidence is preserved. Short-term containment focuses on immediate isolation of affected systems from the network to prevent further spread without powering off systems, because powering off a system destroys the volatile memory contents that may contain critical forensic evidence including running attacker tools, encryption keys, and network connection records. Long-term containment involves more systematic measures including patching the vulnerability that was exploited, hardening adjacent systems, implementing monitoring controls to detect if the attacker attempts reentry, and potentially isolating network segments. The containment strategy must balance the speed of limiting damage against the need to preserve evidence and avoid tipping off the attacker prematurely, which might cause them to accelerate their activities or destroy evidence.

Eradication is the phase in which the cause of the incident is removed from the environment. This includes removing all identified malware, eliminating every persistence mechanism the attacker established including backdoor accounts, scheduled tasks, registry run keys, and web shells, patching all exploited vulnerabilities, disabling or deleting accounts that were compromised or created by the attacker, and resetting credentials for all accounts the attacker may have accessed. Eradication must be thorough: incomplete eradication allows the attacker to reestablish access using a persistence mechanism that was not identified, leading to reinfection. Threat hunting during eradication is valuable to identify attacker activity on systems that were not initially identified as affected.

Recovery is the phase in which affected systems are restored to normal, verified-clean operation. Recovery activities include restoring systems from known-good backups that predate the incident, verifying that restored systems are free from compromise before returning them to service, rebuilding systems from scratch using hardened baseline configurations when backup integrity is uncertain, gradually restoring services in order of business priority, and monitoring restored systems closely for signs of continued or renewed attacker activity. Recovery must include verification that the root cause has been addressed so that the attacker cannot simply recompromise the environment using the same method immediately after recovery.

Lessons Learned, also called the post-incident review or after-action review, is the final phase and is often the most valuable for long-term security improvement. The lessons learned meeting should include all members of the incident response team and relevant stakeholders and should occur within days of incident resolution while events are fresh in participants' memories. The review examines the complete incident timeline, discusses what worked well and what did not, identifies gaps in detection and response that allowed the incident to progress further than it should have, evaluates whether the incident response plan and playbooks were adequate, and produces action items to improve detection, response procedures, tools, training, and security controls before the next incident occurs. Organizations that conduct thorough post-incident reviews and act on their findings measurably improve their security posture over time.

Incident severity classification allows the response team to allocate resources appropriately and set expectations for response timelines. Critical incidents involving active data breaches, ransomware encrypting production systems, confirmed exfiltration of regulated data, or any incident that is immediately reportable to regulators require immediate response with full resource mobilization and executive notification. High severity incidents involving suspected breaches, significant malware infections, or confirmed compromise of privileged accounts require urgent response within hours. Medium severity incidents including confirmed malware infections confined to a single endpoint or policy violations with potential security impact require response within 24 hours. Low severity incidents such as failed attack attempts or minor policy violations with no confirmed impact require response within days.

Communication during incidents requires careful management. The incident war room, whether physical or virtual, centralizes coordination among responders. Communication restrictions based on need-to-know prevent sensitive incident details from circulating broadly — broadcasting on a company-wide channel that a breach is in progress could tip off an insider threat or alert an attacker who retains access that their presence has been detected. Legal counsel should be involved early, particularly for incidents with potential regulatory reporting obligations, because attorney-client privilege can protect sensitive communications. Executive briefings provide non-technical summaries of impact, response status, and resource needs to senior leadership. Regulatory notification requirements, which vary by jurisdiction and industry, must be tracked carefully during an incident. The GDPR requires notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach. HIPAA allows up to 60 days for breach notification. The SEC's cybersecurity disclosure rules require public companies to report material cybersecurity incidents within four business days of determining materiality.

Playbooks provide pre-defined, tested, step-by-step response procedures for specific incident types, eliminating the need to make procedural decisions under the time pressure and stress of an active incident. A ransomware playbook defines the specific steps for isolating infected hosts, identifying patient zero, preserving forensic evidence, assessing backup integrity, engaging external IR assistance, and communicating with stakeholders. A phishing campaign playbook covers analyzing email headers to understand campaign scope, quarantining identified phishing messages from all mailboxes, identifying every recipient who received the campaign, identifying everyone who clicked or interacted with the phishing content, forcing password resets for affected accounts, and blocking associated indicators. A privilege escalation playbook addresses detection of accounts with newly acquired elevated privileges, investigation of how the escalation occurred, remediation of the exploited vulnerability, and verification that no backdoors were left behind.

Tabletop exercises practice incident response through discussion-based scenario walkthroughs that test decision-making, communication, and procedural knowledge without requiring activation of actual systems. The CSIRT, Computer Security Incident Response Team, consists of dedicated roles including the incident commander who coordinates the overall response, technical analysts who conduct the investigation, forensics specialists who preserve and analyze evidence, a communications coordinator who manages internal and external communications, and legal representation to manage privilege and regulatory obligations.
