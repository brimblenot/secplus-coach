Change management is the formal process by which organizations plan, review, approve, implement, and document modifications to their IT systems, applications, network infrastructure, and operational procedures. It is a foundational security and operational discipline because unauthorized, poorly planned, or improperly implemented changes are one of the leading causes of security incidents, system outages, and data loss. Without change management, any individual could modify a critical system at any time, making it impossible to maintain system stability, security configurations, or regulatory compliance. Change management provides a structured, controlled, and auditable process for every modification to the production environment.

The fundamental reason change management exists is the recognition that changes always carry risk. Changing a firewall rule might inadvertently open a port that exposes an internal service to the internet. Applying an operating system patch might break a critical application that depended on a specific behavior of the patched component. Modifying a database schema might corrupt existing records. Even well-intentioned, carefully planned changes sometimes have unintended consequences. Change management provides the structure to evaluate these risks before changes are made, test changes in controlled environments, schedule changes at times of minimal business impact, and document exactly what was changed and who authorized it.

The core change management process follows a consistent sequence regardless of the organization or specific methodology. The process begins when someone identifies a need for a change and submits a formal Request for Change, commonly called an RFC. The RFC documents the reason for the change, a description of what will be changed, which systems and services will be affected, who is requesting the change, and who is the designated owner responsible for the changed system. The RFC provides the Change Advisory Board with all the information they need to evaluate the request.

The Change Advisory Board, commonly abbreviated CAB, is the governance body responsible for reviewing, approving, rejecting, or conditionally approving change requests. The CAB typically includes representatives from IT operations, security, network engineering, application development, business units affected by the change, and senior management. The CAB evaluates each RFC by considering the technical impact on affected systems, the business impact on operations and services, the security impact including whether the change increases the attack surface or introduces new vulnerabilities, and whether the change has been adequately tested in a non-production environment. The CAB may approve the change as proposed, approve it with conditions or additional testing requirements, request additional information before making a decision, or reject the change because the risks outweigh the benefits.

Once a change is approved, it is scheduled for implementation during a defined maintenance window. Maintenance windows are scheduled time periods, typically during off-peak hours such as nights and weekends, when changes can be implemented with minimal disruption to users. The maintenance window must be communicated to stakeholders in advance so that affected users and support teams are prepared for potential service interruptions. After implementation, testing and verification confirm that the change was successfully applied and that existing functionality was not degraded. Finally, the change is formally documented and closed in the change management system, creating an audit record.

Change requests are categorized by their risk level and urgency, which determines what approval process they follow. Standard changes, sometimes called pre-approved changes, are routine, low-risk modifications for which a pre-approved template already exists because the change type has been performed many times and is well understood. Adding a standard user account, applying a known-good patch from an approved vendor, or replacing a failed disk in a RAID array might qualify as standard changes that can be performed without full CAB review, using a simplified pre-approved procedure. Normal changes are those that require full CAB review and approval before implementation. The majority of changes fall into this category: firewall rule modifications, application upgrades, network configuration changes, and server configuration modifications. Emergency changes are required when an urgent security threat or critical operational failure demands immediate action that cannot wait for the normal CAB review cycle. A zero-day vulnerability being actively exploited in the wild might require an emergency patch that must be applied within hours. Emergency change procedures typically involve expedited approval by a designated emergency change authority, such as the CISO and CTO, followed by retrospective CAB review after the emergency has been addressed.

Ownership is a critical concept in change management. Every system, application, and data set has an identified owner who is responsible for that resource. The owner may not be the person who performs the technical change, but they are responsible for ensuring that the change process is followed correctly, that the change is properly tested, and that the change achieves its intended purpose without unacceptable side effects. After a change is implemented, the owner is responsible for user acceptance testing to verify that everything is working as expected. In practice, the owner is usually the business unit manager or department head whose team uses the system, and the technical implementation is performed by the IT team on behalf of the owner.

Stakeholder identification is essential because the impact of a change often extends beyond the immediately obvious systems and people. A change to label printing software in the shipping department might seem to affect only shipping staff, but it may also affect the accounting team that uses shipping data for billing, the customer service team that tracks deliveries, and revenue recognition processes. Failing to identify all stakeholders means that some affected parties are not informed of the change, may not be prepared for service interruptions, and cannot provide important input about the acceptability of the planned change window.

Impact analysis is the CAB's evaluation of what effects the proposed change might have. Technical impact assessment considers which systems are directly modified, which systems might be indirectly affected through dependencies, and whether the change could degrade performance or introduce new failure modes. Business impact assessment considers how the change affects business operations, revenue, customer experience, and employee productivity. Security impact assessment considers whether the change opens new attack surface, removes existing security controls, introduces new network connections, changes access control configurations, or creates compliance gaps. Risk values for changes are often rated on a simple scale of high, medium, or low, with corresponding approval requirements and testing obligations.

Test results are a required component of a change request for significant changes. Before any change is implemented in production, it should be tested in a non-production environment that closely mirrors the production environment. This might be a dedicated test environment, a staging environment (also called pre-production), or a sandboxed environment. Testing should cover not only the primary function of the change but also the behavior of dependent systems, the performance impact under load, and the ability to roll back the change if problems are discovered.

The rollback plan is one of the most critical elements of a change request. A rollback plan, also called a backout plan, documents the specific steps required to undo the change and restore the system to its pre-change state if the change causes problems. Before any change is made to production, administrators should have a complete backup of the affected system so that the rollback option of last resort is always available. Some changes are easy to roll back: uninstalling a software patch and verifying that original files are restored. Other changes are difficult or impossible to fully reverse: database schema changes that have already processed new data, cryptographic key changes where the old keys have been invalidated, or network changes that have caused cascading configuration updates. The rollback plan must account for these complexities and define the exact sequence of steps, who is responsible for executing them, and how long the rollback procedure will take.

Maintenance windows define when changes can be implemented. Organizations typically establish recurring maintenance windows during low-traffic periods, communicate these windows organization-wide through calendars and notification systems, and require that scheduled changes be completed or rolled back within the window. Change freeze periods are blocks of time during which no non-emergency changes are permitted. Retail organizations commonly implement change freezes from Thanksgiving through the new year to protect the stability of systems during their peak revenue period. Financial institutions may freeze changes around fiscal year-end or during regulatory reporting periods.

Documentation requirements for change management serve multiple purposes. Complete documentation of every change creates an audit trail that demonstrates to regulators and auditors that changes are controlled and approved. Documentation provides the information needed to investigate incidents: if a system failure occurs, the change log shows what was modified and when, allowing investigators to correlate the failure with a recent change. Documentation ensures that institutional knowledge about the environment is preserved and not dependent on individual staff members' memories. Updated network diagrams, configuration files, runbooks, and system architecture documents should be produced as part of closing each change.

Unauthorized changes, including shadow IT projects where business units deploy systems or software without IT knowledge, represent a significant security risk. Unauthorized changes may not go through security review, may not be properly patched or configured, may introduce insecure software or services, and create compliance failures because they are not documented. Detecting unauthorized changes requires monitoring for unexpected system changes using file integrity monitoring (FIM), configuration management tools that audit system configurations against known-good baselines, and SIEM alerts for configuration changes outside approved windows.

The relationship between change management and configuration management is close. Configuration management maintains accurate records of the intended configuration of every system in the environment, typically in a Configuration Management Database (CMDB). Every approved change updates the CMDB, ensuring that the recorded configuration always reflects the actual production state. When a system's actual configuration drifts from its documented configuration, called configuration drift, the discrepancy is detected and investigated. Configuration drift can result from unauthorized changes, partial change implementations, or software behaviors that modify configurations outside the change process. Maintaining CMDB accuracy is essential for incident response and for ensuring that change impact analyses are performed against accurate system information.

Patch management is an essential application of change management. Vulnerability patches must be evaluated for security benefit and risk, tested in non-production environments, approved through the change management process, scheduled in appropriate maintenance windows, and deployed in a controlled manner with rollback capability. The change management process ensures that patches are applied in a controlled, documented, and reversible manner rather than being applied ad hoc across systems, which would create inconsistency, potential outages, and an inability to trace problems to their source.
