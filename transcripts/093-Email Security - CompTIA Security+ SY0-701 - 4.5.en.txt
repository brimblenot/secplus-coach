Email is the most heavily exploited attack vector in the modern threat landscape. Phishing campaigns, malware delivery, business email compromise, and ransomware all rely primarily on email as the initial access mechanism. Because email was designed decades ago with almost no security built into its core protocols, the defenses that exist today are layered additions to a fundamentally insecure foundation. Understanding email security requires understanding both how email authentication works to prevent spoofing and how enterprise email filtering, encryption, and behavioral controls work together to protect organizations and individuals from the full range of email-based threats.

The most pressing email security challenge is spoofing, where an attacker sends an email that appears to come from a legitimate, trusted source. The SMTP protocol does not inherently verify that the sending server is authorized to send mail on behalf of the claimed domain, which means an attacker can set any value they want in the sender field of an email. Three DNS-based standards work together to address spoofing: SPF, DKIM, and DMARC. Proper deployment of all three, with DMARC at a reject policy, represents the gold standard for outbound email authentication.

Sender Policy Framework, or SPF, is a DNS TXT record published by a domain owner that lists which mail servers are authorized to send email on behalf of that domain. The record syntax specifies authorized mechanisms such as specific IP addresses, IP ranges, and includes that delegate to other domains' SPF records. An example record might read "v=spf1 include:sendgrid.net ip4:203.0.113.0/24 -all", where the -all at the end is a hard fail directive meaning that any server not matching the listed mechanisms is unauthorized. When a receiving mail server accepts an inbound message, it checks the SPF record of the domain in the envelope sender address and compares it to the IP address of the connecting mail server. If the IP matches an authorized mechanism, SPF passes. If it does not match and the record ends with -all, SPF fails and the receiving server knows the message was sent from an unauthorized server. SPF effectively prevents attackers from using unauthorized mail servers to send email claiming to be from a domain, but it does not protect against attackers who have access to a legitimate mail server, and it cannot prevent spoofed display names where the visible name differs from the actual sending address.

DomainKeys Identified Mail, or DKIM, adds cryptographic signing to the email transmission process. The domain owner generates an asymmetric key pair. The private key is installed on the outgoing mail server, which uses it to sign each outgoing message by creating a cryptographic hash of specified email headers and the message body and encrypting that hash with the private key. The resulting signature is added to the email as a DKIM-Signature header. The corresponding public key is published in a DNS TXT record at a subdomain of the sending domain. When the receiving mail server accepts the message, it retrieves the public key from DNS and uses it to verify the DKIM signature. A valid signature proves two things: first, the email was signed by a server that has access to the private key, meaning an authorized server for that domain; second, the signed portions of the email were not modified in transit. DKIM therefore provides both authentication and integrity protection. Because the signature is part of the message itself rather than tied to the SMTP connection, DKIM survives email forwarding, unlike SPF, which can fail when mail is forwarded because the forwarding server's IP is not in the original domain's SPF record.

Domain-based Message Authentication Reporting and Conformance, or DMARC, builds on top of SPF and DKIM by providing a policy that specifies what the receiving server should do with messages that fail authentication checks. DMARC is also published as a DNS TXT record at the _dmarc subdomain of the sending domain. The policy has three options: none, which instructs receivers to take no action on failing messages but to send reports; quarantine, which instructs receivers to send failing messages to spam or junk folders rather than the inbox; and reject, which instructs receivers to reject failing messages outright so they are never delivered. DMARC also introduces the concept of alignment, requiring that the domain in the From header of the message align with the domain that passed SPF or DKIM authentication. This closes the gap where an attacker might set up SPF for their own domain but spoof the display From address to appear to be a different domain. DMARC reporting is a particularly valuable feature: aggregate reports, identified by the RUA tag in the DMARC record, are sent to the specified address regularly and show statistics on how many messages passed and failed SPF and DKIM for the sending domain. Forensic reports, identified by the RUF tag, provide details on individual messages that failed authentication, helping domain owners identify spoofing attempts and legitimate mail sources they may have missed in their SPF and DKIM configuration.

A Secure Email Gateway, or SEG, is the enterprise-level control point for email filtering. It may be a physical or virtual appliance deployed in the organization's mail flow path, or it may be a cloud-based service. Inbound mail flows through the SEG before reaching users' inboxes, allowing the gateway to apply multiple filtering and analysis techniques. Spam filtering uses a combination of reputation-based analysis, which evaluates the sending IP and domain against blocklists and threat intelligence feeds, and content analysis, which examines message content for characteristics associated with spam. Bayesian filtering is a statistical technique that learns from examples of spam and legitimate mail to improve accuracy over time. Malware scanning inspects all attachments for known malicious code using signature-based antivirus detection supplemented by behavioral sandboxing, where unknown or suspicious attachments are executed in an isolated virtual environment to observe their behavior. A malware attachment that exfiltrates data, downloads additional payloads, modifies system files, or attempts to encrypt files will be detected by the sandbox even if its signature is unknown. Phishing detection analyzes URL contents, checks for lookalike domains that mimic trusted brands by using visually similar characters or slight misspellings, and examines email headers for signs of spoofing. DLP, or data loss prevention, on outbound email monitors messages leaving the organization for sensitive data patterns such as credit card numbers, social security numbers, and classified document keywords, blocking or quarantining messages that violate policy.

Email sandboxing is a specific technique deserving detailed attention. When a suspicious attachment or URL arrives, rather than simply scanning it statically, the sandboxing system detonates it: the file is actually executed in a fully isolated cloud environment designed to look like a real endpoint, and the resulting behavior is observed over a period of two to five minutes. During this detonation, the sandbox monitors network connections to identify command-and-control callbacks, file system changes to identify malware dropping additional payloads, process creation to identify malicious child processes, and any registry modifications that indicate persistence. This behavioral analysis catches sophisticated malware that is designed to evade signature-based detection. The time required for detonation introduces a delay in mail delivery, which is an accepted tradeoff for the additional protection against unknown threats.

URL rewriting and time-of-click analysis address a specific attack technique where URLs are benign at the time of delivery but are weaponized shortly afterward, after the initial scans have passed and before the user clicks. The SEG rewrites all URLs in delivered email, replacing them with proxy URLs. When the user clicks a link, the request first goes to the gateway's proxy, which fetches the current content of the original URL in real time and analyzes it before deciding whether to allow the user through. If the URL has been changed to point to a phishing page or malware download since the email was delivered, the time-of-click analysis catches it where simple delivery-time scanning would not.

Email encryption addresses the confidentiality of message content, both in transit and at rest. TLS for transport encrypts email as it travels between mail servers using the STARTTLS mechanism. STARTTLS-based encryption is opportunistic: if both servers support it, the connection is encrypted; if not, mail may fall back to plaintext. This opportunistic model means that transport encryption is not guaranteed. End-to-end email encryption, in contrast, encrypts the message content itself so that only the intended recipient can decrypt it, regardless of what transport security is used and regardless of whether intermediate mail servers store plaintext copies. S/MIME, Secure/Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions, is the primary standard for enterprise end-to-end email security. S/MIME uses X.509 certificates, the same certificate infrastructure used by HTTPS. A sender signs their outgoing messages using their private key, proving the message's origin. To send an encrypted message, the sender encrypts it to the recipient's public key, so that only the recipient's private key can decrypt it. This requires that both parties have obtained each other's certificates, typically by exchanging signed messages first. Major enterprise email clients including Outlook and Apple Mail support S/MIME natively. PGP, Pretty Good Privacy, and its open-source implementation GPG, GNU Privacy Guard, offer an alternative end-to-end encryption model that uses a web of trust model rather than a certificate authority hierarchy. PGP is widely used in open-source and technical communities and can be integrated with email clients through plugins.

Email header analysis is a practical forensic skill. Every email contains headers that record the path the message took through the mail infrastructure. The Received headers show each server that handled the message in order, allowing the trace of the actual path from origin to destination. The X-Originating-IP header, when present, shows the IP address of the user who submitted the message. Authentication results headers show the outcome of SPF, DKIM, and DMARC checks performed by each receiving server. Analysis of these headers can reveal spoofing indicators, such as an SPF failure showing the message came from an unauthorized server, or a DKIM signature that does not validate, indicating possible modification in transit or a fraudulent signature.

Business Email Compromise, or BEC, is a sophisticated attack where attackers either compromise a legitimate email account or convincingly impersonate an executive to trick employees into authorizing wire transfers, changing payroll deposit information, or sharing sensitive data. BEC attacks often bypass traditional spam and malware filters because they contain no malicious links or attachments — they are simply convincingly written requests that appear to come from trusted parties. Defenses against BEC include requiring MFA on all email accounts to prevent credential-based account compromise, providing security awareness training that teaches employees to verify unusual financial requests through a secondary channel such as a phone call rather than by replying to the email, implementing financial approval workflows that require dual authorization for wire transfers above a threshold, monitoring for email forwarding rules that automatically copy or redirect mail to external addresses, and deploying anomaly detection that alerts on unusual access patterns to executive email accounts such as access from unexpected locations or at unusual times.
