Digital forensics is the application of scientific methods to the collection, preservation, analysis, and presentation of digital evidence in a manner that maintains its integrity and admissibility for legal proceedings. While digital forensics has its roots in law enforcement investigations, security practitioners use forensic techniques routinely in incident response to understand what happened, how an attacker gained access, what data was accessed or exfiltrated, and how to ensure complete eradication before recovery. The discipline requires both technical knowledge of how digital systems store and process information and adherence to strict procedural standards that preserve the evidentiary value of findings.

A legal hold, also called a litigation hold or evidence preservation order, is triggered when an organization reasonably anticipates litigation, regulatory investigation, or any proceeding that may require the production of electronic evidence. At the moment a legal hold is triggered, the organization has a legal obligation to preserve all electronically stored information, or ESI, that may be relevant to the anticipated proceeding. Failure to preserve relevant evidence, called spoliation, can have severe legal consequences including adverse inference instructions, sanctions, and dismissal of claims. Implementing a legal hold requires notifying custodians, who are the employees or former employees likely to have relevant information, through a formal legal hold notice instructing them to preserve all potentially relevant documents, emails, messages, and records and to cease any normal deletion activities that might destroy relevant information. The IT team must suspend automatic deletion, backup overwriting, and other data management processes for systems likely to contain relevant data and must ensure that data is not altered or deleted for the duration of the hold.

Chain of custody is the documented record of who had possession of evidence, when they had it, what actions they took with it, and to whom they transferred it. Every person who handles evidence must be documented. Evidence must be sealed, labeled, and stored in a manner that prevents unauthorized access, modification, or contamination. The chain of custody documentation, which travels with the evidence, must be complete and unbroken from the moment of collection through its presentation in any proceeding. A gap or irregularity in the chain of custody can render evidence inadmissible by raising questions about whether the evidence could have been tampered with while undocumented. In the physical world, evidence is sealed in tamper-evident bags that must be signed and dated by anyone who opens them. In the digital world, cryptographic hashing provides an equivalent mechanism: by calculating and recording hash values of digital evidence at the time of collection, any subsequent modification to the evidence will produce a different hash value, providing mathematical proof that tampering has occurred.

The order of volatility defines the sequence in which digital evidence must be collected, from the most volatile, meaning most quickly lost, to the least volatile. Collecting in this order ensures that the most fragile evidence is secured before it is lost. At the top of the volatility hierarchy are CPU registers and cache contents, which are lost instantly when the processor loses power. Second are the contents of RAM, the system's random access memory, which is lost when the system loses power. RAM is one of the most forensically valuable data sources: it may contain running processes including attacker tools that have never been written to disk, decrypted versions of encrypted files and communications, authentication credentials in memory, active network connections, and the commands recently executed by users or malware. Third in volatility is swap space or the paging file, which may contain memory pages that were swapped out of RAM to disk and could contain valuable fragments of memory contents. Fourth are disk contents, which are persistent and survive power loss but can be overwritten as the system continues to operate. Fifth is remote logging and monitoring data stored on external servers, which is relatively stable. Finally, physical configuration information such as network diagrams is the least volatile.

Forensic disk imaging is the process of creating an exact bit-for-bit copy of a storage medium for analysis, preserving the original while enabling investigation. A write blocker is a hardware or software device that intercepts all write commands sent to the evidence storage device, allowing the forensic investigator to read data from the device without any risk of modifying it. Hardware write blockers are preferred because they operate at a level below the OS and cannot be bypassed by software running on the forensic workstation. The dd command on Linux is a fundamental disk imaging tool that reads every block of the source device and writes it to the output file: dd if=/dev/sda of=/mnt/external/evidence.dd bs=512 conv=noerror,sync reads the source device block by block, continues past read errors with the noerror option, and pads failed read blocks with zeros using sync. dcfldd is an enhanced version of dd that adds built-in MD5 and SHA hashing during the imaging process and outputs progress information. FTK Imager is a widely used GUI forensic imaging tool for Windows that supports multiple output formats and performs hash verification during imaging. The E01 format, used by the EnCase forensic tool, is widely accepted in court proceedings and includes metadata, hash values, and case information alongside the actual disk image data.

Hash verification is performed immediately after creating a forensic image by calculating MD5 and SHA-256 hashes of both the original evidence and the forensic copy. If the hashes match, the copy is mathematically proven to be identical to the original. This verification serves as the foundation for the integrity claim that the evidence has not been modified. Any modification to even a single bit anywhere in the forensic image would produce a completely different hash value, making modification immediately detectable.

Memory forensics captures and analyzes the contents of a system's RAM to recover volatile evidence. Live memory capture must be performed on a running system using tools such as WinPmem, Magnet RAM Capture, or Belkasoft RAM Capturer, which write the memory contents to a file while the system remains running. This capture must happen before the system is powered off, since powering off destroys RAM contents. The Volatility Framework is the standard open-source tool for analyzing memory captures. Volatility plugins enable extraction of specific artifacts from a memory image: the pslist and pstree plugins enumerate running processes and their parent-child relationships, revealing attacker processes that may have been hidden from the task manager; netscan shows all network connections at the time of the capture, including connections to command-and-control infrastructure; dlllist shows the dynamic-link libraries loaded by each process, identifying malicious DLLs injected into legitimate processes; cmdscan and consoles recover command history from console processes, revealing commands executed by the attacker; hivelist enumerates registry hives loaded in memory; and malfind uses heuristics to identify code injected into legitimate processes.

File system forensics encompasses the recovery and analysis of data stored on disk. Deleted file recovery works because deletion in most file systems does not immediately overwrite the file data: instead, the file system marks the space used by the file as available for reuse, leaving the actual data intact until that space is needed. Tools like Autopsy with the Sleuth Kit and PhotoRec can scan the entire disk for file signatures and recover deleted files even when the directory entries that pointed to them have been overwritten. File system timeline analysis examines the metadata timestamps associated with each file — the Modified, Accessed, and Changed times, collectively called MAC times — to reconstruct the sequence of file system activity during and after an attacker's presence. Windows-specific forensic artifacts provide particularly rich evidence of user and attacker activity. The Windows Registry, stored in hive files including NTUSER.DAT for each user profile and SAM, SYSTEM, SOFTWARE, and SECURITY for the system, contains extensive historical information about recently opened files and programs in MRU lists, URLs typed into browsers, USB devices that have been connected, and program execution history in the UserAssist registry key. Prefetch files, with the .pf extension, are created by Windows when programs execute and contain the program name, run count, last run time, and file paths, providing evidence of program execution even if the program itself has since been deleted. Jump lists and LNK files provide additional evidence of recent file access and application usage.

The major forensic analysis tools used in professional practice include Autopsy with the Sleuth Kit, which is a free, open-source forensic platform that provides a graphical interface for disk image analysis, deleted file recovery, timeline analysis, keyword searching, and hash matching against the NIST National Software Reference Library database of known software hashes. FTK, the Forensic Toolkit from AccessData, now Exterro, is a widely used commercial forensic platform that is commonly used in law enforcement investigations. EnCase from OpenText, formerly Guidance Software, is another commercial forensic platform whose proprietary evidence file format is accepted in courts.

Attackers increasingly employ anti-forensics techniques to impede investigation. BleachBit and similar secure deletion tools overwrite data before deletion, preventing recovery. Timestomping modifies file access and modification timestamps to disguise when files were created or accessed. Log clearing, evidenced by Windows Event ID 1102 for Security log clearing and 1104 for other log clearing, removes the primary record of attacker activity. Encrypted volumes using BitLocker, VeraCrypt, or similar tools make disk forensics effectively impossible without the decryption key. Living-off-the-land attacks that use only built-in system tools and memory-resident techniques leave minimal file system evidence, requiring memory forensics and behavioral telemetry analysis to detect.

The e-discovery process is related to but distinct from forensics investigation. E-discovery is the process of collecting, preparing, reviewing, and producing electronically stored information in response to a legal proceeding or regulatory request. The scope of e-discovery is defined by the legal proceeding, and the organization's obligation is to collect and produce the specified data without altering it. E-discovery does not require forensic analysis; it requires complete and accurate collection. A forensic examiner may be engaged to perform the actual collection in a manner that preserves integrity, and the resulting images may subsequently be analyzed for content that is responsive to the e-discovery request.
