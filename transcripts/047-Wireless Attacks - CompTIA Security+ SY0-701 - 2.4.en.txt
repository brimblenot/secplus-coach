Wireless networks introduce a unique attack surface that wired networks do not share: the transmission medium itself is publicly accessible to anyone within radio range. An attacker sitting in a car outside a building, or even at a distance with a directional antenna, can interact with wireless network infrastructure without any physical access to the building. This fundamental property of radio-frequency communication makes wireless security a distinct discipline with its own set of attack techniques and defenses.

The evil twin attack creates a rogue wireless access point that advertises the same Service Set Identifier, or SSID, as a legitimate network. When users scan for available networks, they see two entries with the same name and may connect to the attacker's access point rather than the legitimate one, particularly if the attacker's signal is stronger. More sophisticated implementations copy the legitimate network's Basic Service Set Identifier as well, making the two networks effectively indistinguishable to the client. Once a user connects to the evil twin, the attacker can perform a man-in-the-middle attack on all of that user's traffic, capturing credentials, session tokens, and any unencrypted data. Attackers commonly combine the evil twin with a deauthentication attack to force clients off the legitimate access point and onto the rogue one. In an enterprise environment with a captive portal or a login page, the attacker can serve a convincing copy of that page to harvest credentials. Certificate-based authentication, as used in EAP-TLS with WPA2-Enterprise, is the most effective defense because the client verifies the access point's certificate before connecting, and a rogue AP cannot produce a valid certificate for the legitimate network.

The deauthentication attack exploits a fundamental weakness in the original 802.11 wireless standard. Deauthentication and disassociation frames are management frames that instruct a client to disconnect from an access point. In older versions of the 802.11 standard, these management frames were sent in the clear with no encryption or authentication. An attacker can craft a spoofed deauthentication frame using the MAC address of the legitimate access point as the source address and broadcast it to all clients, or unicast it to a specific target client. The targeted client or all clients on the network will then disconnect, and may repeatedly fail to reconnect as long as the attacker continues sending deauth frames. This serves as a denial of service attack against wireless connectivity and is used as a setup step for evil twin attacks, forcing clients to reconnect and potentially associate with the rogue access point. The IEEE 802.11w amendment introduced Management Frame Protection, which requires that sensitive management frames including deauthentication and disassociation be encrypted and authenticated. Networks using WPA2 or WPA3 with 802.11w enabled are protected against deauth flooding. However, certain management frames such as beacons, probes, and association frames must remain unencrypted even with 802.11w because encryption has not yet been established at that point in the connection process.

The WPA2 KRACK attack, disclosed in 2017, stands for Key Reinstallation Attack and demonstrated a critical vulnerability in the WPA2 four-way handshake that all WPA2 implementations shared, regardless of the specific cipher suite or vendor. The four-way handshake generates the session keys used to encrypt traffic between a client and an access point. KRACK exploited the fact that handshake messages can be retransmitted if expected confirmations are not received. By manipulating the retransmission, an attacker could force the client to reinstall an already-used cryptographic key while resetting associated parameters such as the nonce counter. Reusing a nonce in certain cipher modes, particularly AES-CCMP, violates the cryptographic security guarantees and allows an attacker to decrypt traffic, replay packets, and in some configurations inject data. KRACK was patched by operating system and firmware updates, but it illustrated that even a widely deployed and heavily reviewed protocol could contain implementation-level vulnerabilities of this severity. WPA3 was developed in part to address the architectural weaknesses that KRACK exposed.

WPA3 introduced the Simultaneous Authentication of Equals handshake, also called Dragonfly, which replaces the Pre-Shared Key exchange used in WPA2. SAE provides forward secrecy and is designed to be resistant to offline dictionary attacks even if the passphrase is captured. However, shortly after WPA3 was finalized, researchers disclosed the Dragonblood vulnerabilities, a set of timing side-channel and cache-based attacks against specific SAE implementations that could allow an attacker to perform offline dictionary attacks against the handshake. These were partially addressed through updates to the WPA3 specification and implementation guidance, illustrating that security standards require ongoing scrutiny even after formal adoption.

Wi-Fi Protected Setup, or WPS, was designed to simplify the process of connecting devices to a wireless network by allowing users to press a button or enter a PIN rather than type a long passphrase. The WPS PIN mode has a critical design flaw. The eight-digit PIN is validated by the access point in two separate halves. The first four digits are checked independently from the last four, and the last digit is a checksum. This means an attacker only needs to brute-force approximately 11,000 combinations rather than 100 million, making a successful attack feasible within hours using tools such as Reaver. A successful WPS PIN attack recovers the full WPA2 passphrase, giving the attacker access to the network. The Pixie Dust attack extends this further by exploiting weak random number generation in some access point implementations to recover the PIN in seconds without iterative brute force. The defense is simple and absolute: disable WPS entirely on any access point where it is not required.

Wireless eavesdropping captures and analyzes wireless traffic to extract sensitive information. The effectiveness of eavesdropping depends heavily on the security protocol in use. Wired Equivalent Privacy, or WEP, was the original 802.11 encryption protocol and is completely broken. WEP uses the RC4 stream cipher with a 24-bit Initialization Vector that is reused so frequently in high-traffic environments that statistical analysis of captured packets can recover the encryption key in minutes. Tools such as aircrack-ng automate this process and can crack a WEP key with as few as a few thousand captured packets. WEP should never be used and has been deprecated by all standards bodies. WPA2 using the CCMP cipher based on AES is substantially more resistant to passive eavesdropping, but an attacker who captures a WPA2 four-way handshake between a client and an access point can attempt to recover the passphrase offline using dictionary and brute-force attacks with tools such as hashcat. GPU-accelerated cracking makes short or common passphrases vulnerable to recovery.

War driving refers to the practice of driving through an area with a wireless adapter in monitor mode to discover and catalog wireless networks, recording details such as SSID, signal strength, encryption type, and GPS location. War chalking was the associated practice of marking physical locations with symbols indicating the presence and type of a nearby wireless network. While these terms originated with the early wireless security research community, the reconnaissance technique remains valuable for attackers seeking to discover target networks.

Rogue access points are unauthorized access points connected to a corporate network, typically installed by employees seeking more convenient wireless coverage or by attackers who have gained physical access to the facility. A rogue access point connected to the corporate network bypasses the security controls of the perimeter and provides unauthorized wireless access to any attacker within range. Wireless intrusion detection systems monitor the radio frequency environment for unauthorized access points by comparing detected BSSIDs against an inventory of authorized devices and alerting on anomalies.

Wireless jamming attacks transmit radio frequency interference at the frequencies used by the wireless network, disrupting communication by degrading the signal-to-noise ratio below the threshold required for reliable communication. Jamming can affect all devices attempting to communicate with an access point across an entire frequency band. It is illegal in most jurisdictions but has been used in targeted attacks. Physically locating a jammer requires triangulation using a directional antenna, a technique familiar to amateur radio operators who refer to locating interfering signals as a fox hunt.

Bluetooth attacks represent a distinct but related area of wireless security. Bluejacking sends unsolicited messages to nearby Bluetooth-enabled devices using the device discovery process. Bluesnarfing accesses a target device's data, such as contacts, messages, or calendar entries, through an unauthorized Bluetooth connection exploiting vulnerabilities in the Object Push Profile or similar protocols. Bluebugging gains full control over a Bluetooth-enabled device, allowing an attacker to make calls, send messages, and access data. The BlueBorne vulnerability set, disclosed in 2017, demonstrated that Bluetooth implementations in multiple operating systems contained critical vulnerabilities that could be exploited without pairing or user interaction. NFC relay attacks capture an NFC transaction at one location and replay it at another, allowing attackers to perform transactions using a victim's contactless payment card without having physical access to the card itself.

The strongest available defense for wireless networks is WPA3, which provides forward secrecy, stronger authentication through SAE, and protection against offline dictionary attacks. WPA2-Enterprise with 802.1X authentication and a RADIUS server provides per-user credentials rather than a shared passphrase, making credential compromise affect only one user rather than the entire network. EAP-TLS, which requires certificates on both the client and the server, prevents evil twin attacks because a rogue access point cannot present a valid server certificate. Disabling WPS eliminates the PIN brute-force attack surface entirely. Enabling 802.11w Management Frame Protection prevents deauthentication flooding. Deploying a wireless intrusion detection system provides continuous monitoring for rogue access points and attack traffic. Placing wireless networks on isolated VLANs with firewall controls between the wireless segment and internal networks limits the damage an attacker can do after successfully connecting. Conducting regular site surveys identifies unauthorized access points before attackers can exploit them, and using VPNs over wireless networks adds a layer of encryption that protects traffic even if the wireless security is compromised.
