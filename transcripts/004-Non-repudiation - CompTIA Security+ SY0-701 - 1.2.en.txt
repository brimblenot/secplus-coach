Non-repudiation is a security property that means a party cannot deny having performed a specific action. The term comes from the French word "repudier," meaning to reject or disown. In the context of information security, non-repudiation provides cryptographic or auditable proof that a specific entity sent a message, signed a document, executed a transaction, or performed some other action. This proof must be verifiable by a third party and must be strong enough that the originating party cannot credibly deny their involvement. Non-repudiation is one of the key properties that distinguish a truly secure communication system from one that merely provides privacy.

Think of non-repudiation in terms of a physical contract. When you sign a paper contract, your handwritten signature is a form of non-repudiation. Someone examining the contract later can recognize your signature and reasonably conclude that you reviewed and agreed to the contents. In digital environments, handwritten signatures cannot be applied, so cryptography provides equivalent functionality through digital signatures, certificate authorities, and supporting infrastructure.

Non-repudiation requires two distinct assurances working together. The first is proof of integrity, which guarantees that the data received is exactly the same data that was sent, with no modifications in transit. The second is proof of origin, which guarantees that the data was sent by the specific party who claims to have sent it, and that they cannot later deny having done so. Providing integrity alone, without authentication of the sender, is not sufficient for non-repudiation.

Hashing is the foundation of proof of integrity. A cryptographic hash function takes input data of any size and produces a fixed-length output called a hash value or message digest. SHA-256 (Secure Hash Algorithm with a 256-bit output) and SHA-3 are the current standard algorithms. Any change to the input data, no matter how small, produces a completely different hash value. This property is called the avalanche effect. By including a hash of the data alongside the data itself, a recipient can verify that the data arrived unmodified by computing the same hash and comparing. However, hashing alone does not identify who created the hash, which is why digital signatures are necessary for full non-repudiation.

Digital signatures are the primary cryptographic mechanism for achieving non-repudiation. A digital signature uses asymmetric key cryptography, which involves a mathematically linked key pair: a private key that is kept secret by its owner, and a public key that is freely distributed. The process works as follows. The sender first applies a cryptographic hash function to their message, producing a hash of the plaintext. The sender then encrypts that hash value using their own private key. The result is the digital signature. The sender transmits both the original plaintext message and the digital signature to the recipient.

When the recipient receives the message, they use the sender's public key to decrypt the digital signature, recovering the hash that the sender originally computed. The recipient then independently computes the hash of the received plaintext using the same algorithm. If the two hash values match, two things are confirmed simultaneously: the data has not been altered since it was signed, because any change would produce a different hash; and the message was signed by the holder of the private key corresponding to the public key used to verify it, because only the owner of the private key could have encrypted that hash in that way. Since the private key is secret and uniquely possessed by the sender, the sender cannot claim that someone else created the signature.

This distinction from HMAC (Hash-based Message Authentication Code) is important. HMAC also uses a shared secret key and a hash function to verify integrity, but because both parties share the same key, either party could have produced the MAC. This means HMAC provides integrity and authentication but not non-repudiation, because a third-party arbitrator cannot determine which of the two parties generated the code. Digital signatures, by contrast, rely on a private key that only one party possesses, so non-repudiation is cryptographically provable.

For digital signatures to provide meaningful non-repudiation, the identity of the key's owner must be certified by a trusted third party. This is the role of Certificate Authorities, or CAs, within a Public Key Infrastructure, or PKI. A Certificate Authority is an entity trusted by all parties to verify identities and issue digital certificates. A digital certificate is a document that binds a public key to an identified entity (such as a person, organization, or device) and is signed by the CA. When Alice presents her digital certificate, any party can verify that the CA has certified Alice's ownership of the associated public key. This chain of trust means that if Alice's digital signature on a message verifies correctly against the public key in her certificate, the CA's endorsement of that certificate establishes that Alice was the signer.

S/MIME, which stands for Secure/Multipurpose Internet Mail Extensions, is a widely used standard for applying digital signatures and encryption to email messages. When a sender signs an email with S/MIME, their email client hashes the message content, signs it with the sender's private key, and attaches the signature along with the sender's certificate to the outgoing message. The recipient's email client verifies the signature automatically, confirming both integrity and the sender's identity. S/MIME email signing is one of the most common practical implementations of non-repudiation in enterprise environments.

ECDSA (Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm) is a widely used digital signature algorithm that provides the same security strength as RSA but with much shorter key lengths, making it computationally more efficient. ECDSA is used in TLS (Transport Layer Security) connections, in code signing, and in many modern cryptographic protocols. The mathematical difficulty of the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem is what makes ECDSA signatures computationally infeasible to forge.

Timestamps play an important supporting role in non-repudiation. A digital signature proves who signed a document but does not by itself prove when. Timestamping services, sometimes called Time Stamping Authorities or TSAs, apply a cryptographically signed timestamp to a document alongside the digital signature, providing verifiable proof of when the signature was created. This is important in legal contexts where the timing of an action may be in dispute. For example, a contract signed after a filing deadline would not be enforceable, and without a trusted timestamp the signing time could be disputed.

Audit logs are a supporting non-repudiation mechanism at the system level. When systems record detailed logs of user actions, including login events, file access, database modifications, and administrative changes, these logs create a record that can be used to establish what occurred and when. Audit logs must be protected against tampering, typically through append-only log storage, cryptographic log signing, or shipping logs to an immutable SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) system in real time. If an attacker can delete or modify audit logs, the non-repudiation value of those logs is destroyed.

Non-repudiation has two distinct components when applied to a communication: proof of origin and proof of delivery. Proof of origin means the sender cannot deny having sent the message. Proof of delivery means the recipient cannot deny having received it. These are addressed separately in some systems. Email delivery receipts and read receipts provide weak proof of delivery but can be disabled by the recipient's email client. More robust proof of delivery requires server-side logging and cryptographic acknowledgments.

In regulated industries, non-repudiation is not just a technical nicety but a legal and compliance requirement. In healthcare, the HIPAA Security Rule requires organizations to implement controls to record and examine activity in systems that contain protected health information. In financial services, regulations such as SEC Rule 17a-4 and FINRA rules require firms to preserve electronic records in a non-alterable format. In these contexts, the inability to prove who performed an action, or to prove that a record was not tampered with, can result in regulatory penalties, inability to defend against lawsuits, or failure of forensic investigations.

Blockchain technology provides a form of distributed non-repudiation for transaction records. Each transaction in a blockchain is digitally signed by the initiating party, hashed together with previous transactions into a block, and then distributed across a network of nodes that each maintain an identical copy. The distributed, immutable nature of the ledger means that a recorded transaction cannot be altered or deleted without the alteration being detected, and the digital signature on each transaction provides proof of origin. This is why blockchain is used in cryptocurrency systems, supply chain tracking, and other applications where tamper-evident transaction records are essential. Non-repudiation, when properly implemented through digital signatures, PKI, timestamps, and audit logs, creates the trustworthy evidence trail that organizations need to enforce accountability, resolve disputes, and satisfy legal and regulatory requirements.
