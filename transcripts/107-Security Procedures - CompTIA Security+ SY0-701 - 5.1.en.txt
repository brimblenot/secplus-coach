Security procedures are the operational backbone of a security program. They are step-by-step documented instructions that tell security professionals and end users exactly how to perform specific security-related tasks. The distinction between policies, standards, and procedures is conceptually important for the Security+ exam. Policies express management intent and answer the question of what must be accomplished and why. Standards specify measurable requirements and answer the question of what precisely must be used or configured. Procedures answer the question of how a specific task is actually performed, step by step. Procedures are critical for consistency because they ensure that every person performing a task follows the same sequence of actions and produces a predictable, secure outcome. They are also essential for training new staff, because a well-written procedure allows a new employee to perform a task correctly even before developing deep institutional knowledge. Finally, procedures are essential for audit evidence because an auditor verifying compliance needs to see not only that a policy exists but that it is operationalized through documented, consistently followed procedures.

Change management procedures are among the most important operational security procedures in any organization. The change management procedure details the steps required to submit, review, approve, implement, and document a change to any system, application, network device, or configuration. A typical change management procedure begins with the submission of a Request for Change form that captures the description of the proposed change, the business justification, the systems affected, the implementation timeline, the resources required, the risk assessment, the testing plan, and the rollback plan. The submitted RFC is then reviewed by a Change Advisory Board, which is a committee of technical and business stakeholders who evaluate whether the change is appropriate, assess whether the risk is acceptable, confirm that testing has been planned, and verify that a viable rollback procedure exists in case the change causes unexpected problems. After CAB approval, the change is scheduled for implementation, typically during a defined maintenance window designed to minimize impact on operations. Following implementation, the procedure requires verification that the change achieved its intended effect and that no adverse impacts occurred. All changes must be documented in a change management system so that the historical record of every modification to the environment is preserved and auditable.

Onboarding procedures define the sequence of steps required when a new employee joins the organization or an existing employee transfers to a new role. The procedure typically begins when HR notifies IT of the new hire's start date, role, and department. This notification triggers account creation in Active Directory or Azure Active Directory, provisioning of an organizational email address, and assignment of application access based on the employee's role. The principle of least privilege governs access provisioning, meaning the new hire receives access only to the systems and data required to perform their specific job function. Equipment deployment follows a defined sequence including assigning a device name that conforms to the organization's naming convention, enrolling the device in the mobile device management platform, enabling BitLocker or other disk encryption, installing required software applications, deploying the endpoint protection agent, and confirming successful connectivity to all required systems. New hires must complete security awareness training and formally acknowledge the acceptable use policy before being granted access to sensitive systems.

Offboarding and termination procedures are among the most security-critical procedures in the organization because the failure to promptly and completely remove access from departing employees creates significant risk. For voluntary separations, the offboarding procedure is typically initiated on or before the employee's last day. For involuntary separations, access revocation must occur immediately, often before or at the moment the employee is informed of the termination. The procedure includes disabling the Active Directory account, revoking multifactor authentication tokens and app-specific passwords, invalidating API keys and OAuth tokens, removing access from all business applications including cloud services, collecting physical assets including the laptop, badge, hardware tokens, and keys, disabling VPN access, disabling cloud account access, and preserving the employee's work email and files before any deletion occurs. A legal hold assessment must be performed to determine whether the employee's data must be preserved because of actual or anticipated litigation. If litigation is anticipated, all data associated with the employee must be placed on legal hold and preserved without modification until legal proceedings are resolved. The exit interview typically includes a security component where the departing employee is reminded of their continuing confidentiality obligations under any non-disclosure agreements they signed.

Patch management procedures provide the step-by-step process for identifying, evaluating, testing, approving, deploying, and verifying patches for all systems in the organization's environment. The procedure begins with identifying available patches through a combination of automated vulnerability scanning tools, vendor security advisories, subscription to vendor mailing lists, and output from vulnerability management platforms. Patches are triaged and prioritized based on the CVSS severity score of the associated vulnerability, the exploitability of the vulnerability in the wild, the criticality of the affected systems, and any known active exploitation reported by threat intelligence sources. Critical and high-severity patches addressing actively exploited vulnerabilities receive expedited handling while lower-severity patches follow the standard schedule. Before deployment to production, patches are tested in a representative test environment that mirrors production configurations to identify compatibility issues or regressions. Patches are submitted through the change management process before production deployment. Automated deployment tools such as Windows Server Update Services, Microsoft Endpoint Configuration Manager, or configuration management platforms like Ansible are used to push approved patches to target systems during scheduled maintenance windows. After deployment, verification is performed through automated rescanning to confirm that the patched vulnerability no longer appears as open and through compliance reporting against the patch baseline. Any patches that cannot be applied due to compatibility issues, vendor support restrictions, or operational requirements must be formally documented as exceptions with an explanation of the business reason, an assessment of the residual risk, and identification of any compensating controls.

Password reset procedures define the exact steps for verifying a user's identity before resetting their account credentials. This procedure is a critical control against social engineering attacks in which an attacker impersonates a legitimate user and convinces the help desk to reset the account password, effectively handing control of the account to the attacker. The procedure requires verification of the user's identity through one or more independent methods before any credential change is made. Acceptable verification methods include calling the user back on a phone number already registered in the HR or IT system, requiring the user's manager to confirm the request, or requiring in-person identity verification with a government-issued photo ID. Once identity is confirmed, a temporary password meeting all policy complexity requirements is created, and the account is flagged to require a password change on next login. Every password reset event is documented in the ticketing system with a record of who requested the reset, who performed it, the verification method used, and the timestamp.

Backup and recovery procedures define how backups are performed, how their integrity is verified, how recovery is tested, and under what circumstances recovery may be initiated. The backup procedure specifies the backup type for each system, the schedule, the retention period, the destination storage, and the encryption requirements for backup data. Verification steps include automated integrity checks of backup completeness and periodic restore testing where backup data is actually restored to a test environment and verified to be complete and functional. The recovery procedure specifies who has authority to declare a disaster and initiate recovery operations, the sequence in which systems are restored based on business criticality, and the communication responsibilities during a recovery event.

Vulnerability management procedures define the schedule for vulnerability scanning, the remediation service level agreements for each severity level, and the exception handling process. Typical remediation SLAs require critical vulnerabilities to be remediated within fifteen days of discovery, high-severity vulnerabilities within thirty days, medium-severity within ninety days, and low-severity within one hundred eighty days. Systems that cannot be remediated within these timeframes require formal exception requests documenting the business reason and compensating controls.

Access review procedures define the periodic process for certifying that all current access rights remain appropriate and removing access that is no longer needed. Quarterly access certification reviews require system owners or managers to review lists of users with access to their systems and confirm that each user's access level is still appropriate for their current role. Users whose access cannot be confirmed as appropriate are revoked promptly. Physical security procedures cover visitor management including sign-in requirements and mandatory escort policies, the badge issuance and replacement process, periodic clean desk inspection checklists, and the procedure for reporting physical security incidents such as tailgating or the discovery of unauthorized individuals in restricted areas.
