Network segmentation is the practice of dividing a network into smaller, isolated portions to limit the scope of a security incident, control traffic flows between different trust zones, and comply with regulatory requirements that mandate separation of sensitive data. The core security principle behind segmentation is containment: if an attacker compromises a host in one segment, the network architecture itself should prevent that attacker from freely accessing hosts in other segments. Segmentation does not prevent initial compromise, but it dramatically limits the damage an attacker can do after gaining a foothold and makes lateral movement visible and more difficult. The appropriate granularity of segmentation depends on the sensitivity of the data and systems involved and the regulatory environment.

Physical segmentation creates genuinely separate networks by using distinct physical infrastructure for each segment. Different cables, switches, and physical ports separate the segments, and traffic can only flow between them through explicitly configured inter-segment connections such as a firewall or router. Physical segmentation is the most complete form of isolation but is also the most expensive because it requires dedicated hardware for each segment. It is appropriate for the highest-sensitivity environments, such as networks handling classified information or industrial control systems that must not be connected to corporate IT infrastructure.

Virtual Local Area Networks, or VLANs, provide logical segmentation at Layer 2 of the OSI model without requiring separate physical hardware for each segment. Managed switches support VLANs by assigning each switch port to one or more VLANs, creating separate broadcast domains within the shared physical infrastructure. Traffic within a VLAN flows freely, but traffic between VLANs must traverse a Layer 3 router or firewall, which can enforce access control policies. VLANs are the most common segmentation mechanism in enterprise networks because they are cost-effective, flexible, and manageable through centralized switch configuration. VLAN hopping attacks attempt to bypass VLAN boundaries without traversing a router. The double-tagging attack crafts frames with two 802.1Q VLAN tags; the outer tag is stripped by the first switch, and the inner tag causes the frame to be forwarded to the target VLAN on the second switch. The native VLAN attack exploits the fact that traffic on the native VLAN, which is VLAN 1 by default, is not tagged on trunk links, potentially allowing an attacker on the native VLAN to reach other VLANs through specific trunk configurations. Defenses against VLAN hopping include changing the native VLAN from the default VLAN 1 to an unused VLAN, disabling Dynamic Trunking Protocol on access ports, and using VLAN ACLs to control intra-VLAN and inter-VLAN traffic.

The Demilitarized Zone, or DMZ, is a classic network architecture pattern that places public-facing servers in an intermediate network segment between the untrusted internet and the trusted internal network. Web servers, email servers, and public DNS servers that must accept connections from any internet user are placed in the DMZ. Firewall rules allow inbound connections from the internet to DMZ servers on specific ports but restrict outbound connections from DMZ servers to the internal network, limiting the exposure if a public-facing server is compromised. A dual-firewall DMZ architecture uses two separate firewalls: one between the internet and the DMZ, and another between the DMZ and the internal network. This means an attacker who compromises a public-facing server must additionally compromise a second firewall before reaching internal resources, providing substantially better isolation than a single-firewall design.

Microsegmentation takes the concept of network segmentation to its logical extreme, isolating individual workloads, applications, or even processes from each other rather than creating broad segments for groups of hosts. In traditional network segmentation, all servers in a data center segment can communicate freely with each other, creating east-west traffic paths that an attacker can exploit for lateral movement after compromising a single host. Microsegmentation applies access controls at the workload level, allowing each server or container to communicate only with the specific other workloads it has a legitimate reason to reach. This dramatically reduces the blast radius of a compromise. Microsegmentation is typically implemented in software-defined networking environments and cloud platforms where virtual network policies can be applied with the same granularity as individual virtual machine or container identities. Zero trust architectures depend on microsegmentation principles because they eliminate the concept of an implicitly trusted internal network.

East-west traffic flows between servers and services within the data center, while north-south traffic flows between the data center and external networks such as the internet or remote users. Traditional perimeter security focused almost entirely on north-south traffic, leaving east-west traffic largely uncontrolled. As attackers have increasingly relied on lateral movement through east-west paths to reach high-value targets after an initial compromise of a less-sensitive host, internal segmentation and east-west traffic inspection have become essential components of data center security architecture.

An air gap is the ultimate form of physical segmentation: a system or network that has absolutely no electronic connection to any external network. Air-gapped systems are used for the most sensitive information and critical control systems, including nuclear facility management systems, classified national security networks, and some industrial control systems for critical infrastructure. Data transfer to and from air-gapped systems is accomplished only through approved physical media following strict procedures. The Stuxnet worm demonstrated that even air-gapped networks are not invulnerable: the malware spread into the Iranian nuclear facility's air-gapped network through infected USB drives, illustrating that air gaps protect against network-based attacks but not against supply chain compromise or insider threats involving physical media.

Jump servers, also called bastion hosts, are hardened systems that provide a single, closely controlled access point into an otherwise isolated network segment such as a production data center or an operational technology network. Administrators who need to manage servers in the protected segment must first authenticate to the jump server, which logs all sessions and provides a centralized audit trail. All administrative traffic enters the protected segment through this controlled chokepoint rather than being allowed directly from any administrative workstation. Jump servers are configured minimally with only the tools required for their function and are subject to heightened patching and monitoring.

Access control models define the rules by which subjects, typically users or processes, are granted or denied access to objects such as files, databases, and systems. The model in use determines who makes access decisions, how those decisions are made, and what attributes factor into them.

Mandatory Access Control, or MAC, is a model in which the operating system or security kernel enforces access policy based on classification labels assigned to both subjects and objects. A user with a Secret clearance cannot access Top Secret data regardless of any other circumstances, because the system enforces the label hierarchy absolutely. Users have no discretion to share their access with others. MAC is the model underlying government and military classification systems and is implemented in operating systems such as SELinux and Trusted Solaris.

Discretionary Access Control, or DAC, allows the owner of a resource to decide who else may access it. The Windows NTFS permission system and the standard Unix file permission model are both DAC implementations. A user who owns a file can grant or deny read, write, or execute permissions to other users at their discretion. DAC is the most common model in commercial systems and provides flexibility but also means that a compromised account can share access to its data with attacker-controlled accounts.

Role-Based Access Control, or RBAC, grants access based on a user's organizational role rather than their individual identity. Access rights are assigned to roles such as accountant, developer, or help desk technician, and users are then assigned to roles. This simplifies administration in large organizations because adding a new employee of a given type requires only assigning them to the appropriate role rather than individually configuring all of their access rights. RBAC is widely used in enterprise identity management systems, cloud platforms, and application authorization frameworks.

Attribute-Based Access Control, or ABAC, makes access decisions based on a combination of attributes from multiple categories: attributes of the requesting user such as their department, clearance level, or training certifications; attributes of the resource being accessed such as its data classification or owning business unit; and environmental attributes such as the time of day, the location of the request, or the security posture of the requesting device. ABAC is the most flexible and expressive access control model, capable of enforcing complex policies such as allowing read access to sensitive medical records only during business hours by clinical staff using organization-managed devices. Its flexibility comes with implementation complexity.

Rule-based access control applies static rules regardless of user identity. Firewall access control lists are the canonical example: a rule permitting HTTP traffic on port 80 from any source applies to all traffic matching that description without reference to who sent it.

The need-to-know principle holds that users should have access only to the specific information required for their current task or job function, even if their clearance level would permit broader access. A security cleared employee working on one program should not have access to data from unrelated programs simply because their clearance is sufficient.

Separation of duties ensures that no single person can complete a critical or high-risk transaction alone. By dividing sensitive processes so that two or more people must each perform a step, the principle reduces the risk of fraud, error, and insider threat. Dual control and two-person integrity rules are specific implementations where two authorized individuals must both be present and take action for a critical operation such as accessing a safe containing cryptographic material to proceed.

Least privilege requires that every user account, service account, and application process be granted only the minimum permissions necessary to perform its intended function, and no more. Accounts that exceed their required permissions provide an attacker with more capability if those accounts are compromised. Implementing least privilege requires understanding what each account actually needs to do and provisioning accordingly, rather than defaulting to broad or administrative access for convenience.

Privileged Access Management, or PAM, encompasses specialized controls for the high-risk administrative accounts that have elevated access to critical systems. PAM solutions provide just-in-time access, granting elevated permissions only when needed and revoking them immediately afterward; session recording of all privileged sessions for audit purposes; password vaulting that stores and automatically rotates the credentials for privileged accounts; and separation of the accounts used for privileged work from accounts used for everyday tasks such as email and web browsing.

Network Access Control, or NAC, enforces security policy by verifying the compliance posture of devices before granting them network access. A NAC system checks whether the connecting device meets standards such as having current antivirus signatures, an approved operating system version with current patches, an active host-based firewall, and a valid device certificate. Devices that do not meet the policy are redirected to a quarantine network where they can receive updates to bring themselves into compliance before being granted full access. 802.1X provides the authentication framework for NAC in wired and wireless environments, with a RADIUS server performing policy decisions.
