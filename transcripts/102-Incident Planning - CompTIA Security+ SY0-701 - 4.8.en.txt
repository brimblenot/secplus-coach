Incident planning is the work done before incidents occur to ensure that when a security event does happen, the organization can respond effectively, consistently, and in accordance with its legal and regulatory obligations. The quality of incident planning directly determines the quality of incident response: organizations that have invested in comprehensive plans, well-trained teams, tested procedures, and established external relationships consistently outperform those that improvise their response. Incident planning is not a one-time activity but a continuous improvement process that is refined through testing, after-action reviews, and adaptation to the evolving threat landscape.

The Incident Response Plan, or IRP, is the foundational document that defines how the organization responds to security incidents. It must exist before incidents occur and must be reviewed and updated at least annually, with additional updates triggered by significant changes in the organization's infrastructure, threat landscape, or regulatory environment, and by lessons learned from actual incidents or exercises. A complete IRP contains several essential components. The policy statement establishes management commitment to incident response and grants the incident response team the authority to take the actions necessary to contain and remediate incidents, including isolating systems from the network and preserving evidence. The purpose and scope defines what types of events are covered by the plan, which systems and data are in scope, and how the plan relates to other organizational plans such as the Business Continuity Plan and Disaster Recovery Plan. The roles and responsibilities section defines the membership of the Computer Security Incident Response Team, or CSIRT, including specific roles and their duties, contact information for all team members and their alternates, and the escalation path for incidents of increasing severity. The definitions section establishes consistent terminology including what constitutes an incident, how incidents are classified by severity, and what the specific thresholds are for each severity level. The incident response procedures provide step-by-step guidance for responding to common incident types. The communication plan defines who is notified for each severity level, what information is included in notifications, what communication channels are used, and what external parties must be contacted including legal counsel, regulatory bodies, law enforcement, customers, and business partners. The retention and evidence handling section defines how evidence is collected, preserved, and handled in compliance with legal requirements. The training and testing requirements section defines how frequently team members receive training, what exercises are conducted, and how exercise results are incorporated into plan improvements.

The CSIRT organizational structure can take several forms depending on the organization's size, resources, and risk profile. An internal dedicated CSIRT consists of full-time security professionals whose primary responsibility is incident response. This model provides the fastest and most effective response capability because team members are continuously practiced and available, but it requires significant budget to staff and is typically found only in large organizations or those with particularly high-risk profiles. A virtual CSIRT consists of employees from various departments who have designated incident response roles in addition to their primary responsibilities. IT, legal, HR, communications, and operations staff are trained in their IR roles and activated when needed. This model is common in mid-size organizations because it provides a capable response team without the cost of a dedicated team, though virtual teams may be slower to mobilize and less practiced than dedicated teams. Many organizations use a hybrid model with a small dedicated core security team supplemented by on-call specialists from relevant departments and an external DFIR, Digital Forensics and Incident Response, vendor who can provide additional capacity and specialized expertise for major incidents.

Third-party IR vendor retainer agreements establish a relationship with a specialist IR firm before incidents occur. This is important because attempting to engage and contract with an IR firm during an active major incident introduces delays that can allow an attacker to cause additional damage. With a retainer in place, the IR firm already understands the organization's environment, the response team has an established working relationship with the firm's consultants, and the legal and commercial terms are already agreed, so the firm can begin work immediately when called. Major DFIR firms include Mandiant (now part of Google), CrowdStrike Services, Secureworks, and many regional and boutique specialists. The retainer agreement typically defines the response time SLA, the scope of services available, the hourly rates for various service types, and the notification and escalation process.

Playbooks and runbooks serve distinct purposes within the incident planning framework. A playbook is a high-level decision tree or workflow that guides the responder through the strategic decisions of an incident response: when to escalate to the next severity level, when to move from monitoring to containment, when to notify executive leadership, when to engage external IR assistance, and when to notify regulatory authorities. A runbook is a detailed technical document containing the specific commands, tools, and procedures used to accomplish specific tasks during an incident. A runbook for evidence collection might contain the exact commands to capture a memory image on a Linux system using WinPmem or a similar tool, the specific incantation for creating a forensic disk image with dcfldd including hash verification, and the file naming and chain-of-custody documentation requirements. Runbooks eliminate the need for responders to recall procedural details under pressure, reducing errors and ensuring consistency. Organizations should maintain playbooks and runbooks for their most likely incident types, including ransomware, phishing campaign, data breach, DDoS, insider threat, credential compromise, malware infection, and unauthorized access.

Legal and regulatory planning ensures the organization understands its notification obligations before an incident occurs, so that compliance actions can be executed promptly rather than researched under time pressure. The 72-hour notification requirement under GDPR for personal data breaches requires that the organization be able to determine within 72 hours whether a breach has occurred and what personal data was affected, necessitating sufficiently detailed logging and monitoring to support this determination. HIPAA breach notification requirements mandate notification to affected individuals within 60 days of discovery and notification to HHS, with additional media notification requirements for breaches affecting more than 500 individuals in a state. State breach notification laws vary in scope, timing, and required content and must be mapped to the organization's data and customer base. International organizations must consider breach notification requirements in all relevant jurisdictions. Legal hold procedures must be established in advance so that when litigation is anticipated, relevant evidence can be preserved immediately before normal data retention and deletion processes destroy it. Relationships with law enforcement contacts including the FBI's cyber division, CISA, and sector-specific information sharing organizations should be established before incidents occur so that the engagement process during an incident is not delayed by unfamiliar procedures.

The communication plan for incidents is a critical and often underestimated component of incident planning. Pre-defined communication templates for common scenarios — customer breach notification letters, employee internal notifications, media statements, and regulatory notifications — allow communications to be drafted, reviewed, and approved in advance rather than being written from scratch during a crisis. Pre-approved contact lists with verified contact information for the CEO, General Counsel, CISO, board security committee, communications or PR lead, and investor relations must be maintained and tested. Communication restrictions during active incidents are particularly important: disclosing to all employees that a breach is in progress could tip off an insider threat or alert an attacker who retains access to pivot or accelerate their activities. Information about an active incident should be shared on a strict need-to-know basis until the attacker has been ejected from the environment.

Training for incident response effectiveness requires regular, progressive exercises. Annual IR training ensures that all CSIRT members understand the current IR plan and their specific roles. Tabletop exercises, conducted quarterly or more frequently for mature teams, walk participants through realistic scenarios using discussion and decision-making without activating real systems. Effective tabletop exercises present scenario injects that complicate the initial situation — for example, a scenario that begins with a malware detection but then reveals that the attacker has also compromised the backup system, forcing the team to revise its recovery plan in real time. Functional exercises test specific components of the IR plan in isolation, such as verifying that the backup restoration process works as documented or confirming that the communication notification tree reaches all contacts within the required time. Full-scale exercises activate actual IR procedures against test systems to verify that the complete process works end-to-end. Post-exercise reviews produce action items that feed directly back into plan improvements, creating a continuous improvement cycle.

NIST Special Publication 800-61, the Computer Security Incident Handling Guide, is the authoritative framework reference for incident planning and should be thoroughly understood for the Security+ examination. The MITRE ATT&CK framework complements incident planning by providing a structured taxonomy of adversary tactics, techniques, and procedures that can be used to develop realistic exercise scenarios, ensure that playbooks address the full range of attacker behaviors the organization might encounter, and identify detection and response gaps by mapping existing controls against the ATT&CK matrix. Threat intelligence integration into the planning process ensures that playbooks address the specific TTPs of threat actors who are likely to target the organization based on its industry, geography, and profile.
