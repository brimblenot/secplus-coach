Privacy is the right of individuals to control their personal information and determine how it is collected, used, shared, and retained by others. Privacy is distinct from security, though the two concepts are deeply interrelated. Security protects information from unauthorized access, modification, and disclosure, which is a necessary condition for privacy. But privacy is broader than security: it encompasses not only whether information is technically protected but whether the collection, use, and sharing of that information respects individuals' reasonable expectations and complies with applicable law. An organization can have strong security controls and still violate privacy by using personal data for unauthorized purposes, retaining it longer than necessary, or sharing it with parties the individual did not consent to.

Privacy principles form the conceptual foundation of data protection law and practice. The Fair Information Practice Principles, the OECD Privacy Guidelines, and the GDPR privacy principles are all expressions of the same core ideas, and understanding them provides a framework for analyzing privacy questions on the Security+ exam and in professional practice.

The principle of lawfulness, fairness, and transparency requires that organizations process personal data on a legal basis that is clear, that they process it in ways that do not harm data subjects or violate their reasonable expectations, and that they inform individuals about how their data is used in language they can understand. The principle of purpose limitation requires that personal data be collected for specified, explicit, and legitimate purposes and not processed further in ways incompatible with those original purposes. If an organization collects email addresses for the purpose of delivering transaction confirmations, using those email addresses to send marketing messages without obtaining separate consent would violate purpose limitation. The principle of data minimization requires that organizations collect only the personal data that is actually necessary for the stated purpose. Collecting additional data fields because they might someday be useful, or because it is technically easy to collect them, violates data minimization. The principle of accuracy requires that organizations maintain personal data in an accurate and current state, updating it when necessary and removing or correcting inaccurate information. The principle of storage limitation requires that personal data be retained only as long as is necessary for the purpose for which it was collected, after which it must be deleted or anonymized. The principle of integrity and confidentiality, which is essentially the security principle applied to personal data, requires that organizations implement appropriate technical and organizational measures to protect personal data against unauthorized access, loss, alteration, and destruction. The principle of accountability requires that the data controller be responsible for and able to demonstrate compliance with all of these principles.

Personal data categories are important to distinguish because different categories trigger different levels of protection and different legal bases for processing. Personally Identifiable Information, abbreviated PII, is the general term for any information that identifies or can be used to identify a specific individual. Direct identifiers uniquely identify an individual on their own: name combined with social security number, passport number, driver's license number, or biometric data. Quasi-identifiers do not uniquely identify an individual alone but can do so in combination: a zip code, birth date, and gender combination can uniquely identify a significant proportion of individuals in a population. Protected Health Information, abbreviated PHI, is the healthcare-specific category of PII that includes diagnoses, treatment records, prescription histories, insurance information, and any other health-related information that can be linked to a specific individual. PHI is protected by HIPAA in the United States. Special category data under GDPR requires heightened protection because of its particular sensitivity: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious or philosophical beliefs, trade union membership, genetic data, biometric data processed for the purpose of uniquely identifying a person, health data, and data concerning a person's sex life or sexual orientation. Processing special category data requires either explicit consent from the data subject or a specific derogation established by law, and the bar for satisfying these requirements is significantly higher than for ordinary personal data.

Privacy by Design is a framework of seven foundational principles that calls for privacy protections to be proactively built into systems, processes, and products from the beginning rather than added on afterward. The first principle is proactive not reactive, meaning the organization anticipates and prevents privacy-invasive events before they occur rather than remedying harm after it has happened. The second principle is privacy as the default setting, meaning the maximum privacy protection is provided automatically without requiring any action from the individual. Third, privacy is embedded into design, meaning privacy protection is an integral component of the system's architecture rather than an add-on feature. Fourth, privacy by design is full functionality, not a zero-sum approach, meaning it seeks positive-sum outcomes where privacy, security, and functionality all coexist rather than treating privacy as inevitably in conflict with other objectives. Fifth, privacy by design requires end-to-end security throughout the entire information lifecycle from collection through deletion. Sixth, visibility and transparency means that all data processing activities are verifiable by users, auditors, and regulators. Seventh, respect for user privacy means keeping the system user-centric and providing mechanisms for individuals to exercise their rights.

GDPR establishes enumerated rights for data subjects that organizations must be prepared to fulfill within defined timeframes. The right of access, exercised through a Data Subject Access Request or DSAR, entitles individuals to receive a copy of all personal data the organization holds about them along with information about how it is processed. The right to rectification entitles individuals to request correction of inaccurate or incomplete personal data. The right to erasure, sometimes called the right to be forgotten, entitles individuals to request deletion of their personal data when it is no longer needed for the purpose for which it was collected, when consent has been withdrawn and there is no other lawful basis for processing, when the data was processed unlawfully, or when deletion is required to comply with a legal obligation. The right to restriction of processing entitles individuals to request that the organization stop processing their data in certain ways while a dispute about accuracy or lawfulness is being resolved. The right to data portability entitles individuals to receive their personal data in a structured, commonly used, machine-readable format that can be transferred to another data controller. The right to object entitles individuals to object to processing of their data for direct marketing purposes, which must always be honored, and to object to processing based on legitimate interests, which the organization may override by demonstrating compelling legitimate grounds. Rights related to automated decision-making entitle individuals not to be subject to decisions made solely by automated processing, including profiling, that produce significant legal or similarly significant effects, unless the decision is necessary for a contract, authorized by law, or the individual has given explicit consent, and in those cases the individual must have the right to obtain human review of the decision.

GDPR requires organizations to respond to data subject access requests within thirty calendar days, with an extension of up to two additional months available for complex or numerous requests, provided the individual is notified of the extension within the initial thirty-day period. California's CCPA requires responses within forty-five days. Organizations must implement processes for receiving, verifying the identity of, and responding to data subject requests within these timeframes.

The Privacy Impact Assessment, sometimes called a Data Protection Impact Assessment or DPIA under GDPR, is a systematic evaluation of the privacy risks posed by a new data processing activity before it begins. GDPR requires a DPIA whenever processing is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals, which includes large-scale processing of sensitive data, systematic monitoring of publicly accessible areas, and systematic and extensive evaluation of individuals through automated processing. The DPIA identifies the nature, scope, context, and purposes of the processing; assesses the necessity and proportionality of the processing relative to its purposes; assesses the risks to data subjects; and identifies measures to address those risks. If the DPIA identifies a high residual risk that cannot be mitigated to acceptable levels, the organization must consult with the supervisory authority before beginning the processing.

Privacy notices must inform data subjects about who is processing their data, what personal data is being collected, the purposes for which it will be processed, the legal basis for each processing purpose, the recipients or categories of recipients, any international transfers and the safeguards applied, the retention period or the criteria used to determine it, the data subject's rights and how to exercise them, the right to withdraw consent where consent is the legal basis, and the contact information of the Data Protection Officer if one has been appointed. Privacy notices must be provided at the time of collection and must be written in clear, plain language accessible to the intended audience.

Consent management is critically important under GDPR and similar laws. For consent to be a valid legal basis for processing, it must be freely given, meaning the individual genuinely has a choice and suffers no disadvantage for refusing; specific, meaning separate consent must be obtained for each distinct purpose rather than bundled together; informed, meaning the individual understands what they are consenting to; and unambiguous, meaning the consent must be given through a clear affirmative action rather than inferred from silence, pre-ticked boxes, or inactivity. Organizations must maintain records of when, how, and for what specific purposes consent was given and must provide individuals with a simple mechanism to withdraw consent at any time.

Cross-border data transfers present a particular challenge for multinational organizations. The GDPR restricts the transfer of EU personal data to countries outside the European Economic Area unless an appropriate safeguard is in place. Adequacy decisions by the European Commission identify countries whose data protection laws provide a level of protection essentially equivalent to the GDPR; data may flow freely to these countries. Standard Contractual Clauses are model contract terms adopted by the European Commission that, when incorporated into contracts between EU data exporters and non-EU data importers, provide a legally recognized safeguard for data transfers. Binding Corporate Rules are intra-group transfer mechanisms used by multinational companies to authorize personal data flows between entities within the same corporate group when some of those entities are located in non-EU countries.

A comprehensive privacy program includes a public privacy notice, an internal data inventory and records of processing activities, a DSAR handling process with documented procedures and response tracking, a breach notification procedure meeting all applicable timelines, regular privacy training for all staff, a DPIA process for new data processing initiatives, and data mapping that traces how personal data flows through the organization's systems and to third parties.
