Once risks have been identified and analyzed, the organization must decide how to respond to each one. There are four fundamental risk response strategies that apply to virtually every risk an organization faces: risk mitigation, risk acceptance, risk avoidance, and risk transference. Each strategy is appropriate under different circumstances, and sophisticated organizations use all four in combination depending on the nature of the risk, the cost of available controls, and the organization's risk appetite. Understanding when each strategy is appropriate and how it is implemented is tested directly on the Security+ exam.

Risk mitigation involves reducing the level of risk by implementing security controls that decrease either the likelihood that a threat will successfully exploit a vulnerability or the impact of a successful exploitation. Mitigation is the most common response to high-priority risks. Controls that reduce likelihood include patching vulnerabilities so they cannot be exploited, implementing multifactor authentication so that stolen credentials alone cannot enable unauthorized access, enforcing least-privilege access controls so that an attacker who compromises a standard user account gains limited ability to move laterally, and deploying network segmentation so that a breach in one segment cannot easily spread to others. Controls that reduce impact include encrypting data at rest and in transit so that stolen data cannot be read without the encryption key, implementing comprehensive backup solutions so that ransomware cannot permanently destroy critical data, developing and testing incident response plans so that the organization can respond quickly and effectively when a breach occurs, and purchasing cyber liability insurance so that the financial impact of a breach is partially offset. Risk mitigation is ongoing because the threat landscape evolves, new vulnerabilities are discovered, and the controls implemented today may become inadequate as attackers develop new techniques. Even after mitigation, some level of residual risk remains, and that residual risk must be compared to the organization's risk tolerance to determine whether additional mitigation is needed.

Risk acceptance is the conscious, documented decision to live with an identified risk without implementing additional controls beyond those already in place. Risk acceptance is appropriate under specific circumstances: when the cost of implementing mitigation controls exceeds the expected financial loss from the risk over a reasonable time horizon, making the investment economically unjustifiable; when the risk falls below the organization's established risk tolerance and does not warrant additional investment; or when the timeline for the risk naturally decreasing is short enough that implementing a control would be wasteful. A common example is the decision to accept the risk of an unpatched vulnerability in a system that will be decommissioned in thirty days, when implementing the patch would require expensive testing and the system will no longer exist by the time an attack would realistically occur. Risk acceptance must be distinguished from neglect. Neglect is the failure to address a risk due to ignorance, inattention, or resource constraints without conscious decision-making. Risk acceptance is an informed, documented decision that requires formal documentation of the decision rationale, signature by the risk owner who is an accountable senior manager, identification of any compensating controls that partially offset the risk, and a review date when the decision will be reassessed. Risk acceptance without documentation provides no legal or governance protection.

Policy exemptions and policy exceptions are closely related to risk acceptance and often appear together on the Security+ exam. A policy exemption removes a specific system, user, or situation from the scope of a policy requirement entirely, typically because compliance with the policy is technically impossible in that specific context. An example would be a manufacturing system running an embedded operating system that cannot receive standard operating system patches without breaking the manufacturing software. The manufacturer prohibits patching, making policy compliance impossible, so the organization approves an exemption from the patch management policy for that specific device, subject to compensating controls such as network isolation. A policy exception is a temporary deviation from a policy requirement when compliance is technically possible but creates a specific operational conflict. An exception might be granted when a newly released patch causes a critical application to crash and the organization needs additional time to work with the vendor to resolve the compatibility issue before applying the patch. Both exemptions and exceptions must be formally documented, approved by an appropriate authority, and reviewed periodically to ensure they do not become permanent workarounds that undermine the security program.

Risk avoidance eliminates the activity that creates the risk entirely. This is the most extreme form of risk response because it typically has significant business implications, requiring the organization to forgo an activity, product, market, or technology entirely. Examples include deciding not to collect and store payment card numbers directly, instead routing all payment transactions through a third-party payment processor, which eliminates PCI DSS scope and the associated risk of cardholder data breach. Another example is deciding not to enter a market where the regulatory environment creates unacceptable legal risk. A technology company might decide not to deploy a particular technology that has known, unfixable security vulnerabilities until those vulnerabilities are resolved by the vendor. Risk avoidance is appropriate when the risk associated with an activity is so high that no feasible combination of mitigation, acceptance, and transference can reduce it to within the organization's risk tolerance, and when the activity can be discontinued or replaced without unacceptable business consequence.

Risk transference shifts the financial burden of a risk to another party without eliminating the underlying risk. Someone still bears the financial consequences of the risk materializing; transference simply changes who bears them. Cyber liability insurance is the most common form of risk transference in cybersecurity. Organizations purchase cyber insurance policies that cover the costs associated with a breach including forensic investigation, breach notification to affected individuals, credit monitoring services for affected customers, legal fees, regulatory fines, and business interruption losses. The average cyber insurance claim in the United States is roughly three hundred thousand dollars, but significant breaches can result in claims in the tens of millions. The premium an organization pays for cyber insurance reflects its risk profile: organizations with stronger security controls, lower prior incident rates, and more mature security programs typically pay lower premiums. It is essential to understand that cyber insurance does not reduce the probability of a breach; it only reduces the financial impact to the organization when a breach occurs. Contractual risk transference uses hold-harmless clauses, indemnification provisions, and service level agreement penalties to shift financial responsibility to vendors or partners whose failures cause harm. Outsourcing functions to third parties can shift operational risk, though organizations must understand that they retain ultimate legal liability for the protection of customer and employee data regardless of who processes it on their behalf.

Secondary risks are new risks created as a consequence of risk treatment activities. Implementing a backup system creates a new risk because the backup infrastructure itself is an attractive target for attackers and may introduce new attack vectors. Migrating to cloud infrastructure as part of a risk avoidance strategy for on-premises infrastructure failure creates cloud-specific risks including misconfiguration, supply chain risk through the cloud provider, and data residency issues. Risk managers must account for secondary risks when evaluating risk treatment options.

Control selection involves evaluating candidate controls against several criteria. Effectiveness measures how significantly the control reduces the risk, expressed either as a reduction in likelihood, impact, or ALE. Cost encompasses not only the purchase price of a control but the full lifecycle cost including implementation labor, ongoing operations and maintenance, licensing fees, and training. Usability impact assesses whether the control creates friction in business processes that might lead users to find workarounds, which would undermine the control's effectiveness. Regulatory compliance evaluates whether the control satisfies specific regulatory requirements, since some compliance mandates require specific controls regardless of cost-benefit analysis.

Enterprise risk management, known as ERM, integrates cybersecurity risk with the full spectrum of business risks that boards and executive teams must manage, including financial risk, operational risk, strategic risk, market risk, and compliance risk. When security risk is presented to boards and executives in the context of overall enterprise risk, it receives more appropriate attention and more appropriate resource allocation. The CISO reporting on security risk at the board level using the language of expected financial loss, risk appetite, and residual risk enables informed governance decisions about security investment and risk tolerance.

The risk register must be maintained as a living document. Risks that have been successfully mitigated should be closed or reclassified. New risks identified through threat intelligence, vulnerability assessments, or business changes must be added. Risk scores should be recalculated when the threat landscape or control environment changes significantly. The risk register should be reviewed by risk owners quarterly and presented to executive leadership and the board on at least an annual basis to ensure that risk management decisions remain aligned with the organization's current strategy and risk appetite.
