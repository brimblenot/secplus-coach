A zero-day vulnerability is a security flaw in software or hardware that is unknown to the vendor responsible for producing and maintaining that product. The term zero-day reflects the fact that the vendor has had zero days in which to develop and release a patch — from the vendor's perspective, the vulnerability does not yet exist. Zero-day vulnerabilities are the most dangerous category of security flaw because the normal patch-and-update defense chain is broken: there is no patch to apply, no vendor advisory to respond to, and no signature for traditional security products to match. The Security+ exam covers zero-days in the context of the full vulnerability lifecycle, the CVE and CVSS systems, responsible disclosure practices, and defensive strategies that function without available patches.

Zero-day vulnerabilities are discovered through several distinct channels with different implications for how the information is used. Academic and corporate security researchers find vulnerabilities through code auditing, fuzzing, and reverse engineering and typically share their discoveries through coordinated disclosure programs. Bug bounty hunters work independently to find vulnerabilities in vendor-defined scope and submit findings through programs hosted on platforms such as HackerOne and Bugcrowd in exchange for monetary rewards. Nation-state intelligence agencies maintain dedicated research teams that discover and stockpile zero-days for offensive use in intelligence and military operations rather than disclosing them to vendors. Criminal organizations discover and purchase zero-days for use in financially motivated attacks. The same vulnerability may be discovered independently by multiple parties with very different intentions.

The lifecycle of a zero-day vulnerability begins with discovery, proceeds through weaponization — the development of working exploit code — and then exploitation in the wild, which may occur immediately or after a period of targeted use. At some point, the exploitation becomes visible to defenders or a researcher independently discovers the same flaw and discloses it, triggering vendor notification, patch development and testing, patch release, and finally patch deployment by the affected organizations. The most dangerous period is the window between when an attacker first begins exploiting the vulnerability and when organizations have successfully deployed the vendor's patch. During this window, no standard defensive measure addresses the specific flaw, and attackers with knowledge of it have a significant asymmetric advantage.

A prominent example from 2023 illustrates how rapidly zero-days can be discovered and patched in major products. Google disclosed a Chrome browser zero-day involving memory corruption and a sandbox escape in April of that year. Microsoft patched a zero-day in May that allowed self-signed code to execute during the UEFI boot process, bypassing the protections Secure Boot is designed to provide. Apple released three separate iOS and iPadOS patches the same month addressing a sandbox escape, sensitive information disclosure, and arbitrary code execution — all being actively exploited in targeted attacks before patches were available.

The Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures system provides a standardized naming and cataloging framework for security vulnerabilities. Maintained by MITRE at cve.mitre.org, the CVE system assigns each vulnerability a unique identifier in the format CVE followed by the year of assignment and a sequence number, such as CVE-2021-44228 for the Log4Shell vulnerability. This common identifier allows vendors, security products, vulnerability databases, and organizations to refer unambiguously to the same vulnerability across all contexts. Without CVE identifiers, the same flaw might be discussed under different names by different parties, complicating coordination and patch management.

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System provides a standardized numerical severity rating from 0.0 to 10.0 for each vulnerability. The Base Score captures intrinsic characteristics of the vulnerability: the Attack Vector indicates whether exploitation requires local, adjacent network, or internet-level access; the Attack Complexity reflects whether exploitation requires special conditions beyond the attacker's control; the Privileges Required dimension captures whether any authentication is needed before exploitation; the User Interaction field indicates whether a victim must take an action; the Scope metric captures whether exploitation can affect components beyond the vulnerable one; and the three Impact metrics quantify effects on Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability. A vulnerability with a CVSS Base Score of 10.0 — the maximum — is remotely exploitable over the internet, requires no authentication or user interaction, affects components beyond the vulnerable system itself, and causes complete compromise of all three security objectives simultaneously. The Temporal Score modifies the Base Score based on factors that change over time: exploit maturity (whether working exploit code is publicly available), remediation level (whether a patch exists), and report confidence (how certain the vulnerability details are). The Environmental Score allows organizations to adjust the score based on their specific circumstances, such as the criticality of the affected assets or compensating controls already in place. The resulting severity ratings — Critical for scores of 9.0 to 10.0, High for 7.0 to 8.9, Medium for 4.0 to 6.9, and Low for 0.1 to 3.9 — are widely used to prioritize patching efforts. The National Vulnerability Database at nvd.nist.gov enriches CVE entries with CVSS scores, Common Platform Enumeration identifiers linking vulnerabilities to affected products, references, and additional analysis.

Responsible disclosure, also called coordinated disclosure, is the practice of reporting a discovered vulnerability privately to the vendor and agreeing to withhold public disclosure for a defined period — typically ninety days, which is the standard established by Google's Project Zero research team — to allow the vendor time to develop, test, and release a patch before attackers can exploit the published details. At the end of the agreed period, the researcher publishes the vulnerability details regardless of whether the vendor has released a patch, which creates an incentive for vendors to act promptly. Full immediate disclosure, in which researchers publish vulnerability details publicly without notifying the vendor or waiting for a patch, remains controversial: it maximizes pressure on vendors and gives the security community immediate awareness, but it also gives attackers the same information before users can protect themselves.

Bug bounty programs are formal frameworks through which vendors pay security researchers for responsibly disclosed vulnerabilities. Major technology companies including Microsoft, Google, and Apple operate programs that pay substantial rewards — up to one million dollars or more for critical vulnerabilities in flagship products. Bug bounty programs align researcher incentives with vendor interests by creating a legitimate financial reward for disclosure rather than sale to exploit brokers. Exploit brokers such as Zerodium purchase zero-day vulnerabilities from researchers and resell them to government intelligence agencies at prices that reflect the operational value of exclusive access. Zerodium has published price lists indicating payments of up to 2.5 million dollars for complete iOS exploit chains that require no user interaction, reflecting the enormous value these capabilities have in offensive intelligence operations.

Nation-states stockpile zero-days for strategic purposes. The NSA's EternalBlue exploit for Windows SMB, developed for offensive intelligence operations and later stolen and published by the Shadow Brokers hacking group in 2017, was subsequently weaponized in both the WannaCry ransomware worm and the NotPetya wiper attack, causing tens of billions of dollars in global damage. This incident illustrates the risk that government-stockpiled zero-days pose when they escape their intended operational context.

Defending against zero-day vulnerabilities requires abandoning the assumption that patching known vulnerabilities is sufficient. Defense-in-depth — maintaining multiple independent security layers so that no single vulnerability enables a complete compromise — is the foundational principle. Behavioral monitoring and endpoint detection and response platforms detect exploitation behavior patterns including shellcode execution, process injection, and unusual parent-child process relationships without needing to know the specific vulnerability being exploited. Application sandboxing limits the blast radius of successful exploitation by constraining what resources a compromised process can access: browsers sandbox renderer processes, operating systems sandbox application extensions, and enterprise endpoint protection products can sandbox unknown executables. Network segmentation prevents lateral movement after initial compromise so that exploiting a single endpoint does not grant immediate access to critical internal systems. Least privilege limits the damage a compromised process can cause by ensuring it has minimal permissions. Threat intelligence from government agencies such as CISA, information sharing and analysis centers, and commercial threat intelligence feeds provides timely awareness of zero-days being actively exploited in the wild. Virtual patching through WAF rules or IPS signatures can block known exploitation patterns while vendors develop official patches. Maintaining a minimal attack surface — reducing the number of exposed services, systems, and network interfaces — limits the number of systems that can be targeted by any given zero-day.

For the Security+ exam, candidates must understand the definition of a zero-day vulnerability, be able to explain the CVE naming convention and CVSS scoring bands, distinguish between responsible disclosure and full immediate disclosure, and articulate defensive strategies that remain effective in the absence of available patches.
