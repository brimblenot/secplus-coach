Third-party risk refers to the risks that arise from an organization's relationships with external entities including vendors, suppliers, partners, contractors, service providers, and managed service providers who have access to the organization's systems, data, facilities, or networks. The management of third-party risk is sometimes called vendor risk management or supply chain risk management, and it has become one of the highest-priority concerns in enterprise security because some of the most damaging breaches in history have originated not through direct attacks on well-defended organizations but through compromises of less-well-defended third parties who had trusted access to those organizations.

The importance of third-party risk management is illustrated by several landmark incidents. In 2013, attackers accessed Target's corporate network by stealing credentials belonging to an HVAC vendor that had remote access to the retail network for monitoring and billing purposes. Using those credentials, attackers navigated from the HVAC vendor's connection into the point-of-sale network and installed malware that stole credit card data from approximately forty million customers. The attacker never needed to penetrate Target's defenses directly because a less-secure third party provided an easier path. The SolarWinds attack, disclosed in December 2020, demonstrated supply chain risk on a massive scale. Attackers compromised the SolarWinds development environment between March and June of 2020 and inserted malicious code into an update for the Orion network monitoring platform. SolarWinds digitally signed the malicious update and distributed it to customers through their normal update mechanism. Approximately eighteen thousand of the three hundred thousand customers who used the affected version installed the malicious update, giving attackers access to networks including those of multiple US federal agencies and major corporations. The Kaseya VSA attack in July 2021 used a vulnerability in a managed service provider platform to deploy ransomware to approximately fifteen hundred organizations through a single attack on one software vendor.

Third-party risk categories help organize the types of risk that different vendor relationships create. Data access risk arises when a vendor can access the organization's sensitive data, requiring strong contractual protections, technical access controls, and monitoring of vendor data access activities. System access risk arises when a vendor has remote access to organizational systems and networks, requiring network access controls, monitoring of vendor sessions, and ideally privileged access management with session recording. Availability risk arises when the organization depends on a vendor's services for its own operations, creating exposure to the vendor's uptime and reliability. Concentration risk is a specific form of availability risk that arises when the organization relies too heavily on a single vendor or service, creating a single point of failure. Security posture risk arises when a vendor's own security weaknesses create exposure for the organization through the connection between them. Supply chain risk extends the concern beyond the immediate vendor to the vendor's own suppliers and service providers, recognizing that a compromise anywhere in an extended supply chain can ultimately affect the organization. Geographic and geopolitical risk arises when a vendor operates in jurisdictions with different legal requirements, unstable conditions, or under the influence of adversarial nation-states.

The third-party risk management process provides a lifecycle approach to managing vendor relationships from initial engagement through termination. Vendor identification and inventory is the foundation of the process, requiring a comprehensive catalog of all vendors the organization works with and what they access. Many organizations are surprised to discover during this exercise that they have hundreds or thousands of vendor relationships that were individually approved without a holistic view of the total third-party exposure. Tiering and criticality assessment classifies vendors by their risk level, which is determined by the combination of what they can access and the sensitivity of that access. Tier 1 vendors are high-risk because they access highly sensitive data or have broad system access and require the most rigorous assessment and ongoing monitoring. Tier 2 vendors have moderate access and moderate sensitivity and require less intensive but still substantive assessment. Tier 3 vendors have limited access to low-sensitivity information and may require only basic questionnaire review.

Vendor security assessment methods vary in depth, cost, and the reliability of the evidence they produce. Security questionnaires are the most commonly used initial assessment tool. The Standardized Information Gathering questionnaire, known as SIG, is widely used in the financial services industry and covers a comprehensive set of security domains including access control, asset management, audit, change management, cloud computing, compliance, cryptography, data security, endpoint security, human resources security, incident management, network security, physical security, and operational resilience. The Vendor Security Assessment Questionnaire, known as VSAQ, is used primarily in the technology sector. Organizations also develop custom questionnaires tailored to their specific risk concerns and vendor profile. The limitation of questionnaires is that they rely on vendor self-reporting, which may be inaccurate, incomplete, or optimistic.

SOC 2 Type 2 reports provide independent audit evidence of vendor security controls. A Type 2 report covers a period typically ranging from six to twelve months and is produced by an independent auditing firm that examined whether the vendor's controls related to the Trust Service Criteria were designed appropriately and operated effectively throughout the period. When reviewing a vendor's SOC 2 report, the reviewer should note the scope of the assessment, confirming that it covers the systems and services the organization will use; the time period, confirming that the report is current and less than twelve months old; any exceptions or qualified opinions noted by the auditor, which indicate control failures; and the trust service categories included, confirming that Security is included at minimum.

ISO 27001 certification indicates that the vendor has implemented an Information Security Management System that has been certified by an accredited third-party certification body through an initial certification audit and annual surveillance audits. Certification provides evidence of a structured, risk-managed security program, though the specific controls implemented may vary based on the vendor's risk assessment.

The right-to-audit clause is a contractual provision giving the organization the right to assess the vendor's security controls through questionnaire completion, document review, or on-site audit. This clause is increasingly important in vendor contracts, particularly for Tier 1 vendors with access to the most sensitive data. Exercising the right to audit provides direct evidence of vendor security posture beyond what the vendor chooses to disclose voluntarily.

Vendor contracts must include specific security provisions to create legally enforceable security obligations. Service Level Agreements define uptime guarantees and penalties for unavailability. Non-Disclosure Agreements protect the confidentiality of information shared during the relationship. Data Processing Agreements, required under GDPR, specify the nature and purpose of personal data processing, the security measures the vendor must implement, sub-processor requirements, breach notification obligations, data deletion requirements, and audit rights. Master Service Agreements define the overarching terms of the relationship. Statements of Work define specific deliverables, timelines, and performance expectations for individual projects. Security addenda or schedules may specify encryption standards, access control requirements, patch management obligations, incident notification timeframes, and the right to audit.

Ongoing monitoring recognizes that a vendor's security posture can change over time, requiring continuous attention rather than a one-time assessment at the start of the relationship. Vendor risk monitoring services such as BitSight and SecurityScorecard continuously collect observable signals from outside the vendor's network, including evidence of open vulnerabilities, malware infections, participation in botnets, patching cadence, and configuration issues, and translate these signals into a security rating that the organization can monitor. Annual reassessment questionnaires recollect information about the vendor's security program. Monitoring news and public disclosures allows organizations to detect vendor breaches or significant security incidents quickly. Annual review of updated SOC 2 reports tracks changes in the vendor's control environment over time. The secure termination of vendor relationships when they end must also be addressed, including revoking all access, retrieving or destroying organizational data held by the vendor, and documenting the termination.
