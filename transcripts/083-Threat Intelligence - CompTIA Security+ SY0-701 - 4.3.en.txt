Threat intelligence is processed, analyzed information about threats and threat actors that enables informed security decisions. The key distinction between raw data and threat intelligence is context and analysis — a list of IP addresses is data, but understanding that those IP addresses are command-and-control servers operated by a specific ransomware group that targets healthcare organizations, combined with knowledge of the group's preferred attack techniques and entry vectors, is intelligence. Threat intelligence transforms isolated data points into actionable understanding of who is attacking organizations, what methods they use, what targets they prefer, and how defenders can detect and stop them.

Security teams consume threat intelligence to make better decisions across multiple functions. Analysts use it to enrich SIEM alerts with context that helps prioritize response efforts. Architects use it to understand which attack vectors are popular and therefore which controls deserve investment. Vulnerability management teams use it to identify which CVEs are actively being exploited and therefore require accelerated patching beyond normal cycles. Incident responders use it to understand the tools, techniques, and procedures of the threat actors likely responsible for an active incident, enabling faster identification of attacker activity and more thorough eradication.

Open Source Intelligence, abbreviated OSINT, refers to threat intelligence gathered from publicly available sources without any special access or legal authority. OSINT is gathered from threat feeds that provide lists of malicious IP addresses, domains, URLs, and file hashes compiled from honeypots, sinkholes, and malware analysis. Security research blogs such as Krebs on Security, SANS Internet Storm Center, and vendor threat research publications from CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks Unit 42, and Mandiant regularly publish detailed analyses of new threats and attack campaigns. Government sources provide OSINT relevant to national security threats: the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency publishes alerts and advisories about active threats to US critical infrastructure, FBI Flash reports describe specific threat actor activities, and NSA cybersecurity advisories cover nation-state attack techniques. Social media monitoring covers attacker communities on Twitter and other platforms where researchers and threat actors discuss techniques and tools, paste sites like Pastebin where stolen data and malware source code are sometimes posted, and dark web forums where attackers buy and sell access, credentials, and exploit code. Code repositories like GitHub host proof-of-concept exploit code that can be discovered shortly after vulnerability disclosure. VirusTotal, any.run, and similar online sandboxes provide malware analysis results and behavioral indicators from submitted samples. WHOIS and passive DNS services allow analysts to investigate domain registration histories and IP address associations used by attacker infrastructure.

Threat intelligence sharing organizations enable the collective defense concept that organizations facing similar threats are more effective when they share intelligence than when each organization operates in isolation. Information Sharing and Analysis Centers, known as ISACs, are sector-specific nonprofit organizations that facilitate threat intelligence sharing among peer organizations in a particular industry. The Financial Services ISAC, known as FS-ISAC, serves the banking and financial services sector. The Health Information ISAC, known as H-ISAC, serves healthcare organizations. The Multi-State ISAC, known as MS-ISAC, is sponsored by CISA and serves state, local, tribal, and territorial governments. The Electricity ISAC, known as E-ISAC, serves the electric power sector. ISAC membership typically involves sharing threat indicators and incident information with other members under agreed confidentiality terms, and receiving a stream of curated threat intelligence relevant to the sector. Information Sharing and Analysis Organizations, known as ISAOs, were established by executive order as a more flexible complement to ISACs that allows any group of organizations to form a sharing community regardless of sector.

Threat intelligence formats standardize the representation of threat information to enable automated sharing and consumption. STIX, which stands for Structured Threat Information eXpression, is a standardized language for describing threat intelligence. STIX can represent Indicators of Compromise, attack patterns, malware characteristics, threat actor profiles, campaigns, courses of action, and relationships between all these objects. A STIX bundle might describe a threat actor with its known aliases, the campaign it is currently conducting, the malware it uses with behavioral indicators, the attack patterns it follows mapped to MITRE ATT&CK techniques, and the specific indicators such as file hashes and domain names associated with recent activity. TAXII, which stands for Trusted Automated eXchange of Intelligence Information, is the transport protocol used to share STIX data between organizations. TAXII uses a REST API model with servers that provide collections of threat intelligence objects that subscribing clients can retrieve. Together, STIX and TAXII enable automated, machine-readable threat intelligence sharing that can be integrated directly into security tools without requiring manual handling.

Commercial threat intelligence platforms provide curated, analyzed, and enriched intelligence products beyond what free OSINT sources offer. CrowdStrike Adversary Intelligence provides detailed profiles of tracked threat groups including their targeting patterns, toolsets, and motivation. Palo Alto Networks Unit 42 publishes threat research and provides intelligence subscriptions. Mandiant Threat Intelligence provides in-depth analysis of nation-state and criminal threat actors. Recorded Future correlates open web, dark web, and technical intelligence sources to provide early warning of emerging threats. ThreatConnect provides a platform for aggregating, analyzing, and operationalizing threat intelligence across security teams.

Indicators of Compromise, abbreviated IoCs, are observable artifacts that indicate a system may have been compromised or that an attack is underway. IP addresses associated with known malicious infrastructure including command-and-control servers, scanning sources, and phishing delivery systems can be blocklisted in firewalls and proxies to prevent communication with attacker-controlled systems. Domain names registered by threat actors for phishing landing pages, malware distribution, and command-and-control can be blocked at DNS filtering layers. URL patterns that match phishing pages or malware download paths can be blocked at web proxies. File hashes, typically MD5 or SHA-256 values, uniquely identify known malware samples and can be checked against endpoint security tools and file analysis systems. Email subject lines and sender addresses from known phishing campaigns can be blocklisted in email security gateways. YARA rules are pattern-matching expressions that can identify malware by matching byte sequences, string patterns, or structural characteristics in files — YARA rules can detect malware families even when individual file hashes change due to packing or obfuscation.

Tactics, Techniques, and Procedures, abbreviated TTPs, represent the behavioral patterns of threat actors and are more valuable for long-term defense than IoCs alone. IP addresses and domain names are easily changed — a threat actor whose command-and-control domains are blocked simply registers new domains. TTPs describe how attackers operate: their preferred initial access techniques, the post-exploitation tools they use, how they move laterally through networks, and how they exfiltrate data. Changing TTPs requires threat actors to develop new capabilities, making TTP-based detection more durable than IoC-based blocking. Understanding an adversary's TTPs allows defenders to focus detection and prevention efforts on the behaviors that characterize that adversary's operations.

The MITRE ATT&CK framework is a comprehensive, publicly available knowledge base of adversary TTPs organized into a matrix of Tactics and Techniques. Tactics represent the adversary's objectives at each stage of an attack: Initial Access describes how attackers get their first foothold in the target environment through methods including phishing, exploiting public-facing applications, and compromising supply chain software. Execution describes techniques for running attacker-controlled code including PowerShell, WMI, scheduled tasks, and exploiting interpreters. Persistence describes methods for maintaining access across reboots including registry run keys, scheduled tasks, boot or logon autostart execution, and backdoor accounts. Privilege Escalation describes how attackers gain higher-level access including exploiting vulnerabilities, token impersonation, and abusing sudo permissions. Defense Evasion describes techniques for avoiding detection including disabling security tools, timestomping, process injection, and obfuscated code. Credential Access describes methods for stealing credentials including keylogging, credential dumping from LSASS memory, Kerberoasting, and phishing. Discovery describes how attackers learn about the target environment including network scanning, account enumeration, and system information gathering. Lateral Movement describes how attackers move from one system to another including pass-the-hash, pass-the-ticket, Remote Desktop Protocol, and SMB-based movement. Collection describes how attackers gather data of interest including screen capture, keylogging, and data from network shares. Command and Control describes how attackers communicate with compromised systems including web protocols, DNS tunneling, and use of legitimate cloud services. Exfiltration describes how attackers move data out of the target environment including compression, encryption, and use of cloud storage services. Impact describes actions attackers take to achieve their objectives including ransomware deployment, data destruction, and service disruption.

Threat hunting is the proactive practice of searching through an organization's environment for threats that have evaded automated detection. Threat hunting begins with a hypothesis derived from threat intelligence — if intelligence indicates that a specific threat actor is using a particular living-off-the-land technique such as PowerShell with Base64-encoded commands for lateral movement, a threat hunter formulates a hypothesis that such activity might be present and then searches SIEM logs, EDR telemetry, and network traffic metadata for evidence of that behavior. Baseline anomaly hunting approaches the problem from the opposite direction, establishing what normal looks like and then searching for deviations: users accessing systems they have never accessed before, processes making network connections they have never made before, or services communicating with external hosts on unusual ports. Threat hunting requires human expertise and analytical skill to follow the evidence through a complex environment and distinguish legitimate anomalies from malicious activity.

Vulnerability intelligence connects threat intelligence to the patching process by tracking which vulnerabilities are being actively exploited in real-world attacks. The CISA Known Exploited Vulnerabilities catalog lists specific CVEs that CISA has confirmed are being actively exploited. Organizations that monitor threat intelligence feeds can identify when a newly disclosed vulnerability begins appearing in exploit frameworks or is attributed to specific ransomware groups, enabling accelerated patching before the wave of exploitation reaches their environment. Integrating vulnerability intelligence feeds directly into vulnerability management platforms allows automatic escalation of vulnerability priority based on real-world threat context.

Dark web monitoring involves observing underground marketplaces, forums, and paste sites for information relevant to the monitored organization. Threat intelligence services monitor the dark web for stolen credentials matching the organization's domain being offered for sale, data exfiltrated from the organization appearing in public dumps, discussions of planned attacks targeting the organization or its sector, and newly discovered vulnerabilities in software the organization uses being traded or discussed before public disclosure. When monitored credentials are discovered, the security team can invalidate those credentials before attackers use them for account takeover attacks. Dark web intelligence must be handled carefully because accessing certain dark web resources can create legal and policy issues.
