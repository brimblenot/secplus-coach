A security policy is a formal document that expresses management's commitment to protecting the organization's information assets. It establishes the rules and expectations for behavior that everyone in the organization must follow, and it provides direction for the security controls that will be implemented and maintained. Security policies are approved at the senior management level, which means the Chief Information Security Officer, executive team, and in regulated industries the board of directors must formally endorse these documents before they carry organizational authority. Once approved, security policies must be communicated to all personnel and reviewed and updated at least annually to remain relevant as threats, technology, and business requirements evolve.

Understanding the distinction between the different levels of the policy hierarchy is essential for the Security+ exam. A policy sits at the top of the hierarchy and expresses high-level management intent. A policy statement like "all data must be encrypted" tells the organization what must be accomplished but does not specify how. Below the policy level sits the standard, which provides specific and measurable requirements that operationalize the policy. A standard would specify that "AES-256 must be used for all encryption of data at rest," giving technical staff the precise requirement they need to implement controls. Guidelines exist at a third tier and represent recommendations and best practices that are advisory rather than mandatory. An organization might issue a guideline recommending the use of a particular password manager product, but employees who choose a different approved tool are not in violation. Procedures occupy the most detailed tier and are step-by-step instructions for performing specific tasks. A procedure for encrypting a laptop would include every discrete action a technician must take, from enabling BitLocker in the operating system settings through saving the recovery key to a designated location. The hierarchy flows downward from broad intent to operational specifics, and violations at any tier can expose the organization to risk.

The acceptable use policy, commonly abbreviated as AUP, is one of the most universally applicable policies because it applies to every person in the organization who touches organizational technology. The AUP defines what users are permitted and prohibited from doing with computers, mobile devices, telephones, networks, and any other technology provided by the organization. It addresses personal use of corporate equipment, defining whether and to what extent employees may browse personal social media or conduct personal business on work devices. The AUP explicitly prohibits access to certain categories of content, such as material that is illegal, offensive, or unrelated to work in ways the organization deems excessive. It establishes acceptable internet use, addresses whether employees may install software not approved by IT, and governs social media use on corporate systems. One of the most legally significant provisions of the AUP is the statement that employees have no expectation of privacy when using organizational technology. This clause notifies employees that their activities may be monitored, which is both an ethical obligation and a legal protection for the organization. The AUP must be acknowledged by all employees at the time of hire and recertified annually, creating documentation that protects the organization in any disciplinary or legal proceeding. The AUP also governs bring-your-own-device usage, specifying what personal devices may be used for work purposes and under what conditions.

The information security policy serves as the umbrella document that establishes the overall security program. It defines the scope of the security program, assigns high-level roles and responsibilities, and references all subordinate policies. Every other security policy in the organization should trace its authority back to this master document.

The password policy defines complexity requirements, minimum length, expiration intervals, prohibitions on reuse and sharing, requirements for secure storage, and requirements for the types of authentication that are acceptable in specific contexts. Organizations may require that passwords be a minimum of twelve characters and include a combination of uppercase letters, lowercase letters, numbers, and special characters. The policy also specifies how often passwords must be changed, how many previous passwords cannot be reused, and under what circumstances passwords may be reset.

The data classification policy governs how information is categorized according to its sensitivity and value, how it must be labeled at each classification level, how it must be handled in storage and transmission, and how it must be disposed of when it is no longer needed. A typical classification scheme for a private organization might include public, internal, confidential, and restricted tiers, each with escalating handling requirements.

The clean desk policy requires that all sensitive materials be locked away when an employee is not actively working with them and that workstation screens be locked whenever an employee leaves their desk, even briefly. This policy directly mitigates the risk of shoulder surfing, where unauthorized individuals observe sensitive information on screens or papers, and physical data theft through the removal of documents left unattended.

The social media policy defines what employees may and may not post publicly on personal or organizational social media accounts. It establishes confidentiality requirements that prevent disclosure of proprietary information, customer data, or internal details that could be exploited by adversaries. It governs the use of the organization's name, logo, and branding in personal social media contexts and typically requires employees to clarify that opinions expressed on personal accounts are their own.

The remote access policy establishes requirements for accessing corporate resources from outside the organizational network. It typically mandates the use of a VPN for all remote connections to internal resources, specifies minimum security requirements for devices used remotely such as up-to-date operating systems and enabled encryption, and restricts remote access to networks that meet minimum security standards. Home networks may be acceptable while public Wi-Fi hotspots may be explicitly prohibited without additional controls.

The incident response policy establishes reporting requirements for security events, defines what constitutes a reportable incident, provides contact information for the security team and escalation chain, and outlines the general escalation requirements. It answers the critical question of what an employee should do first when they observe a potential security incident, ensuring that the organization can respond rapidly and consistently.

The change management policy requires that all changes to systems, applications, configurations, and infrastructure pass through a formal change request and approval process before implementation. This policy exists because unauthorized changes are one of the most common causes of outages and security incidents. The policy defines who can submit change requests, what information must be included, who has authority to approve changes, and what testing and rollback planning is required.

The business continuity policy establishes requirements for maintaining critical operations during disruptions that range from temporary outages to major disasters. It defines which processes are considered critical, sets requirements for redundancy and recovery capabilities, and links to the disaster recovery plan for scenarios where primary facilities are unavailable.

The vendor and third-party policy establishes security requirements that suppliers and partners must meet before they are granted access to organizational systems or data. It defines the assessment requirements that vendors must satisfy, the contractual security obligations that will be imposed, and the ongoing monitoring activities that will occur throughout the vendor relationship.

The bring-your-own-device policy addresses the security requirements that apply to personal devices used for work purposes. These requirements typically include enrollment in a mobile device management system, a minimum supported operating system version, mandatory encryption of the device, consent to remote wipe capabilities that allow the organization to erase organizational data from the device, restrictions on which applications may access organizational data, and requirements for how organizational data must be handled and stored on the device.

The privacy policy published externally provides public notice of how the organization collects, uses, shares, and protects personal data. This document is required by major privacy laws including the General Data Protection Regulation and the California Consumer Privacy Act and must be accurate, complete, and easily accessible to individuals whose data the organization processes.

Policies must include provisions for exceptions and formal approval of those exceptions. A policy exception process allows the organization to temporarily deviate from a policy requirement when strict compliance is not feasible, provided that the risk is formally accepted by an authorized manager and that compensating controls are identified and implemented to partially offset the elevated risk. Exception requests must be documented, have a defined expiration date, and go through a review process.

Enforcement is the mechanism that gives policies their teeth. Policies that exist only on paper without consistent enforcement become merely aspirational documents. Effective policies define the consequences for violations, specify HR's involvement in the enforcement process, and leverage technical controls to enforce policy requirements wherever possible. When a password policy can be enforced through Group Policy settings in Active Directory, that technical enforcement is far more reliable than relying solely on employee awareness and good behavior.
