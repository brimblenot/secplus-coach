Data governance is the framework through which organizations manage data quality, security, availability, and proper use throughout the entire lifecycle of that data. Effective data governance assigns clear ownership and accountability for every dataset the organization holds, ensuring that someone is always responsible for deciding how data is used and protected. Without clear data governance, organizations tend to accumulate data without understanding who is responsible for it, how it should be protected, or when it should be deleted. The SY0-701 exam tests knowledge of the specific roles defined in data governance frameworks and, importantly, the GDPR-specific terminology that applies to organizations processing personal data.

The data owner is a senior leadership role that holds overall accountability for a specific dataset. Data owners are typically business executives rather than technical staff. The Vice President of Sales is the data owner for customer relationship data. The Chief Financial Officer or Treasurer is the data owner for financial data. The Chief Human Resources Officer is the data owner for personnel data. The data owner is accountable for the overall security and compliance of their data, makes decisions about who is permitted to access the data and for what purposes, approves data handling policies for their data domain, and bears ultimate responsibility for ensuring that the data is used in accordance with organizational policy and applicable law. Critically, the data owner does not typically perform technical security tasks. The data owner delegates operational security implementation to the data custodian while retaining accountability for the outcomes.

The data controller is a GDPR-specific term that describes the organization or individual who determines the purposes and means of processing personal data. The data controller decides why personal data is collected and processed and how that processing will be carried out. A healthcare provider that decides to collect and process patient health information for purposes of delivering medical treatment is a data controller with respect to that patient data. A company that collects and processes employee data for purposes of administering payroll and benefits is a data controller with respect to that employee data. The data controller is legally responsible for compliance with data protection regulations, must implement appropriate safeguards to protect the personal data under its control, must respect the rights of data subjects, and must document the lawful basis for each processing activity. When a data controller shares personal data with a vendor or partner who processes that data on the controller's behalf, the receiving party is a data processor, and the relationship must be governed by a Data Processing Agreement.

The data processor is a GDPR-specific term for the organization or individual that processes personal data on behalf of the data controller. The data processor acts under the instructions of the data controller and may not process the personal data for its own purposes or in ways not authorized by the controller. A payroll company that processes employee payroll data on behalf of the employer is a data processor. A cloud storage provider that stores customer data according to the customer's instructions and without accessing the content of that data for its own purposes is a data processor. An email marketing platform that sends marketing campaigns to a client's subscriber list on the client's behalf is a data processor. Data processors must implement appropriate technical and organizational security measures to protect the personal data they process, must notify the data controller of any data breaches within the timeframes specified in their Data Processing Agreement, must delete or return all personal data at the end of the processing relationship, and must ensure that any sub-processors they engage meet the same requirements. A Data Processing Agreement must exist between every data controller and every data processor before personal data is transferred and processing begins.

The data custodian, sometimes called the data steward, is the role responsible for the day-to-day technical security and management of data on behalf of the data owner. Where the data owner makes decisions about what protections are needed, the data custodian implements those protections. Data custodians implement access controls, configure encryption, manage backup and recovery, monitor for unauthorized access, and implement data classification and labeling schemes. The data custodian is also responsible for ensuring that data handling practices comply with the policies set by the data owner and with applicable laws and regulations. When a business unit manager who serves as the data owner decides that customer records require encryption at rest and that access should be limited to specific roles, the data custodian is the technical professional in IT security or data management who actually configures the encryption, sets up the access control lists, and monitors compliance with those requirements. The separation between the data owner's decision-making authority and the data custodian's implementation authority is an important control that prevents any single individual from having both the policy authority and the technical ability to misuse data without oversight.

The Data Protection Officer, abbreviated DPO, is a role required by the GDPR for certain categories of organizations. Organizations that process personal data on a large scale as a core business activity, organizations that process special category data such as health data or biometric data on a large scale, and certain public authorities must appoint a DPO. The DPO must have expert knowledge of data protection law and practice. The DPO's responsibilities include monitoring the organization's compliance with GDPR and other applicable data protection laws, advising on and monitoring the conduct of data protection impact assessments, cooperating with the supervisory authority when issues arise, and serving as the primary point of contact for data subjects who wish to exercise their rights. A legally significant protection of the DPO role is that the DPO cannot be dismissed or penalized by the organization for performing their DPO duties, which preserves their independence. The DPO may be an internal employee with the requisite expertise or an external consultant engaged for this purpose.

The principle of separation of duties applies directly to data governance roles. The data owner makes policy decisions about how data should be protected but does not implement the technical controls, which is the custodian's responsibility. The data custodian implements the technical controls but does not have the authority to override the data owner's access decisions. The auditor verifies compliance with both the data owner's policies and the custodian's implementation without being involved in either the policy decisions or the day-to-day data processing. This three-way separation ensures that no single person has unchecked control over all aspects of data security.

The data subject is the individual whose personal data is being processed. In GDPR terminology, data subjects have enumerated rights that the data governance framework must support exercising. The right of access allows data subjects to request a copy of all personal data the organization holds about them. The right to rectification allows data subjects to request correction of inaccurate personal data. The right to erasure, sometimes called the right to be forgotten, allows data subjects to request deletion of their personal data when it is no longer needed for the purpose for which it was collected or when they withdraw their consent. The right to portability allows data subjects to receive their personal data in a machine-readable format that can be transferred to another organization. The right to object allows data subjects to object to certain processing activities including direct marketing. Organizations must have documented processes for receiving, verifying, and responding to data subject rights requests within the timeframes required by applicable law, which under GDPR is generally thirty days.

Records of processing activities, required under Article 30 of the GDPR, must document all data processing operations the organization conducts. For each processing activity, the records must describe the purpose of the processing, the categories of data subjects and personal data involved, the recipients of the data, any international transfers and their safeguards, the retention periods, and a description of the technical and organizational security measures implemented. These records serve as the foundational documentation for demonstrating GDPR compliance to supervisory authorities.

Role-based training ensures that each category of personnel with data-related responsibilities understands their specific obligations. Data owners must understand data classification principles, privacy decision-making frameworks, and the regulatory implications of their data handling decisions. Data custodians require technical training on encryption, access control systems, backup management, and monitoring tools. Data processors require training on their contractual obligations, data breach notification requirements, and the restrictions on their use of data they process on behalf of controllers. All staff require training on the acceptable use policy and basic data handling requirements appropriate to their level of data access.
