Phishing is a form of social engineering delivered through electronic communications — primarily email — in which the attacker impersonates a trusted entity to deceive the recipient into revealing credentials, providing sensitive information, or performing an action that benefits the attacker. The word is a deliberate misspelling of "fishing," reflecting the idea of casting a wide net and waiting for victims to take the bait. Phishing messages typically impersonate banks, popular web services, IT departments, shipping companies, or government agencies — anything the target is likely to recognize and trust. The psychological mechanics of phishing rely on urgency, fear, and authority: a message claiming "Your account has been compromised — click here immediately to secure it" is engineered to override the recipient's critical thinking by creating emotional pressure for fast action.

A generic phishing message is sent to thousands or millions of recipients without personalization. The attacker accepts a very low success rate because the volume makes even one or two percent enough. These messages often contain spelling errors, awkward phrasing, mismatched fonts, suspicious sender addresses, or domain names that are similar but not identical to the legitimate organization. For example, an email claiming to be from Rackspace email services might be sent from an iCloud address, or it might contain subtle visual inconsistencies in the login page it leads to when compared side by side with the legitimate page. Careful users notice these discrepancies, but many do not, especially when operating under time pressure or when the message mimics a service they genuinely use.

Spear phishing is a highly targeted variant that uses personal information about the victim to create a convincing, individualized message. The attacker gathers information from LinkedIn profiles, company websites, social media, previous data breaches, and open-source intelligence to learn the victim's name, job title, colleagues, projects they are working on, and vendors they interact with. A spear phishing message might reference a real project by name, appear to come from a colleague's address, or mention a recent event relevant to the target. Because the message appears personalized and contextually appropriate, the recipient is far less likely to be suspicious. Spear phishing has a dramatically higher success rate than generic phishing and is the primary initial access method used by APT groups and organized cybercriminals targeting specific organizations.

Whaling is spear phishing directed specifically at high-value targets — typically C-suite executives such as the CEO, CFO, COO, or General Counsel. Because executives have authority to approve large financial transactions, access sensitive corporate data, and override standard procedures, they are extremely valuable targets. Whaling attacks frequently seek to initiate fraudulent wire transfers, obtain access to financial systems, or harvest credentials to executive email and cloud service accounts. The messages sent to these targets are typically polished and professionally written, referencing real business contexts and using language appropriate to senior leadership.

Vishing, or voice phishing, uses telephone calls rather than email. Attackers call targets while impersonating bank fraud departments warning of suspicious activity, IRS agents threatening legal action, IT helpdesk staff needing to fix a problem with the victim's account, or tech support representatives from Microsoft or Apple. The attacker creates urgency — an account about to be locked, a tax lien about to be filed — to push the victim into providing credit card numbers, Social Security numbers, bank account details, or remote desktop access via tools like AnyDesk or TeamViewer. Caller ID spoofing through VoIP services allows the attacker to make the call appear to originate from a bank's legitimate phone number, a government office, or a familiar corporate number, lending significant credibility to the impersonation. The defense against vishing is to never provide sensitive information to inbound callers, and instead hang up and call back using a number found independently — such as the number printed on a bank card or listed on an official website.

Smishing is phishing delivered via SMS text messaging. Typical smishing messages claim to be from shipping carriers such as USPS, FedEx, or UPS reporting a delivery problem, from banks alerting about suspicious account activity, or from government agencies about benefits or tax refunds. Mobile users are more susceptible than desktop users for several reasons: smartphone screens truncate long URLs, making it hard to inspect the actual destination; the casual, fast-paced nature of texting reduces scrutiny; and mobile browsers often display less security information than desktop browsers. An example of a real smishing message claims to be from the United States Postal Service saying a package has been suspended due to an incorrect delivery address, urging the recipient to click an embedded link — a link that leads to a credential-harvesting site, not the USPS.

Quishing is phishing using malicious QR codes. Attackers print malicious QR codes on stickers and place them over legitimate QR codes on restaurant menus, parking meters, conference room reservation signs, and event materials, or they embed malicious QR codes in emails and documents. QR codes are particularly effective as a phishing vector because users cannot read the destination URL before scanning the code — they must scan first, and by then the browser may already be loading the malicious page. QR codes in emails also tend to bypass some email security filters that scan links but do not decode and evaluate QR code destinations.

Business Email Compromise, commonly abbreviated BEC, is a sophisticated fraud attack in which the attacker impersonates an executive or a trusted vendor to authorize fraudulent financial transactions. Unlike most phishing attacks, BEC typically involves no malware — it is pure social engineering. The attacker may spoof an executive's email address or, in more sophisticated cases, actually compromise the executive's legitimate email account. From that position, the attacker sends messages to the finance department instructing them to wire funds to a new account, change vendor payment details, or redirect payroll deposits. Because the message appears to come from a legitimate authority figure and the request is framed as urgent and confidential, many employees comply without further verification. BEC causes billions of dollars in losses annually and is one of the most financially destructive cyberattack categories tracked by law enforcement.

Attackers employ numerous technical techniques to make phishing messages and pages more convincing. Domain spoofing involves forging the From header of an email to display a trusted organization's address, or registering a domain name that closely resembles the legitimate one — amazon-security.com versus amazon.com, for example. Lookalike domains and typosquatted domains use common misspellings or visual substitutions: the domain "arnazon.com" uses "rn" that, in certain fonts, appears to be "m." Homograph attacks exploit the Unicode character set, substituting characters from Cyrillic, Greek, or other alphabets that are visually identical or nearly identical to Latin letters; a domain displayed as "apple.com" may actually use a Cyrillic "a" that is a different character entirely, making the domain technically distinct while appearing identical. URL obfuscation uses URL shorteners, redirect chains, excessive subdirectory paths, or legitimate-looking domains as intermediary hops to conceal the final destination. HTML tricks present link text that appears to be a legitimate URL but the underlying href attribute points to the attacker's site — hovering over the link in most email clients reveals the true destination, which is why training users to hover before clicking is an important defensive behavior. Prepending adds text to increase credibility, such as "SAFE:" or "Re:" at the beginning of a subject line or visible URL fragment to suggest prior interaction or trusted scanning.

Pharming is a closely related but technically distinct attack. Rather than delivering a deceptive message and hoping the user clicks a link, pharming manipulates DNS resolution so that users who type a legitimate domain name are silently redirected to an attacker-controlled site. This can be accomplished by poisoning DNS cache entries at a recursive resolver — known as DNS cache poisoning — or by modifying the hosts file on an individual victim's computer. Because pharming attacks the infrastructure of name resolution rather than relying on user interaction with a deceptive message, they can affect large numbers of users who are not suspicious and who type the correct URL themselves.

Effective defenses against phishing operate at multiple layers. SPF — Sender Policy Framework — is a DNS record that specifies which mail servers are authorized to send email on behalf of a domain, allowing receiving mail servers to reject messages from unauthorized senders. DKIM — DomainKeys Identified Mail — adds a cryptographic signature to email headers that the receiving server can validate against a public key in the sender's DNS records, verifying that the message was not altered in transit and that the signing domain matches the claimed sender. DMARC — Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance — ties SPF and DKIM together with a policy that instructs receiving servers what to do when checks fail: the policy can be set to "none" for monitoring, "quarantine" to send failing messages to spam, or "reject" to drop them outright. DMARC also enables reporting, so domain owners receive data on messages being sent with their domain spoofed. Email gateway filtering uses threat intelligence, reputation data, and attachment sandboxing to evaluate messages before delivery. Multi-factor authentication is a critical compensating control: even if phishing successfully steals a user's password, MFA prevents the attacker from using it without the second factor. Security awareness training combined with simulated phishing exercises — where organizations send fake phishing emails to their own employees and use the results for targeted training — measurably reduces click rates over time. Anti-phishing browser extensions, user education on URL inspection, and organizational cultures that support questioning unusual requests without fear of embarrassment are all important components of a comprehensive anti-phishing defense.
