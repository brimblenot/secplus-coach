Resiliency is the capacity of systems, services, and organizations to withstand disruptions, adapt to adverse conditions, and recover rapidly when failures occur. In the context of the CIA triad, resiliency directly supports availability, the requirement that information and systems are accessible to authorized users when needed. Every organization depends on its technology infrastructure to conduct business, and every component of that infrastructure will eventually fail. The question resiliency engineering answers is not whether failure will occur but whether the organization can continue operating when it does. Resiliency is achieved through a combination of redundancy, diversity, geographic dispersion, high availability design, and documented disaster recovery capabilities.

Redundant Array of Independent Disks, universally known by its acronym RAID, is a fundamental storage resiliency technology that uses multiple physical disk drives in combination to achieve redundancy, improved performance, or both. Different RAID levels represent different trade-offs among these goals, and the Security+ exam expects candidates to understand the key characteristics of the most common levels.

RAID 0, called striping, divides data across multiple drives. Because simultaneous read and write operations occur across all drives in parallel, RAID 0 delivers the best read and write performance of any RAID level. However, RAID 0 provides no redundancy whatsoever. If any single drive in a RAID 0 array fails, the entire dataset is lost because each drive contains only a portion of every file. RAID 0 is appropriate only for temporary data or data that is easily regenerated, and security professionals must recognize that it actually increases the probability of data loss compared to a single drive, because any one of multiple drives can trigger total loss.

RAID 1, called mirroring, maintains identical copies of data on two drives simultaneously. Every write operation is performed to both drives at once. If one drive fails, the other contains a complete copy of all data, and operation continues without interruption or data loss. RAID 1 provides the simplest form of redundancy and the fastest recovery from a drive failure, but it uses only fifty percent of total storage capacity since each drive is a complete duplicate of the other. Read performance is enhanced because reads can be distributed across both drives, but write performance is not improved because both drives must receive every write.

RAID 5, called striping with distributed parity, distributes data and parity information across all drives in the array. A minimum of three drives is required. Parity is a form of redundancy information: for any given set of data blocks, the corresponding parity block allows the missing data to be reconstructed mathematically if one drive fails. Because parity is distributed across all drives rather than dedicated to a single drive, RAID 5 makes efficient use of storage capacity, losing only the equivalent of one drive's capacity to parity overhead regardless of how many drives are in the array. RAID 5 provides good balance among performance, capacity, and redundancy and has historically been the most popular RAID level for general server storage. It can survive the failure of any single drive but not a second failure during the rebuild process, which is a significant risk consideration for large modern drives that take many hours to rebuild.

RAID 6, called striping with double parity, extends RAID 5 by computing two independent parity values for each stripe, requiring a minimum of four drives. The double parity allows the array to survive the simultaneous failure of any two drives. RAID 6 addresses the vulnerability of RAID 5 during reconstruction: with large drives taking twelve or more hours to rebuild, the probability of a second drive failure during reconstruction is non-trivial, and RAID 6 provides protection against that scenario. The trade-off is that two drives' worth of capacity is consumed by parity, and write performance is lower than RAID 5 due to the additional parity computation.

RAID 10, also written as RAID 1+0, combines mirroring and striping by first creating mirrored pairs and then striping across those pairs. A minimum of four drives is required, with drives organized as two mirrored pairs. RAID 10 provides both the redundancy of RAID 1 and the performance of RAID 0. It can survive multiple simultaneous drive failures as long as both drives in any single mirrored pair do not fail simultaneously. RAID 10 delivers the best combined performance and redundancy of any commonly deployed RAID level, but it is also the most expensive in terms of storage efficiency, as fifty percent of total capacity is used for mirroring.

Hot spare drives are additional drives installed in a storage array that are not part of the active RAID set but are available to automatically begin rebuilding the array the moment a drive failure is detected. Without a hot spare, a failed drive must be manually replaced before reconstruction begins, leaving the array in a degraded state vulnerable to a second failure for however long that replacement takes. With a hot spare, reconstruction begins automatically within minutes of failure detection, minimizing the vulnerability window.

Geographic dispersion distributes infrastructure across multiple physically separated locations to protect against regional disasters that would affect an entire facility or metropolitan area. A hurricane, earthquake, flood, or power grid failure affecting a data center's geographic region may leave a data center in another state or country completely unaffected. Multi-region cloud deployments spread application instances and data replicas across multiple geographic regions within the same cloud provider's infrastructure, and multi-cloud deployments further spread workloads across different cloud providers.

Active-active and active-passive are the two primary models for geographically dispersed operations. In an active-active configuration, both primary and secondary sites are simultaneously handling production traffic, with load balancing distributing requests across sites. Active-active provides the best utilization of resources and the fastest failover, since both sites are already warmed up and serving traffic. In an active-passive configuration, the secondary site is maintained in a standby state and is not handling production traffic. When the primary site fails, operations are switched to the passive site, which must be activated before it can handle the load. Active-passive requires less capacity duplication than active-active but involves some delay during failover activation.

Data replication synchronization between sites can be either synchronous or asynchronous. Synchronous replication requires that a write operation be confirmed at both sites before being acknowledged to the application. This guarantees zero data loss because the secondary site always has an exact copy of the primary, but the requirement for round-trip confirmation introduces latency that may be unacceptable for high-performance workloads or across long distances. Asynchronous replication acknowledges writes at the primary site immediately and replicates to the secondary site afterward, typically within seconds to minutes. Asynchronous replication introduces lower latency but creates a replication lag window during which a primary site failure could result in loss of the most recently written data.

High availability clustering allows multiple server nodes to function as a single logical system. Applications and services running on the cluster can continue operating even when individual nodes fail, because surviving nodes automatically assume the workload of failed nodes. Active-active clusters have all nodes handling workload simultaneously, maximizing resource utilization. Active-passive clusters have primary nodes handling workload and secondary nodes in standby, consuming resources without contributing to throughput but ready to assume the workload instantly on primary failure. Most HA clustering implementations use a shared storage architecture in which all cluster nodes access a common storage array containing the application data, ensuring that whichever node is active always has access to the same data.

Load balancing distributes incoming requests across multiple server instances, improving performance and providing availability benefits. Load balancers continuously perform health checks against backend servers. When a server fails a health check, the load balancer automatically removes it from the pool of available servers and redistributes its portion of the traffic to remaining healthy servers. Users experience no interruption, as they are simply directed to a different server instance. Load balancers can use various algorithms to distribute traffic, including round-robin, least connections, and weighted distribution based on server capacity.

Disaster recovery sites are physically separate facilities maintained for the purpose of restoring operations in the event that the primary site becomes unavailable. Hot sites are fully operational, continuously maintained replicas of the primary data center. All hardware, operating systems, and applications are installed and configured, and data is continuously synchronized from the primary site. A hot site can assume production operations within minutes to hours of a primary site failure. Hot sites are the most expensive recovery option because they require duplicating the entire primary site infrastructure and paying for it even when not in use. Warm sites have hardware and connectivity in place but are not fully configured and may not have fully current data. Activating a warm site requires additional configuration steps and data restoration operations that may take hours to days. Warm sites represent a compromise between the cost of a hot site and the lengthy activation time of a cold site. Cold sites provide only the basic physical infrastructure: floor space, power, cooling, and network connectivity. No hardware is in place, meaning the organization must procure and ship equipment, install and configure it, and restore data from backups before operations can resume. Cold site activation can take days to weeks, making it appropriate only for organizations whose business processes can tolerate extended outages.

Failover is the process of switching production operations from a failed primary system or site to a backup. Automatic failover occurs without human intervention, triggered by automated health monitoring detecting a failure condition. Automatic failover minimizes downtime but requires careful design and testing to ensure that false positives, in which healthy systems are unnecessarily failed over, are avoided. Manual failover requires a human operator to evaluate the situation and make the decision to switch, providing more control but introducing delay. The choice between automatic and manual failover depends on the criticality of the service, the risk tolerance for false positive failovers, and the time available for human decision-making.

Platform diversity is a resilience strategy that uses different operating systems, vendors, or technology platforms for different components of the infrastructure. Because security vulnerabilities are typically operating-system-specific or vendor-specific, a vulnerability that allows an attacker to compromise all Windows servers simultaneously would not affect Linux servers providing the same function. Platform diversity limits the blast radius of any single vulnerability by ensuring that not all systems share the same attack surface. The trade-off is increased operational complexity in managing multiple platforms, requiring expertise in each.

Continuity of Operations Planning, abbreviated COOP, addresses the scenario in which technology systems are entirely unavailable and business must continue through manual, non-technology-dependent processes. COOP documents include manual procedures for conducting business transactions that are normally automated, methods for communicating with customers and partners when digital channels are unavailable, and paper-based record-keeping systems for capturing transactions that will later be reconciled when systems are restored. COOP procedures must be designed and documented before a crisis occurs and must be practiced through exercises to ensure that staff can execute them effectively under stress.

Infrastructure as Code supports resiliency by treating infrastructure configuration as version-controlled software rather than as manually maintained systems. When servers or entire environments fail, IaC tooling can re-provision them automatically from code templates in a fraction of the time manual rebuilding would require. IaC also ensures consistency: every system built from the same template is configured identically, eliminating configuration drift that can cause unexpected behavior during failover.

Recovery Time Objective is the maximum acceptable duration of an outage for a given system or service, defining how quickly recovery must be completed. Recovery Point Objective is the maximum acceptable data loss measured in time, defining how old the restored data can be. Mean Time to Repair is the average time required to restore a failed component. These three metrics quantify resiliency requirements and drive decisions about what redundancy and recovery investments are justified by the business value of the protected systems.
