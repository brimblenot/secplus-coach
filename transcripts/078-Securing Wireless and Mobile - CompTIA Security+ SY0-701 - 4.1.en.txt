Securing wireless and mobile environments requires understanding both the deployment models that define how devices are owned and managed and the technical controls that enforce security policy on devices that are inherently mobile, frequently connected to untrusted networks, and often used for both personal and professional purposes. The organization's deployment model determines the degree of control available and the appropriate technical countermeasures.

BYOD, which stands for Bring Your Own Device, is the deployment model in which employees use personally owned devices to access corporate resources. BYOD has the lowest cost to the employer because the organization does not purchase or provision the device. However, it also carries the highest risk because IT has the least control over a device the employee owns, configures, and uses for personal purposes. Employees may resist enrolling personal devices in Mobile Device Management because they are concerned about employer visibility into their personal data and activities. When BYOD is permitted, the minimum requirements typically include MDM enrollment, deployment of a separate encrypted work container that segregates corporate applications and data from personal applications and data, and the ability to remotely wipe only the corporate container without affecting personal data. Privacy policies must be clearly communicated to employees explaining what the organization can and cannot see on the enrolled device.

COPE, which stands for Corporate-Owned, Personally Enabled, is the deployment model in which the company purchases the device and assigns it to an employee but permits personal use on the device alongside corporate use. Because the organization owns the device, IT has full authority to configure and manage it, including the ability to remotely wipe everything on the device if it is lost or compromised. COPE provides more IT control than BYOD while still offering employees the convenience of using a single device for both work and personal activities. The device is typically configured with partitioned storage separating corporate and personal data, and MDM policy applies to the entire device rather than just a container.

CYOD, which stands for Choose Your Own Device, is a model in which the organization purchases the device but allows the employee to select from a pre-approved list of devices. This provides better hardware standardization than BYOD while still giving employees some degree of personal preference. Because the organization purchases the device, it retains ownership and full management authority. Corporate-Owned, Business Only, sometimes abbreviated COBO, is the most restrictive model — the organization owns the device and strictly prohibits personal use. COBO is most appropriate for sensitive roles where the risk of personal application data interacting with corporate data is unacceptable, or for specialized equipment such as field service tools that are not suitable for personal use.

Mobile Device Management is the centralized platform organizations use to manage the configuration, security, and compliance of enrolled mobile devices. MDM provides policy enforcement capabilities including specification of PIN complexity requirements, biometric authentication settings, screen lock timeout intervals, and encryption status. Application management through MDM allows administrators to push corporate applications to devices, remove applications when a device is decommissioned or an employee leaves, and prevent unauthorized application installation. Configuration management through MDM allows remote delivery of email configuration profiles, VPN settings, Wi-Fi authentication certificates, and other settings that would otherwise require manual configuration on each device. Security management capabilities include detection of jailbroken or rooted devices that have had operating system security controls bypassed, enforcement of minimum operating system version requirements, and remote wipe capabilities that can either perform a full factory reset or selectively erase only corporate data and applications while preserving personal content. Inventory and tracking functions provide administrators with a list of all enrolled devices showing operating system version, installed applications, and compliance status.

Mobile Application Management, abbreviated MAM, focuses on managing and securing specific applications rather than the entire device. MAM can wrap corporate applications with security policies enforced at the application level, apply data loss prevention controls that prevent corporate application data from being copied to personal applications, and perform application-level wipe of corporate applications and data without affecting personal applications or personal data on the device. MAM is particularly valuable in BYOD scenarios where full device management may be resisted by employees, because it allows the organization to protect corporate data without requiring comprehensive visibility or control over the entire device.

Enterprise Mobility Management, abbreviated EMM, is the comprehensive framework that combines Mobile Device Management, Mobile Application Management, Mobile Content Management, and Identity and Access Management into a unified platform for managing the complete mobile computing environment. Major EMM platforms include Microsoft Intune, VMware Workspace ONE, and Jamf Pro.

Geofencing uses GPS location data or Wi-Fi positioning to define geographic boundaries and automatically trigger policy changes or alerts based on a device's location. Organizations use geofencing to restrict access to corporate resources when devices are detected outside approved geographic areas, to alert when devices leave a defined perimeter, and to automatically adjust device policy based on location — for example, automatically disabling the camera when a device enters a manufacturing facility or government building where photography is prohibited, then re-enabling the camera when the device leaves that geofenced area.

Application containerization creates an encrypted, isolated container on a mobile device that houses corporate applications and data separately from personal applications and data. Technologies such as Samsung Knox and Citrix Secure Hub implement containerization at either the hardware or software level. Corporate data within the container is encrypted and inaccessible to personal applications. If the device is lost or an employee leaves the organization, administrators can wipe the corporate container without affecting personal data. Applications within the container cannot copy data to personal applications — clipboard access and data sharing between container applications and personal applications are blocked by policy, preventing data leakage through seemingly innocuous actions like copying text from a corporate email and pasting it into a personal application.

Remote access for mobile devices should be provided through VPN configurations pushed via MDM so that devices connect to corporate resources through encrypted tunnels. Per-app VPN configurations route traffic from only specific corporate applications through the VPN while allowing personal application traffic to go directly to the internet, improving performance while still protecting corporate data in transit. Always-on VPN configurations ensure that all device traffic passes through the corporate VPN connection at all times, providing the highest level of traffic inspection and control.

Mobile devices face a range of security threats that must be addressed through both technical and policy controls. Malicious applications distributed outside official application stores, known as sideloading, can contain malware designed to steal corporate data or credentials. Phishing delivered via SMS, commonly called smishing, targets mobile users who may be more likely to click a link when browsing on a small screen. Wi-Fi attacks in public locations can intercept traffic from mobile devices connected to open or poorly secured networks, making VPN enforcement particularly important for mobile users. Physical theft remains a significant threat given how frequently mobile devices are lost or stolen, making screen lock, encryption, and remote wipe capabilities essential controls. Bluetooth presents a short-range wireless attack surface that should be managed by disabling automatic pairing and keeping Bluetooth disabled when not actively in use.

Sideloading, the installation of applications from sources outside the official application store, should be controlled through MDM policy. On iOS devices configured in supervised mode, MDM can completely prevent installation of applications from sources other than the App Store or the organization's enterprise distribution mechanism. On Android, MDM policy can disable the unknown sources setting that enables sideloading. Organizations that distribute proprietary internal applications to employees can host them in enterprise application stores provisioned through MDM, allowing controlled distribution without requiring publication in public application stores.

Certificate management for mobile devices is often handled through MDM. When the organization operates an internal public key infrastructure, the MDM platform can push the organization's root certificate authority certificate to the trusted certificate store on each enrolled device. This ensures that TLS certificates issued by the internal CA are trusted by corporate applications on the device without requiring manual installation by each user. VPN client certificates, Wi-Fi authentication certificates using EAP-TLS, and email signing and encryption certificates can all be pushed to enrolled devices through MDM, enabling secure authentication without requiring users to manage certificates manually.

Wireless site surveys are a foundational step in deploying a secure and performant wireless network. A site survey involves physically moving through a facility with survey tools to measure signal strength, identify interference sources, discover existing access points, and produce a heat map showing wireless coverage across the facility. Heat maps visually represent signal strength across physical space, identifying areas of strong coverage and dead zones where additional access points may be needed, and also revealing areas where wireless signal extends outside the facility boundary where it could be intercepted. Spectrum analyzers identify all signals in a given frequency band including non-802.11 sources such as microwave ovens, Bluetooth devices, and neighboring wireless networks, helping diagnose interference that degrades performance. Site surveys should be repeated periodically to account for changes in the environment such as new construction, new neighboring wireless networks, or changes in the organization's floor plan. Minimizing signal leakage outside the physical facility boundary reduces the area from which an attacker could attempt to connect to or monitor the wireless network.
