Physical attacks represent a category of security threats that circumvent digital controls entirely by targeting the physical layer of an organization's infrastructure. While much of security focuses on software vulnerabilities, network defenses, and authentication systems, an attacker who gains physical access to hardware can frequently bypass every technical control in place. Physical access to a computer means access to its data: drives can be removed, boot processes can be manipulated, interfaces can be exploited, and implants can be installed. The Security+ exam addresses physical attacks across several specific categories: brute force entry, RFID cloning, USB-based attacks, card skimming, hardware implants, and environmental attacks, along with the defensive controls that mitigate each.

Brute force in the physical context means using physical force to overcome physical access controls — breaking locks, cutting fences, smashing windows, or ramming vehicles through barriers to gain unauthorized entry to facilities containing critical infrastructure or sensitive equipment. Physical security assessors regularly test the resilience of data center doors, windows, loading docks, and access barriers against forced entry. An attacker who can reach server hardware directly can remove drives, install implants, reset administrative credentials through out-of-band management interfaces, or cause physical damage to equipment. The common security axiom that door locks only keep out honest people reflects the reality that determined attackers with sufficient motivation and tools can overcome most physical barriers; defense-in-depth in physical security means layers of detection, delay, and response rather than reliance on any single barrier.

Tailgating and piggybacking — following an authorized person through a controlled entry point without presenting credentials — are physical attack vectors that rely on social norms rather than technical exploitation. Employees are often reluctant to challenge colleagues entering behind them, particularly in cultures that emphasize politeness. Mantrap vestibules that allow only one person through at a time physically enforce individual entry regardless of social dynamics.

Card skimming involves attaching additional hardware to card readers to capture magnetic stripe data and PINs as legitimate transactions occur. ATM skimmer overlays fit over the card insertion slot and read the magnetic stripe as the card enters, while a hidden camera mounted above the PIN pad or a false PIN pad overlay captures the PIN as it is typed. Skilled criminal groups have developed skimmer hardware so thin and precisely fitted that it is visually indistinguishable from the original card reader without physical examination. Gas pump skimmers are installed inside the pump housing where they are even harder to detect, often using Bluetooth to transmit captured data to an attacker waiting nearby. Point-of-sale terminal skimmers target retail checkout systems. Shimmer devices, a newer variant, insert as a thin wafer inside the chip card slot to read chip card transaction data as the chip communicates with the terminal. Captured magnetic stripe data is used to create cloned cards for card-present fraud; chip data captured by shimmers can be used for card-not-present online transactions where the chip is not required. Examining card readers for signs of tampering before use, covering the PIN pad when entering a PIN, and using contactless payment methods that do not expose magnetic stripe data are practical defenses.

RFID cloning attacks target the radio frequency identification technology used in building access badges, key fobs, and contactless payment cards. HID proximity cards used for building access transmit their unique identifier when energized by a reader's radio field, and that identifier can be captured by an RFID reader held within range of the card, even through a wallet or pocket. A 2013 demonstration at a security conference showed that an RFID reader concealed in a backpack could clone access badges from passersby on a commuter train without any physical contact. Cloning requires only seconds and inexpensive hardware available for less than fifty dollars. The cloned identifier can be written to a blank card and used to access any area the original card authorized. Contactless payment cards are partially protected by the dynamic cryptogram generated for each transaction, which the static RFID clone lacks, but older cards using only static identifiers remain vulnerable. RFID-blocking wallets and sleeves that attenuate the radio frequency field prevent passive cloning. Multi-factor authentication for physical access — requiring both the card and a PIN or biometric — means that even a successfully cloned card cannot be used alone. Monitoring access logs for the same card appearing at multiple locations simultaneously can detect active use of cloned credentials.

USB-based attacks leverage the trust that operating systems place in USB devices, particularly those presenting as keyboards and other Human Interface Devices. The USB Rubber Ducky, produced by the security tools company Hak5, is a commercial device in a standard USB thumb drive form factor that presents itself to any host operating system as a USB keyboard. Because operating systems accept keyboard input from any connected USB device without authentication or user confirmation, the Rubber Ducky can execute a pre-programmed sequence of keystrokes at hundreds of characters per second — far faster than any human typist — injecting a complete PowerShell reverse shell command, persistence mechanism, or data exfiltration script in seconds before any user can react. The O.MG Cable is a visually identical replacement for a standard USB cable that contains an embedded wireless-enabled microcomputer. Plugged into a laptop to charge a phone, it simultaneously acts as a remotely controlled HID device, allowing the attacker to inject keystrokes over WiFi from a distance. These attacks bypass antivirus software because they use the keyboard interface to type commands rather than writing malicious files to disk, and bypass application allowlisting because they invoke legitimate system utilities.

The baiting physical social engineering technique exploits human curiosity by leaving USB drives in parking lots, common areas, or mailed directly to targets. Security research studies have demonstrated that between forty-five and ninety-eight percent of found USB drives are plugged into computers by the finders, despite widespread awareness of the risk. A found drive labeled with something enticing — payroll data, confidential report, personal photos — has even higher connection rates. Organizations should implement USB port restriction policies enforced through endpoint security software that prevents unauthorized USB mass storage devices from being mounted, while allowing authorized peripherals through device allowlisting. Physical USB port blockers that fill unused ports and require a key to remove provide a hardware-level enforcement mechanism.

USBkill devices are a category of destructive USB tool that charges capacitors from the host system's USB power supply and discharges that stored energy back through the USB data lines at high voltage, physically destroying the USB controller and potentially damaging the system board. These have been used in evidence destruction scenarios and as anti-forensic tools to destroy devices being seized by law enforcement.

Juice jacking refers to the installation of malicious hardware in public USB charging stations at airports, hotels, conference centers, and transportation hubs. A charging station that has been compromised can both charge a connected mobile device and simultaneously access its data connection or install malware. Defense options include using power-only USB cables that physically lack data lines, using AC power adapters instead of USB charging, or using USB data blockers — small pass-through dongles that break the data lines while passing power — between the charging cable and the untrusted charging port.

Cold boot attacks exploit the physical property that DRAM memory retains its contents for seconds to minutes after power is removed, particularly when the memory modules are cooled rapidly to low temperatures. An attacker with physical access to a running or recently powered-off system can remove DRAM modules, briefly cool them to slow the decay of stored data, and read the contents using a specialized device or by inserting the modules into another system running custom memory-reading software. Encryption keys, session tokens, and other sensitive data present in RAM at the time of power removal may be recoverable. Full disk encryption protects stored data but not necessarily keys that are loaded into RAM during operation. Mitigation approaches include memory encryption features available on some modern CPUs, secure memory overwriting before shutdown, and physical security controls that prevent access to running systems.

Hardware implants installed by adversaries with physical access to equipment provide persistent remote access or data exfiltration capabilities below the level of the operating system. The NSA ANT catalog, leaked documents from 2013, described US intelligence capabilities including the COTTONMOUTH family of USB implant devices, the IRONCHEF PCI implant that provided persistent access via a hardware backdoor, and RAGEMASTER, a cable tap that extracted video signals from VGA cables to enable covert monitoring of display output. At a simpler level, keylogger hardware installed between keyboards and computers serves similar purposes. Physical inspection of hardware for unexpected components, tamper-evident seals on chassis screws and cable connectors, and maintaining hardware chain of custody from trusted suppliers are the primary defenses against implants.

Environmental attacks target the physical infrastructure that computing equipment depends on rather than the equipment itself. Power disruption is the most straightforward: external electrical panels, utility connections, and generator fuel supplies can be targeted to cause a data center power failure without requiring internal access. HVAC system compromise can cause computing equipment to overheat, triggering automatic thermal shutdown. Fire suppression system activation can displace oxygen in a data center and disable equipment. Water-based threats include flooding from overhead plumbing or external water intrusion.

Effective physical security defense requires multiple control layers. Physical access controls using badge readers, biometric verification, security guards, and CCTV surveillance restrict and monitor entry. Tamper-evident seals on hardware chassis and cable connectors provide evidence of physical inspection. Cable locks on laptops and workstations prevent opportunistic theft. USB port blockers in unused ports prevent USB-based attacks. Secure media disposal using degaussing for magnetic media and physical destruction meeting NIST Special Publication 800-88 standards for solid-state media ensures data cannot be recovered from discarded equipment. Clean desk policies remove sensitive materials from workspaces when unattended, reducing the value of physical access for intelligence gathering. Visitor management with escort requirements ensures that non-employees are supervised throughout their presence in sensitive areas. BIOS and UEFI passwords prevent unauthorized changes to boot configuration and restrict booting from external media. Full disk encryption renders drives recovered from stolen or discarded equipment unreadable without the decryption key. Faraday cages or shielded rooms block radio frequency signals, preventing wireless exfiltration from equipment inside and preventing remote RF attacks against equipment using wireless interfaces.

For the Security+ exam, candidates should understand the specific attack mechanisms for RFID cloning, USB HID attacks, card skimming, and juice jacking; know what cold boot attacks target and why encryption at rest does not fully mitigate them; and be able to identify the appropriate physical security controls — including tamper-evident seals, disk encryption, BIOS passwords, USB port blockers, and secure disposal procedures — for each physical attack scenario.
